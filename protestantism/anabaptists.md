# The Anabaptists — A Catholic Comparative Study

> **Scope and method.** This document presents the Anabaptist tradition from its own confessions and then compares it with Catholic doctrine. It is written **for comparison and study only**, in the spirit of [protestantism/README.md](README.md). Nothing here endorses an Anabaptist position or licenses contempt toward Anabaptist persons, whose forebears suffered severely for their convictions.

The **Anabaptists** ("re-baptizers") were the **Radical Reformation**: the wing of the sixteenth-century upheaval that judged both Rome and the magisterial reformers (Luther, Zwingli, Calvin) to have stopped short. Where the magisterial reformers kept infant Baptism, a territorial church, and the sword of the magistrate, the Anabaptists rejected all three. Their descendants include the **Mennonites**, the **Amish**, the **Hutterites**, the **Brethren**, and related peace churches. They are among the most distinctively "counter-cultural" Christian communities in the world.

---

## 1. Historical Origins

### 1.1 The Birth of the Movement: Zürich, 1525

The first adult rebaptisms took place in **Zürich on 21 January 1525**, when **Conrad Grebel**, **Felix Manz**, and **Georg Blaurock** baptized one another after the city council refused to abolish infant Baptism. The movement spread rapidly through Switzerland, southern Germany, the Tyrol, and the Low Countries. Its adherents were persecuted by Catholic and Protestant authorities alike: **Felix Manz** was drowned in the Limmat in 1527 as a "re-baptizer," and **Michael Sattler** was burned at Rottenburg the same year.

### 1.2 The Schleitheim Confession (1527)

The earliest Anabaptist confession, the **_Schleitheim Articles_** (February 1527), set out seven points: **believers' Baptism**; the **ban** (church discipline); the **breaking of bread**; **separation from the world**; the **pastor**; the **sword** (rejected); and the **oath** (rejected).

### 1.3 Münster (1534–1535) and the Mennonite Consolidation

The violent apocalyptic experiment at **Münster** (1534–1535), where radical Anabaptists seized the city and established a short-lived theocratic kingdom under Jan van Leiden, was disowned by the mainstream and used to justify further persecution. The peaceful stream consolidated under **Menno Simons (1496–1561)**, a former Dutch priest, from whom the **Mennonites** take their name. **Jakob Hutter** organized the Tyrolean communities noted for the **community of goods**, and **Jakob Ammann** led the 1693 schism that produced the **Amish**.

### 1.4 Migration and the Modern Communities

Persecution drove the Anabaptists into refuge areas: Moravia, the Netherlands, the Palatinate, and eventually **Pennsylvania** (from 1683) and the wider Americas. Today the tradition includes **Mennonite Church USA** and **Mennonite Church Canada**, **Old Order Amish** and **Old Order Mennonite** communities, **Hutterian Brethren**, the **Church of the Brethren** (from **Alexander Mack's** 1708 baptism at Schwarzenau), and peace-church bodies that remain closely connected to their Anabaptist roots while engaging modern society.

## 2. Distinctive Doctrines as the Anabaptists State Them

### 2.1 Believers' Baptism

The defining doctrine is that **Baptism is for believers only** — for those who can personally confess faith in Christ — and that **infant Baptism is invalid**. Baptism is understood as the believer's public commitment, an ordinance to be received upon personal repentance and faith, rather than a sacrament that confers regeneration on a passive subject.

### 2.2 Discipleship and _Gelassenheit_

Anabaptist theology is oriented not around a forensic doctrine of justification but around **discipleship** (_Nachfolge_): following Christ in the literal obedience of the Sermon on the Mount. The Mennonite _Confession of Faith in a Mennonite Perspective_ and the older **_Dordrecht Confession_** (1632) express this. The German term **_Gelassenheit_** — yieldedness, surrender of the self to God — names the disposition expected of the believer.

### 2.3 The Two Kingdoms, Nonviolence, and the Sword

The **Schleitheim** articles reject the **sword**: the Christian may not use force, serve as a magistrate, or go to war. This **nonresistance** (pacifism) is grounded in the Sermon on the Mount and the example of Christ's passion. The church is a **separated** community, distinct from the world and from the coercive order of the state.

### 2.4 The Oath, the Ban, and Separation from the World

Anabaptists refuse **oaths** (Matthew 5:33–37) and practice the **ban** — excommunication for persistent sin, sometimes with **shunning** (particularly among the Amish). They cultivate visible **separation from the world**: simple dress, plain speech, and the refusal of ostentatious wealth. Some communities (the **Hutterites**) hold goods **in common** after the pattern of Acts 2 and 4.

### 2.5 The Ordinances of the Church

Alongside Baptism, the Anabaptists observe the **Lord's Supper**, in the main understood as a memorial and a covenant of brotherly love rather than as a sacrifice or as a sacramental conveyance of grace. Some also practice **foot washing** (John 13) as an ordinance, anointing for healing, and the **holy kiss**.

## 3. The Catholic Assessment

### 3.1 What the Church Affirms in Common

The Catholic Church recognizes in the Anabaptists a serious, sacrificial following of Christ: the love of **Sacred Scripture** (especially the Sermon on the Mount), the commitment to **discipleship**, the recognition of the cost of faith, a life of **simplicity** and **mutual charity**, the dignity of the **person**, and the refusal to compromise with sin. The Anabaptist witness to the seriousness of the baptismal commitment, to the Gospel's demands on the whole of life, and to peace, contains elements of genuine Christian value (_Unitatis Redintegratio_ 3–4). The Catholic Church also deplores the executions of Anabaptists by the civil and ecclesial authorities of the time and joins Pope John Paul II's request for _forgiveness_ on the Day of Pardon (12 March 2000) for the sins of her members against separated Christians.

### 3.2 Where the Church Has Defined Otherwise (Dogma)

| Anabaptist position                                   | Catholic definition and source                                                                                                                                                                                                |
| :---------------------------------------------------- | :---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Baptism for believers only; infant Baptism invalid    | Baptism is a sacrament conferring grace and an indelible character; infants are to be baptized; those baptized in infancy are not to be rebaptized. Council of Trent, Session VII, canons on Baptism; CCC 1250–1255, 1284.    |
| Baptism as an ordinance of commitment                 | Baptism is an efficacious sign that "makes us sons of God"; it regenerates, incorporates into Christ and the Church, and is necessary for salvation where known. CCC 1213–1284.                                               |
| The Lord's Supper as memorial only                    | The Eucharist is the Real Presence and the sacrifice of the Cross re-presented. Council of Trent, Sessions XIII and XXII; CCC 1362–1381, 1406.                                                                                |
| Two ordinances; no sacrament of Order                 | Seven sacraments; Holy Orders in three degrees. Council of Trent, Sessions VII and XXIII; CCC 1113–1134, 1536–1600.                                                                                                           |
| Absolute refusal of the sword and of military service | Legitimate defense and just war are morally permissible; the state bears the sword from God (Romans 13). CCC 2263–2267, 2309–2317; _Gaudium et Spes_ 79–81.                                                                   |
| Absolute refusal of oaths                             | Oaths are lawful when taken with truth, judgment, and justice; Scripture forbids rash and superfluous swearing, not oath-taking as such. CCC 2153–2155.                                                                       |
| Community of goods as required                        | Private property is legitimate, held under the universal destination of goods; the renunciation of property is counseled in religious life, not imposed on all. CCC 2401–2406, 915.                                           |
| Separated church, discipline by the ban               | The Church is the universal sacrament of salvation; excommunication is medicinal and is administered by legitimate authority. CCC 776, 1463; [sacraments/penance-reconciliation.md](../sacraments/penance-reconciliation.md). |

> **On the "schism" of Münster.** The violent kingdom of Münster (1534–1535) is not representative of Anabaptism, and mainstream Anabaptists disowned it. It illustrates, however, what the Church has always taught about religious coercion: the Kingdom of Christ is not of this world, and temporal power is not given to the Church to establish it (Vatican II, _Dignitatis Humanae_ 1–2; CCC 2245–2246).

### 3.3 Baptismal Status

Because Anabaptist communities administer **Trinitarian Baptism with water** to confessing believers, those baptized among them are **validly baptized** and share the one Baptism that constitutes the sacramental bond of unity among Christians (_Unitatis Redintegratio_ 22). The Catholic Church therefore does not rebaptize an adult who was validly baptized in an Anabaptist community, but she does require that a person seeking full communion be received into the Church, and she does not recognize Anabaptist ordinations as valid. The rejection of infant Baptism, however, remains a grave doctrinal divergence, since the Church has always regarded the baptism of infants as apostolic practice and has defined that such Baptism is neither to be delayed nor repeated (Council of Trent, Session VII).

### 3.4 Conflict of Conscience and the Duty of the State

Anabaptist history raises the question of the relation between the Church and the coercive power of the state. The Catholic Church teaches that the state is legitimate and that it may, within the moral law, exercise force for the common good (CCC 2263–2267), while it also teaches the freedom of the human person in religious matters (_Dignitatis Humanae_ 2). The Church's own history includes sins against that freedom, for which she has asked forgiveness. What she cannot concede is either the Anabaptist claim that the sword is always forbidden to the Christian or the Erastian claim that the state is competent in the Church's own spiritual governance.

## 4. Latin and Eastern Catholic Perspectives

The Eastern Catholic and Eastern Orthodox Churches share with Rome the **seven sacraments**, including the Baptism of infants, and the **sacrificial Divine Liturgy**; they too reject the reduction of the Eucharist to a mere memorial. Yet the Eastern tradition also enshrines the **monastic** and **eremitic** vocation — the radical following of Christ in poverty, chastity, and obedience — and the **hesychast** tradition of continual prayer. The Anabaptist instinct toward radical discipleship finds its properly ordered home in Catholic **religious life** and the **evangelical counsels**, not in the rejection of the sacraments. See [church-history/eastern-catholic-churches.md](../church-history/eastern-catholic-churches.md).

## 5. Ecumenical Relations

Catholic dialogue with the Anabaptist tradition is comparatively recent and often local:

- the **Mennonite–Catholic International Dialogue** has produced reports such as **_Called Together to Be Peacemakers_** (2003), which examines justification, the Church, and peace;
- the **Pontifical Council for Promoting Christian Unity** has engaged Mennonite and Brethren bodies on reconciliation, including a 2001 encounter in the Netherlands recalling the persecution of Anabaptists;
- the **Declaration on the Way** (2015) and the Lutheran dialogues touch the Anabaptist question indirectly, since the earliest Anabaptists were put to death by both Catholic and Protestant magistrates.

The remaining obstacles are substantial and doctrinal: **Baptism** (infants), the **sacraments**, **ministry and orders**, and the relation of the Christian to the **civil order**. The Church seeks unity in truth and charity, sensitive to the wounds of history (_Ut Unum Sint_ 18, 34).
