# Anglicanism — A Catholic Comparative Study

> **Scope and method.** This document presents the Anglican tradition from its own formularies and then compares it with Catholic doctrine. It is written **for comparison and study only**, in the spirit of [protestantism/README.md](README.md). It endorses no position but the Catholic faith, while treating Anglicans as separated brethren who share the one Baptism (_Unitatis Redintegratio_ 22).

The **Anglican Communion** is the family of churches descended from the **Church of England**, which separated from the See of Rome in the sixteenth century. Alone among the Reformation communities the Second Vatican Council singles it out: "Among those in which Catholic traditions and institutions in part continue to exist, the Anglican Communion occupies a special place" (_Unitatis Redintegratio_ 13). Anglicanism retains a threefold ministry, an episcopal structure, and a liturgical and sacramental inheritance, yet remains in imperfect communion with the Catholic Church.

---

## 1. Historical Origins

### 1.1 Henry VIII and the Royal Supremacy

Henry VIII had defended the seven sacraments against Luther in the **_Assertio Septem Sacramentorum_** (1521), for which Pope Leo X named him **"Defender of the Faith."** His rupture with Rome arose from the **annulment crisis** over Catherine of Aragon. In 1533 Thomas Cranmer, Archbishop of Canterbury, declared the marriage null; in **1534** the **Act of Supremacy** declared the king "the only supreme head on earth of the Church of England." The **dissolution of the monasteries** (1536–1541) followed. The **Six Articles** (1539) still reaffirmed transubstantiation, clerical celibacy, and auricular confession — the doctrinal Protestant turn came later.

### 1.2 The Edwardian and Elizabethan Settlements

Under **Edward VI**, Cranmer produced the **_Book of Common Prayer_** (1549; 1552) and the **Forty-Two Articles** (1553), which moved the Church of England decisively toward Reformed doctrine. **Mary I** (1553–1558) restored communion with Rome; Cranmer, Ridley, and Latimer were burned.

Under **Elizabeth I** the **Elizabethan Settlement** (1559) established the enduring shape of Anglicanism: the **Act of Supremacy** (the monarch as "Supreme Governor"), the **Act of Uniformity**, and a revised Prayer Book, followed by the **_Thirty-Nine Articles_** (1563; finally in 1571). This settlement is often called the **_via media_** — a middle way between Rome and Geneva.

### 1.3 From the Stuarts to the Oxford Movement

- The **Authorized (King James) Version** of the Bible appeared in **1611**.
- The **Puritan** conflicts, the Civil War, and the **Westminster** era were followed by the **1662** Prayer Book and the Great Ejection.
- In the nineteenth century the **Oxford Movement** (Keble, Pusey, and John Henry Newman, from 1833) reasserted the Anglican claim to catholicity. Newman was received into the Catholic Church in **1845** and later created cardinal; his journey is reflected in [church-history/catholic-conversion-reading-list.md](../church-history/catholic-conversion-reading-list.md).
- The **Lambeth Conference** (from 1867) gave the emerging **Anglican Communion** one of its "instruments of communion."

### 1.4 The Modern Communion and Its Fractures

The Anglican Communion now comprises dozens of autonomous **provinces** linked by the Archbishop of Canterbury, the Lambeth Conference, the Primates' Meeting, and the Anglican Consultative Council. It is not a single church but a communion of churches. Decisions to ordain women to the priesthood (from the 1970s; in England from 1992) and to bless or solemnize same-sex unions have produced deep division, leading to the formation of the **Global Anglican Future Conference (GAFCON)** in 2008 and the **Anglican Church in North America (ACNA)** in 2009. These developments make the Anglican picture internally plural, and any Catholic assessment must say so.

## 2. Confessional Sources

Anglican identity rests on a distinctive set of formularies:

- the **_Book of Common Prayer_** (1552; 1662) — its liturgies, collects, and offices;
- the **_Ordinal_** (1550; 1662) — the rites for ordaining bishops, priests, and deacons;
- the **_Thirty-Nine Articles of Religion_** (1571);
- the **Authorized Version** of Scripture and, in the wider tradition, the **Homilies** and the historic **catechism**.

Alongside these "historic formularies," Anglicanism has always contained a **High Church** (Anglo-Catholic) strand that reads them in continuity with the ancient Church, a **Low Church** (Evangelical) strand that reads them in continuity with the Reformation, and a **Broad Church** (liberal) strand. This comprehensiveness is itself a defining feature of the tradition.

## 3. Distinctive Doctrines as Anglicanism States Them

### 3.1 Scripture, the Creeds, and the Formularies

The **Thirty-Nine Articles** teach the sufficiency of Scripture (Article VI) and the authority of the three ecumenical creeds. Article XI affirms justification by faith; Articles XIX–XXXVI treat the Church, the sacraments, and the ministry. The Articles are the historic doctrinal standard, though their authoritative status has weakened in much modern Anglicanism.

### 3.2 The Sacraments

Article XXV teaches that there are "two Sacraments ordained of Christ our Lord in the Gospel" — **Baptism** and the **Lord's Supper** — while the other five "commonly called Sacraments" are "not to be counted for Sacraments of the Gospel" but have a sacramental character in a looser sense. Article XXVII on Baptism calls it a "sign of regeneration"; Article XXVIII on the Lord's Supper denies **transubstantiation** and teaches that Christ's body is received "after a heavenly and spiritual manner," the means being faith. Article XXIX denies that the wicked receive Christ's body. Article XXXI states that "the Sacrifices of Masses" are "blasphemous fables, and dangerous deceits."

Anglo-Catholic practice, however, often affirms the Real Presence, Eucharistic adoration, and the sacrificial character of the Eucharist in a sense its own formularies do not fully articulate. The gap between **official formularies** and **prevailing practice** is a real feature of Anglicanism and must be reported as such.

### 3.3 Ministry and the Threefold Order

Anglicanism retains the ancient threefold order — **bishops, priests, and deacons** — and claims **apostolic succession** through the Elizabethan and earlier consecrations. It rejects the papacy (Articles XXXVII), the papal claim of jurisdiction, and the doctrine of the Mass as sacrifice. Article XXXVII ascribes supreme jurisdiction to the civil magistrate.

### 3.4 Mary, the Saints, and the Communion of Saints

The Articles reject the invocation of saints, purgatory, pardons, relics, and images (Article XXII). Yet the Anglican liturgical and theological tradition has preserved a high (if restrained) regard for the **Blessed Virgin Mary**, and modern Anglican-Catholic dialogue has produced convergence on her (see _Mary: Grace and Hope in Christ_, ARCIC, 2005).

## 4. The Validity of Anglican Orders

The status of Anglican orders is the central ecumenical issue, and the Catholic Church's answer is settled by a definitive judgment of the Apostolic See.

On **13 September 1896**, Pope **Leo XIII** issued the bull **_Apostolicae Curae_**, declaring:

> "We pronounce and declare that ordinations carried out according to the Anglican rite have been and are absolutely null and utterly void."

The judgment rests on a **defect of form** and a **defect of intention**. The Edwardine **Ordinal** (1550) abandoned the Roman pontifical's explicit references to the conferral of the sacrificial priesthood, and the Reformation context in which it arose denied the sacrificial character of the ordained ministry. The Church has not reversed this judgment. Anglican bishops and priests are therefore **not validly ordained** in the Catholic understanding, and Anglican Eucharistic celebrations are not valid Masses. Their **Baptism**, however, is valid, which is why converts from Anglicanism are not rebaptized. See [sacraments/holy-orders.md](../sacraments/holy-orders.md).

> **A note on ecumenical discussion.** Some ARCIC documents have explored the question of ministry and ordination and have argued for a convergence of understanding. These documents are theological studies, not magisterial acts, and do not rescind _Apostolicae Curae_. The Catholic Church continues to regard it as a settled judgment on the validity of Anglican orders.

## 5. The Catholic Assessment

### 5.1 What the Church Affirms in Common

The Catholic Church recognizes in Anglicanism an inheritance of much value: the **Trinity**, the **Incarnate Word**, the **creeds**, a deep attachment to **Scripture** and the **Prayer Book**, the sacraments of **Baptism** (validly administered) and a Eucharistic faith (however explained), an episcopal and liturgical instinct, the daily offices, and a historic commitment to theological learning. Vatican II described the Anglican Communion as occupying "a special place" among the communities born of the Reformation.

### 5.2 Where the Church Has Defined Otherwise (Dogma)

| Anglican formulary / practice                                    | Catholic definition and source                                                                                                                                                                                                    |
| :--------------------------------------------------------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Two sacraments "of the Gospel" (Article XXV)                     | Seven sacraments instituted by Christ. Council of Trent, Session VII, canons 1 and 2; CCC 1113–1134, 1210.                                                                                                                        |
| Denial of transubstantiation (Article XXVIII)                    | Transubstantiation defined; the whole substance of the bread and wine becomes the Body and Blood of Christ. Council of Trent, Session XIII, canon 2; CCC 1373–1381.                                                               |
| "Sacrifices of Masses" as blasphemous (Article XXXI)             | The Mass is a true propitiatory sacrifice. Council of Trent, Session XXII; CCC 1362–1372.                                                                                                                                         |
| Denial of purgatory, invocation of saints, images (Article XXII) | Defined at Trent, Session XXV; CCC 956, 1030–1032, 2132; [sacramentals/sacred-images.md](../sacramentals/sacred-images.md).                                                                                                       |
| Royal supremacy over the Church                                  | The Church's authority is from Christ, not the civil power; the pope is the successor of Peter. Vatican I, _Pastor Aeternus_ (1870); _Lumen Gentium_ 18–25; CCC 880–887.                                                          |
| Anglican orders claimed as valid                                 | Judged null and void by _Apostolicae Curae_ (1896); defect of form and intention.                                                                                                                                                 |
| Ordination of women to the priesthood                            | The Church has no authority to ordain women; the teaching is definitive and to be held definitively. John Paul II, _Ordinatio Sacerdotalis_ (1994); CCC 1577. Whether the practice also invalidates orders is a further question. |

### 5.3 Matters of Discipline and Theological Opinion

Much of what divides Anglicans and Catholics is **discipline** rather than dogma: liturgical language, vesture, the calendar, fasting rules, the ordination of married men (the Church already admits married Eastern Catholic priests), and the form of the offices. The **Anglican patrimony** — choral liturgy, the Prayer Book, the parochial and missionary ethos — is not itself a doctrinal obstacle, and the Church has made explicit provision to receive it:

> **_Anglicanorum coetibus_ (2009).** Pope Benedict XVI's apostolic constitution established **personal ordinariates** — canonical structures like dioceses, without territory — for former Anglicans entering full communion while retaining elements of the Anglican liturgical and spiritual tradition. Three ordinariates exist: **Our Lady of Walsingham** (England and Wales), the **Chair of Saint Peter** (United States), and **Our Lady of the Southern Cross** (Australia). This is the Church's concrete expression of receiving the Anglican patrimony without compromise of doctrine.

## 6. Latin and Eastern Catholic Perspectives

The Catholic answer to the Anglican reform is not merely Latin. The **Eastern Catholic** and **Eastern Orthodox** Churches share with Rome the **sacrificial Eucharist**, the **seven sacraments**, **apostolic succession**, and the **veneration of the Theotokos and saints** — the very elements the Anglican formularies formally set aside. Pope Leo XIII's judgment of 1896 invoked the unbroken tradition of both East and West on the sacrificial priesthood (_Sacerdotium_), and the Church's liturgical and Marian doctrine holds in both communions. See [church-history/eastern-catholic-churches.md](../church-history/eastern-catholic-churches.md) and [church-history/greek-orthodox-church.md](../church-history/greek-orthodox-church.md).

## 7. Ecumenical Relations

Anglican-Catholic dialogue is the oldest of the modern bilateral conversations:

- it began with the **Malines Conversations** (1921–1926) and the meeting of Archbishop Michael Ramsey with Pope Paul VI in **1966**;
- the **Anglican–Roman Catholic International Commission (ARCIC)** has produced a series of agreed statements — on Eucharistic doctrine (1971), ministry and ordination (1973), authority (1981), salvation (1986), the Church as communion (1990), life in Christ (1994), the gift of authority (1999), and **_Mary: Grace and Hope in Christ_** (2005);
- **_Anglicanorum coetibus_** (2009) provided a canonical path to full communion for those who seek it;
- the **International Anglican–Roman Catholic Commission for Unity and Mission (IARCCUM)** advances common witness.

The principal obstacles now are the **ordination of women**, divergent moral teaching on marriage and human sexuality, and the underlying question of **authority** — the absence in Anglicanism of a universally binding teaching office. The Catholic Church continues to seek that full communion "which the Lord willed for His disciples" (_Ut Unum Sint_ 4).

## Sources & Levels of Authority

- **Dogmatic definitions** — Council of Trent (Sessions VII, XIII, XXII, XXIII, XXV); Second Council of Nicaea (787).
- **Definitive judgments and papal teaching** — **_Apostolicae Curae_** (Leo XIII, 1896) on Anglican orders; _Pastor Aeternus_ (Vatican I, 1870); [_Unitatis Redintegratio_](https://www.vatican.va/archive/hist_councils/ii_vatican_council/documents/vat-ii_decree_19641121_unitatis-redintegratio_en.html) (1964); [_Lumen Gentium_](https://www.vatican.va/archive/hist_councils/ii_vatican_council/documents/vat-ii_const_19641121_lumen-gentium_en.html) (1964); [_Ut Unum Sint_](https://www.vatican.va/content/john-paul-ii/en/encyclicals/documents/hf_jp-ii_enc_25051995_ut-unum-sint.html) (1995); _Dominus Iesus_ (2000); _Ordinatio Sacerdotalis_ (1994); _Anglicanorum coetibus_ (2009).
- **Catechism** — CCC 748–975, 1210–1666, 1987–2029, 1030–1032, 1577.
- **Anglican sources (reported, not endorsed)** — The Book of Common Prayer, the Ordinal, the Thirty-Nine Articles, and the ARCIC agreed statements.
- **Theological opinion** — ARCIC studies are cited as ecumenical theology, not as magisterial acts.
