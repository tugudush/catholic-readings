# Adventism — A Catholic Comparative Study

> **Scope and method.** This document presents the Adventist tradition — above all the **Seventh-day Adventist Church** — from its own sources and then compares it with Catholic doctrine. It is written **for comparison and study only**, in the spirit of [protestantism/README.md](README.md). It endorses no Adventist position and treats Adventists as separated brethren, baptized into Christ where their Baptism is Trinitarian (_Unitatis Redintegratio_ 22).

**Adventism** is the family of churches that emerged from the **Millerite** expectation of Christ's imminent return in the 1830s and 1840s. Its largest body, the **Seventh-day Adventist Church (SDA)**, combines three emphases that distinguish it from older Protestant families: the **seventh-day (Saturday) Sabbath**, the **heavenly sanctuary** and "**investigative judgment**," and the writings of **Ellen G. White** as a continuing prophetic authority. It also carries a distinctive **health** and **educational** mission. Other Adventist bodies include the **Advent Christian Church** and the **Church of God (Seventh Day)**.

---

## 1. Historical Origins

### 1.1 William Miller and the Great Disappointment

**William Miller (1782–1849)**, a Baptist lay preacher in upstate New York, began after 1818 to interpret the "2,300 evenings and mornings" of Daniel 8:14 as years ending in the early 1840s, and concluded that Christ would return "about 1843." Under the revival fervor of the **Second Great Awakening**, the **Millerite** movement grew into the tens of thousands. When the spring of 1844 passed, a new date was fixed: **22 October 1844**, the Day of Atonement. Its passing brought the **Great Disappointment**, in which the movement's expectation collapsed.

### 1.2 From Disappointment to Doctrine (1844–1863)

Rather than disband, the remnant reinterpreted the event. **Hiram Edson** reported that on 23 October 1844 he saw in vision Christ entering the **Most Holy Place** of a **heavenly sanctuary** — not to cleanse an earthly one, but to begin a final phase of priestly ministry. This became the doctrine of the **investigative judgment**. **Ellen G. White (1827–1915)**, who had experienced visions from December 1844, became the movement's guiding figure. **Joseph Bates**, influenced by **Seventh Day Baptists**, brought in the **Sabbath** doctrine in the mid-1840s. The name **"Seventh-day Adventist"** was adopted in **1860**, and the church was formally organized at **Battle Creek, Michigan, in 1863**.

### 1.3 Health, Mission, and Worldwide Growth

A vision of 1863 prompted the **health reform** movement, from which grew the **Battle Creek Sanitarium** (with **John Harvey Kellogg**) and a worldwide network of hospitals, schools, publishing houses, and relief organizations (today **ADRA**). The SDA became one of the most globally distributed Protestant bodies, with a strong missionary and educational system and a membership of over twenty million.

### 1.4 Related and Offshoot Movements

- the **Advent Christian Church** (organized 1861) — Sunday-keeping and holding conditional immortality;
- the **Church of God (Seventh Day)** — a Saturday-Sabbath Adventist body of the 1860s;
- the **Worldwide Church of God** (Herbert W. Armstrong, 1934), which moved toward mainstream evangelical theology after 1995;
- the **Branch Davidians**, a schismatic offshoot at Waco, Texas, whose 1993 tragedy must be distinguished from the SDA church itself.

## 2. Distinctive Doctrines as Adventism States Them

The SDA's **28 Fundamental Beliefs** (1980; expanded 2005) set out its teaching. Its most distinctive points are these.

### 2.1 Scripture and the Gift of Prophecy

Adventists hold the Protestant principle of **Scripture alone**, while also holding Ellen White's writings as a **continuing and authoritative source of truth** (Fundamental Belief 18, "The Gift of Prophecy") — a "lesser light" that guides interpretation of the greater. This effectively inserts a further prophetic authority alongside the Bible.

### 2.2 The Seventh-day Sabbath

The **seventh-day Sabbath** (Friday sunset to Saturday sunset) is binding on Christians, and Sunday observance is a later human (and, in Adventist eschatology, an end-time) innovation. In the final crisis, the **seal of God** (Sabbath observance) stands opposed to the **mark of the beast** (allegiance to a Sunday law enforced by state and apostate religion, identified with the papacy).

### 2.3 The Heavenly Sanctuary and the Investigative Judgment

Beginning in **1844**, Christ entered the Most Holy Place of the heavenly sanctuary to conduct an **investigative judgment**, examining the records of the professed faithful before His return. This doctrine gives the movement its distinctive eschatological timetable.

### 2.4 Conditional Immortality and the State of the Dead

Adventists deny the natural immortality of the soul. The dead are unconscious ("**soul sleep**") until the resurrection; the wicked are not eternally tormented but are finally **annihilated**. The saved are raised at the Second Coming to reign with Christ for a **literal thousand years** in heaven before the descent of the New Jerusalem.

### 2.5 Baptism, the Lord's Supper, and the Christian Life

Baptism is for **believers**, by **immersion**, in the **Trinitarian name** (the denomination adopted Trinitarian statements in 1931 and 1980 after an earlier semi-Arian phase). The **Lord's Supper** is celebrated quarterly and is preceded by the **ordinance of humility** (foot washing), with grape juice and unleavened bread. The **health message** — vegetarianism encouraged, abstinence from alcohol and tobacco, care of the body as a temple, the "NEWSTART" principles — is a hallmark. **Tithing** and **stewardship** are strongly emphasized.

## 3. The Catholic Assessment

### 3.1 What the Church Affirms in Common

The Catholic Church recognizes among Adventists a real love of **Scripture** (especially the prophets and Daniel), a firm faith in the **Trinity** (in the contemporary SDA), the **deity and resurrection of Christ**, the **authority of the Ten Commandments**, a sober **moral life**, a serious commitment to **health and temperance**, a zeal for **mission**, and a vast system of charitable and relief work. Adventists who have received **Trinitarian Baptism** are validly baptized Christians.

### 3.3 The Sabbath Question Answered

The Adventist claim is that Christians are obliged to the seventh-day Sabbath and that Sunday worship was a later corruption. The Catholic answer rests on the apostolic origin of the **Lord's Day**:

- **The New Testament** places the Resurrection on "the first day of the week" (Matthew 28:1; Mark 16:2; Luke 24:1; John 20:1) and shows the disciples gathered on that day (John 20:19, 26; Acts 20:7; 1 Corinthians 16:2).
- **St. Ignatius of Antioch** (c. 107), a direct disciple of the apostles, wrote that Christians "no longer keep the Sabbath but live according to the Lord's Day, on which our life also arose through Him" (_Letter to the Magnesians_ 9).
- **St. Justin Martyr** (c. 155) described the Sunday assembly in his _First Apology_ 67.
- The **Council of Laodicea** (c. 363–364) disciplined Judaizing observance; the Christian Sunday is not a human substitute for the Sabbath but the fulfillment of its rest in the **Resurrection** — the "eighth day," the beginning of the new creation.
- Scripture itself declares the Sabbath a **shadow** of things to come (Colossians 2:16–17) and places the true rest in Christ (Hebrews 4). The **moral** content of the Third Commandment — worship of God and the sanctification of time — is fulfilled in the Lord's Day, not abolished (CCC 2168–2178).

### 3.4 Prophecy, Chronology, and the Test of the Prophet

The Millerite date of 1844 was a **failed prediction**. Scripture sets a test for prophets: "when a prophet speaks in the name of the Lord, if the word does not come to pass... that is a word that the Lord has not spoken" (Deuteronomy 18:22). Christ Himself warned that the day and hour are known to the Father alone (Matthew 24:36). The Catholic Church therefore does not recognize **Ellen G. White** as a prophet, and her writings are studied as a nineteenth-century religious phenomenon, not as revelation. The Church's criteria for alleged private revelations — and the **2024 Dicastery norms** for their discernment — apply here; a private revelation can never become a norm of faith binding on the Church. See [miracles/README.md](../miracles/README.md).

### 3.5 Baptismal Status

Because SDA baptism is administered by **immersion with water in the name of the Trinity**, it is **valid**; the Church does not rebaptize a person so baptized. Adventist ministers are not validly ordained priests, and their Communion services are not valid Masses.

## 4. Latin and Eastern Catholic Perspectives

The Eastern Catholic and Eastern Orthodox Churches share with Rome the **immortality of the soul**, the **intermediate state**, the **invocation of the saints**, **prayer for the dead**, and the **sacrificial Liturgy** — the very doctrines Adventism denies. The Eastern tradition also knows the **Sabbath–Sunday** distinction: the Christian **Lord's Day** is the day of the Resurrection, and the East's liturgical week is built upon it. On the **body**, the East's ascetical tradition (fasting, the veneration of relics, the **Theotokos** as "more honorable than the cherubim") provides the properly Catholic context for the Adventist concern about health: the body is a temple, yes — but its destiny is **resurrection**, not annihilation, and its health is ordered to the worship of God and the reception of the sacraments. See [church-history/eastern-catholic-churches.md](../church-history/eastern-catholic-churches.md).

## 5. Ecumenical Relations

Formal Catholic dialogue with the SDA is limited. The **Pontifical Council for Promoting Christian Unity** and the **Seventh-day Adventist Church** have held a series of **informal bilateral conversations** (notably 1971–1977 and later rounds), exploring Scripture and Tradition, the Sabbath, the sacraments, and eschatology. Differences on the **Sabbath**, the **sanctuary/investigative judgment**, the **state of the dead**, and **Ellen White's authority** remain profound; cooperation in **health care**, **relief**, and **religious liberty** is real and valued.

## Sources & Levels of Authority

- **Dogmatic definitions** — **Lateran V** (1513) on the immortality of the soul; Council of Trent: Session VII (Baptism), Session XXII (Mass), Session XXIII (Orders), Session XXV (purgatory, invocation of saints); **Second Council of Nicaea** (787).
- **Papal and dicasterial teaching** — **_Benedictus Deus_** (Benedict XII, 1336) on the immediate vision of God; [_Dies Domini_](https://www.vatican.va/content/john-paul-ii/en/apost_letters/1998/documents/hf_jp-ii_apl_05071998_dies-domini.html) (John Paul II, 1998); [_Dei Verbum_](https://www.vatican.va/archive/hist_councils/ii_vatican_council/documents/vat-ii_const_19651118_dei-verbum_en.html); [_Unitatis Redintegratio_](https://www.vatican.va/archive/hist_councils/ii_vatican_council/documents/vat-ii_decree_19641121_unitatis-redintegratio_en.html); the Dicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith's **2024 norms** on alleged supernatural phenomena.
- **Catechism** — CCC 366, 946–962, 1021–1022, 1030–1037, 1113–1134, 1250–1255, 2168–2178.
- **Adventist sources (reported, not endorsed)** — the 28 Fundamental Beliefs; the writings of Ellen G. White; the doctrines of the heavenly sanctuary and the investigative judgment.
- **Theological opinion** — cited as such.
