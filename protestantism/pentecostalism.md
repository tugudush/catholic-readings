# Pentecostalism — A Catholic Comparative Study

> **Scope and method.** This document presents the Pentecostal and Charismatic movement from its own sources and then compares it with Catholic doctrine. It is written **for comparison and study only**, in the spirit of [protestantism/README.md](README.md). It endorses no position but the Catholic faith, while insisting that the **charisms of the Holy Spirit are real, biblical, and proper to the Catholic Church** — see [gifts-holy-spirit/README.md](../gifts-holy-spirit/README.md).

**Pentecostalism** is the youngest and fastest-growing family of Christian communities in the world. Born at the turn of the twentieth century from the Holiness movement, it emphasizes a post-conversion **baptism in the Holy Spirit**, the **charismatic gifts** — above all **speaking in tongues** and **divine healing** — and a lively, spontaneous worship. Its **Charismatic** offspring spread into the historic Protestant denominations and, from 1967, into the Catholic Church herself as the **Catholic Charismatic Renewal**.

---

## 1. Historical Origins

### 1.1 Topeka (1901) and Azusa Street (1906)

The movement's doctrinal seed was planted by **Charles Parham** at his Bethel Bible College in **Topeka, Kansas**, where on **1 January 1901** a student, **Agnes Ozman**, spoke in tongues — taken as "the initial evidence" of a baptism in the Holy Spirit. The movement's global ignition came with the **Azusa Street Revival** in Los Angeles (**1906–1909**) under **William J. Seymour**, a revival notable for its interracial character and its worldwide missionary reach.

### 1.2 Classical Pentecostal Denominations

Classical Pentecostalism crystallized into denominations, among them:

- the **Church of God in Christ (COGIC)** (1907; the largest predominantly African American Pentecostal body);
- the **Assemblies of God** (1914, Hot Springs, Arkansas);
- the **Church of God (Cleveland, Tennessee)** (from Holiness roots, 1886/1906);
- the **International Church of the Foursquare Gospel** (1923, Aimee Semple McPherson);
- the **United Pentecostal Church International** (1945), representing **Oneness** Pentecostalism, which **denies the Trinity**.

### 1.3 The Charismatic Movement (from 1960)

- **1960** — Dennis Bennett, an Episcopal priest, brought the experience into a mainline congregation.
- **February 1967** — the **"Duquesne Weekend"** at Duquesne University began the **Catholic Charismatic Renewal**, which spread to Notre Dame and worldwide. The experience was interpreted as a **renewal of the graces of Baptism and Confirmation**, not as a new sacrament.
- The Church's response was positive and cautious: Pope **Paul VI** addressed the movement in 1975 ("the Church rejoices…"), and **John Paul II** encouraged it in 1979 and thereafter. In **2019** Pope Francis established **CHARIS**, a single international service for the Catholic Charismatic Renewal.

### 1.4 The Third Wave and Prosperity Teaching

From the 1980s, the **Third Wave** (John Wimber's **Vineyard**, C. Peter Wagner's "signs and wonders" and "**New Apostolic Reformation**") carried charismatic practice into non-Pentecostal churches. Within parts of the movement, the **"Word of Faith"** or **prosperity gospel** (E. W. Kenyon, Kenneth Hagin, Kenneth Copeland, and others) taught that health and wealth are the guaranteed inheritance of the believer — a teaching the Catholic Church rejects.

## 2. Distinctive Doctrines as Pentecostalism States Them

### 2.1 Baptism in the Holy Spirit

Classical Pentecostalism teaches a **distinct experience** of the Spirit, usually **subsequent to conversion**, called the **baptism in the Holy Spirit**, whose "**initial evidence**" is **speaking in tongues**. Some bodies (including many in the Charismatic movement) regard tongues as one evidence among several rather than the necessary sign.

### 2.2 The Continuation of the Charisms

Pentecostals reject **cessationism** — the claim that the miraculous gifts ceased with the apostles — and hold that **all** the gifts of 1 Corinthians 12:8–10 remain for the Church: prophecy, healing, miracles, tongues and their interpretation, word of wisdom and knowledge, faith, and discernment of spirits. See [gifts-holy-spirit/README.md](../gifts-holy-spirit/README.md).

### 2.3 Divine Healing

Healing is central: many teach that healing is provided in the atonement and that it may be claimed by faith, with healing services and prayer teams a normal part of church life.

### 2.4 Worship

Worship is **spontaneous**: contemporary music, extended praise, **altar calls**, hand-raising, and expectant prayer. Eschatologically, much of the movement is **premillennial** and expects a final great revival ("the latter rain").

### 2.5 Ordination, Ministry, and Women

Pentecostal ministry is generally **spiritual rather than sacramental**; ordination does not confer an indelible character. Because the movement broke with the historic episcopate, its ministries lack apostolic succession in the Catholic sense. Many classical Pentecostal bodies (e.g., the Assemblies of God) ordain **women** to ministry.

### 2.6 Oneness Pentecostalism

The **United Pentecostal Church International** and other "**Jesus Only**" bodies teach that the one God manifests as Father, Son, and Spirit — a form of **modalism** (Sabellianism) — and that baptism must be administered **"in the name of Jesus"** only. This is a denial of the **Trinity**.

## 3. The Catholic Assessment

### 3.1 The Church Affirms the Charisms

The Catholic Church **affirms** the charismatic gifts. The Second Vatican Council taught that "the Holy Spirit distributes special graces among the faithful of every rank," and that these **charisms** are "to be received with thanksgiving" (_Lumen Gentium_ 12; CCC 798–801, 2003). The Catholic Charismatic Renewal is a recognized ecclesial movement, and the Church has repeatedly blessed it. The Pentecostal insistence that the Spirit is **active and powerful** today is a genuine correction of a dry rationalism, and the Church does not deny it.

What the Church adds is **order and discernment**:

- "**Test everything; hold fast what is good**" (1 Thessalonians 5:19–21); "**test the spirits**" (1 John 4:1–3);
- "all things should be done **decently and in order**" (1 Corinthians 14:40);
- charisms are **services for the building up of the Church**, not marks of personal sanctity or proofs of salvation (1 Corinthians 12–13; CCC 800);
- the **Catholic "baptism in the Spirit"** is understood as a **renewal of Baptism and Confirmation**, not a second sacrament or a guarantee of the gifts (see _Graced and Gifted_ and the Church's guidance on the Renewal);
- **phenomena** such as being "slain in the Spirit" or "holy laughter" require careful discernment and are not to be equated with the worship of the Church; the **2024 Dicastery norms** on alleged supernatural phenomena and the Church's pastoral caution apply.

### 3.2 Where the Church Has Defined Otherwise

| Pentecostal position                                         | Catholic definition and source                                                                                                                                                                        |
| :----------------------------------------------------------- | :---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Baptism in the Spirit as a distinct, later experience        | The Spirit is given in Baptism; the Renewal is an **actualization** of Baptism and Confirmation. CCC 1265–1266, 1302–1305; John 3:5; Acts 2:38.                                                       |
| Tongues as the **necessary** initial evidence                | The Spirit gives gifts **as He wills** (1 Corinthians 12:11); no single gift is made the necessary sign. CCC 799–801.                                                                                 |
| Two ordinances; memorial Supper                              | Seven sacraments; the Eucharist is the Real Presence and the sacrifice of the Mass. Council of Trent, Sessions VII, XIII, XXII; CCC 1113–1134, 1362–1381.                                             |
| Healing guaranteed in the atonement, to be claimed           | Healing is a gift of God, not a right; suffering is not a sign of unbelief. CCC 1500–1505, 1508, 1499; the mystery of redemptive suffering, CCC 1505, 618.                                            |
| Prosperity gospel                                            | Poverty of spirit and detachment; the goods of this world are not the measure of grace. CCC 2544–2547, 1716–1717; the Beatitudes, Matthew 5:3–12.                                                     |
| Ordination as purely spiritual; no succession                | Holy Orders is a sacrament in three degrees, transmitted by apostolic succession. Council of Trent, Session XXIII; CCC 1536–1600.                                                                     |
| **Oneness** teaching (Jesus Only); a non-Trinitarian baptism | The **Trinity** is defined dogma; a baptism "in the name of Jesus only" is **not a valid Trinitarian Baptism**. Nicene-Constantinopolitan Creed; Council of Trent, Session VII, canon 4; CCC 232–267. |

### 3.3 Oneness Pentecostalism and the Trinity

The **Oneness** denial of the Trinity is not a difference of discipline or opinion but a rejection of **defined dogma**: Father, Son, and Holy Spirit are distinct Persons, each fully God, one God in three Persons (the **Athanasian Creed**; Lateran IV; Council of Florence; CCC 232–267). Because the sacrament of Baptism requires the **Trinitarian formula** (Matthew 28:19), a "Jesus-only" baptism is **invalid**; a person so "baptized" and seeking full communion must be validly baptized. This is a serious matter of dogma, stated here plainly and without rancor.

### 3.4 Baptismal Status of Other Pentecostals

Where Baptism is administered with **water** in the **Trinitarian name** (as in the Assemblies of God, COGIC, Foursquare, and most classical Pentecostal bodies), it is **valid** and constitutes a real bond of communion. Pentecostal ministers, however, are not validly ordained priests, and their Communion services are not valid Masses.

## 4. Latin and Eastern Catholic Perspectives

The Catholic Church is not a stranger to the charisms. The **Eastern Catholic** and **Eastern Orthodox** traditions have maintained an unbroken experience of the Holy Spirit's action: the **hesychast** tradition of the **Jesus Prayer**, the **Palamite** theology of the divine energies, the veneration of **healing saints** and **wonderworkers**, the **sacraments of healing**, and the invocation of the Spirit in every Liturgy ("**O Heavenly King, the Comforter, the Spirit of Truth…**"). The gifts the Pentecostals emphasize are the Church's own inheritance, guarded within the sacraments and the communion of the Church. See [church-history/eastern-catholic-churches.md](../church-history/eastern-catholic-churches.md) and [gifts-holy-spirit/README.md](../gifts-holy-spirit/README.md).

## 5. Ecumenical Relations

Catholic relations with Pentecostals have grown notably in recent decades:

- the **International Catholic–Pentecostal Dialogue** (from 1972), with reports on Christian initiation and the baptism in the Spirit (1977), the charismatic gifts (1983), and "**On Becoming a Christian**" (1998–2006);
- Pope **John Paul II** and Pope **Francis** have met Pentecostal leaders, and Catholic-Pentecostal dialogue has been one of the most productive "**third-millennium**" conversations;
- the **Catholic Charismatic Renewal** and CHARIS provide a Catholic home for charismatic fruitfulness, gathered around the **Eucharist**, under the **bishops**, and in the communion of the Church.

The remaining obstacles include **sola scriptura**, the **sacraments**, **ministry and orders**, and the doctrinal status given to particular spiritual experiences. The **Oneness** question is a matter of the **Trinity** and thus of the faith itself.

## Sources & Levels of Authority

- **Dogmatic definitions** — Nicene-Constantinopolitan Creed; **Athanasian Creed**; **Lateran IV** and **Florence** on the Trinity; Council of Trent: Session VII (sacraments and Baptism, including the Trinitarian form), Session XIII (Eucharist), Session XXII (Mass), Session XXIII (Orders).
- **Conciliar and papal teaching** — [_Lumen Gentium_](https://www.vatican.va/archive/hist_councils/ii_vatican_council/documents/vat-ii_const_19641121_lumen-gentium_en.html) 12; [_Unitatis Redintegratio_](https://www.vatican.va/archive/hist_councils/ii_vatican_council/documents/vat-ii_decree_19641121_unitatis-redintegratio_en.html); Pope Paul VI's address to the Catholic Charismatic Renewal (1975); John Paul II's addresses of 1979 and later; the establishment of CHARIS (2019); the Dicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith's **2024 norms** on alleged supernatural phenomena.
- **Catechism** — CCC 232–267 (the Trinity), 683–747 (the Holy Spirit), 798–801 and 2003 (charisms), 1113–1134, 1265–1266, 1302–1305, 1362–1381, 1500–1510, 1536–1600, 2544–2547.
- **Pentecostal sources (reported, not endorsed)** — the doctrinal statements of the Assemblies of God, COGIC, and the Foursquare Church; the International Catholic–Pentecostal Dialogue reports.
- **Theological opinion** — the interpretation of particular charismatic phenomena is a matter for discernment, not dogma.
