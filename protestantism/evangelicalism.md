# Evangelicalism — A Catholic Comparative Study

> **Scope and method.** This document presents the modern **evangelical movement** from its own sources and then compares it with Catholic doctrine. It is written **for comparison and study only**, in the spirit of [protestantism/README.md](README.md). It endorses no Protestant position, and it does not confuse the modern movement called "evangelical" with the **Gospel** (_euangelion_) itself or with the Church's **evangelical counsels**.

**Evangelicalism** is not a denomination but a **trans-denominational movement**. It names a coalition of Christians — Baptist, Presbyterian, Pentecostal, Methodist, Anglican, and nondenominational — who share a family resemblance in doctrine and practice rather than a single confession or hierarchy. Its adherents number in the hundreds of millions worldwide and it is one of the most dynamic forces in global Protestantism, especially in the global South.

---

## 1. What "Evangelical" Means

The word has three distinct senses that must not be confused:

1. **Historically European** — in continental Europe, "evangelical" (_evangelisch_) means **Protestant**, and in particular Lutheran or Reformed (the _Evangelische Kirche_).
2. **Confessionally Reformation** — the "evangelical" churches of the Reformation over against Rome.
3. **The modern movement** — the Anglo-American trans-denominational coalition described in this document. It is this third sense that is treated here.

The modern movement is commonly defined by what the historian **David Bebbington** called the "**quadrilateral**" (1989):

- **Biblicism** — the Bible as the ultimate authority;
- **Crucicentrism** — the centrality of Christ's atoning death on the cross;
- **Conversionism** — the necessity of a personal conversion or new birth;
- **Activism** — evangelism, mission, and social engagement.

---

## 2. Historical Origins of the Modern Movement

### 2.1 Roots: Reformation, Puritanism, Pietism, Revival

Modern evangelicalism draws on the **Reformation** (see [lutheranism.md](lutheranism.md), [reformed-churches.md](reformed-churches.md), [anglicanism.md](anglicanism.md)), on **Puritan** piety, on German **Pietism** (Spener, Francke), and above all on the **eighteenth-century Evangelical Revival**: **John Wesley** (see [methodism.md](methodism.md)), **George Whitefield**, and **Jonathan Edwards**.

### 2.2 Fundamentalism (1910–1930s)

In response to theological liberalism, conservative Protestants published **_The Fundamentals_** (1910–1915) and organized the **World's Christian Fundamentals Association** (1919). The **Scopes "monkey" trial** (1925) and the ensuing cultural retreat hardened a **separatist** fundamentalism that withdrew from mainline institutions.

### 2.3 Neo-Evangelicalism (1940s–1970s)

After the Second World War a new generation sought to combine orthodox doctrine with cultural engagement:

- the **National Association of Evangelicals** (1942);
- **Fuller Theological Seminary** (1947);
- **Billy Graham**'s crusades and the **Billy Graham Evangelistic Association** (1950);
- **Christianity Today** (1956), with **Carl F. H. Henry** as its first editor;
- the **Lausanne Congress on World Evangelization** (1974) and the **Lausanne Covenant**, shaped by **John Stott** and Graham.

### 2.4 Contemporary Evangelicalism

- the **Religious Right** (Jerry Falwell's Moral Majority, 1979; Pat Robertson's Christian Coalition, 1989);
- the **"Young, Restless, Reformed"** revival of Calvinism (2000s);
- the **emerging church** and the growth of **nondenominational** megachurches;
- the worldwide shift of the movement's center of gravity to **Africa, Asia, and Latin America**;
- the **Evangelicals and Catholics Together** initiatives (from 1994), which sought common ground with Catholics on doctrine and culture (see below).

---

## 3. What Evangelicals Hold in Common

Because evangelicalism has no single magisterium, its unity is a **family resemblance** rather than a confession. Common convictions include:

- the **Trinity** (in the great majority of bodies);
- the **deity**, **atoning death**, and **bodily resurrection** of Jesus Christ;
- the **authority and sufficiency of Scripture**, often with a doctrine of **inerrancy** (the **Chicago Statement on Biblical Inerrancy**, 1978);
- **salvation by grace through faith** in Christ alone;
- the necessity of a personal **conversion** and a life of **discipleship**;
- the **Second Coming** of Christ, often in a premillennial or dispensational form (see [eschatology/README.md](../eschatology/README.md));
- **evangelism** and **world mission** as the Church's central task;
- the **family**, **sexual morality** (generally traditional), and often **pro-life** commitments.

Evangelicals differ among themselves on **Baptism**, the **Lord's Supper**, **predestination**, church **polity**, the **charisms**, and the role of **women** in ministry — differences they often regard as secondary to the "**gospel**" they share.

## 4. The Catholic Assessment

### 4.1 What the Church Affirms in Common

The Catholic Church recognizes in evangelicalism a genuine Christian inheritance and a vital witness: the **Trinity**, the **creeds** (where held), the **deity and resurrection of Christ**, the centrality of the **cross**, a **love of Sacred Scripture**, the urgency of **conversion**, a serious moral life, missionary zeal, and often a strong defense of **human life** and the **family**. These are "elements of sanctification and truth" that build up the one Church (_Unitatis Redintegratio_ 3–4; _Lumen Gentium_ 8). Evangelicals who have received **Trinitarian Baptism** are separated brethren, and the Church gives thanks for the graces manifest among them.

### 4.2 Where the Church Has Defined Otherwise (Dogma)

| Evangelical position (in its common forms)                       | Catholic definition and source                                                                                                                                                                                                    |
| :--------------------------------------------------------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Scripture alone as ultimate authority                            | Scripture and Tradition form one deposit, authentically interpreted by the Church. Council of Trent, Session IV; _Dei Verbum_ 9–10; CCC 74–100.                                                                                   |
| The canon of 66 books                                            | The canon of **73** books includes the **deuterocanonical** books. Council of Trent, Session IV; CCC 120; [scripture/bible-history-canon.md](../scripture/bible-history-canon.md).                                                |
| Justification by faith alone, often as forensic imputation       | Justification is remission of sin and infusion of grace; faith working through love. Council of Trent, Session VI; CCC 1987–2029. (The _Joint Declaration_, 1999, marks real convergence — see [lutheranism.md](lutheranism.md).) |
| Two sacraments/ordinances                                        | Seven sacraments. Council of Trent, Session VII, canons 1 and 2; CCC 1113–1134, 1210.                                                                                                                                             |
| The Supper as memorial or spiritual feeding                      | Transubstantiation and the Real Presence; the Mass as sacrifice. Council of Trent, Sessions XIII, XXII; CCC 1362–1381.                                                                                                            |
| Ministry without apostolic succession; no sacramental priesthood | Holy Orders, three degrees, apostolic succession. Council of Trent, Session XXIII; CCC 1536–1600.                                                                                                                                 |
| Rejection of the papacy                                          | The pope is the successor of Peter by divine institution. Vatican I, _Pastor Aeternus_; CCC 880–887.                                                                                                                              |
| Rejection of purgatory, invocation of saints, Marian doctrine    | Defined at Trent, Session XXV; CCC 956, 963–975, 1030–1032.                                                                                                                                                                       |
| Dispensationalism (where held)                                   | A modern theological system foreign to Catholic eschatology; a matter of **theological opinion**, not Catholic teaching. CCC 668–677, 1038–1050.                                                                                  |
| Authority reduced to private judgment                            | Christ entrusted His teaching authority to the Church; "**he who hears you hears me**" (Luke 10:16). _Dei Verbum_ 10; CCC 85–87, 888–892.                                                                                         |

### 4.3 Scripture, Authority, and the Canon

The deepest structural difference is **authority**. Evangelicalism holds the **Bible alone** as the final arbiter, and leaves its interpretation to the individual under the Spirit's guidance. The Catholic Church holds that Scripture is read **within the living Tradition of the Church**, guided by the **Magisterium** (_Dei Verbum_ 9–10; CCC 85–87). This bears directly on the **canon**: the same Church that produced the New Testament canon under the Spirit's guidance is the Church that reads it (CCC 120; Council of Trent, Session IV).

The Catholic response to **biblical fundamentalism** is set out in the Pontifical Biblical Commission's 1993 document **_The Interpretation of the Bible in the Church_**, which welcomes the fundamentalist love of Scripture but criticizes its refusal of the human and historical dimension of the text, its insistence on an undifferentiated literalism, and its neglect of the Church's interpretive tradition. Faith and reason cooperate: "**faith and reason are like two wings on which the human spirit rises to the contemplation of truth**" (_Fides et Ratio_, 1998).

### 4.4 Baptism, Sacraments, and the Church

Where evangelicals administer **Trinitarian Baptism**, it is **valid**; the Church does not rebaptize. Their ministers are not validly ordained priests, and their services of the Lord's Supper are not valid Masses. The Church's fuller answer to the "**regenerate church**" instinct is that the Church is at once **holy and always in need of purification** (CCC 827), a **communion of sinners** with the sacraments as Christ's medicine — not a society of the already-perfect.

### 4.5 _Evangelicals and Catholics Together_

From **1994**, a group of evangelical and Catholic leaders, convened by **Charles Colson** and **Richard John Neuhaus**, issued **_Evangelicals and Catholics Together: The Christian Mission in the Third Millennium_** (1994) and **_The Gift of Salvation_** (1997). These statements affirmed common ground on the **creeds**, **justification**, and **Scripture**, and proposed cooperation on moral and cultural questions. They are **not magisterial documents**; they are private ecumenical initiatives, valuable as a sign of goodwill and convergence, but not binding on Catholics and not a substitute for the Church's own teaching. Some Catholic authorities were cautious about their formulations, and they should be received as theology, not as doctrine.

## 5. Latin and Eastern Catholic Perspectives

The Catholic response to evangelicalism is not merely Latin. The **Eastern Catholic** and **Eastern Orthodox** Churches share the same answer to _sola scriptura_ and _sola fide_ — Scripture read within the Tradition and the Liturgy, and salvation as **theosis**, the transformation of the whole person by grace — while framing it in the language of the Fathers, the icons, and the divine energies. The **Jesus Prayer**, the **Liturgy**, and the **sacraments** are the Eastern path to the same holiness the evangelical movement rightly seeks. The Church is one, East and West, in holding that the **Word of God is not a book alone but a Person** (_Dei Verbum_ 2, 4: Christ, the Word made flesh), and that He is found in His Body, the Church. See [church-history/eastern-catholic-churches.md](../church-history/eastern-catholic-churches.md).

## 6. Ecumenical Relations

Catholic engagement with evangelicalism takes several forms:

- the **International Catholic–Evangelical Dialogue** and the **Pontifical Council for Promoting Christian Unity**'s contacts with evangelical bodies, especially on Scripture and mission;
- **_Evangelicals and Catholics Together_** and similar national initiatives (as noted above, private, not magisterial);
- cooperation on **religious liberty**, **human rights**, **religious persecution**, and the **pro-life** cause;
- the **Lausanne** movement's engagement with Catholic mission theology, and the growth of shared reflection on the **Great Commission**.

The central obstacles are **authority** (Scripture and Tradition), the **canon**, the **sacraments**, **orders**, and the **Church**. The Catholic Church seeks visible unity in faith, sacraments, and government, "**that they may all be one**" (John 17:21; _Ut Unum Sint_ 4, 9).

## Sources & Levels of Authority

- **Dogmatic definitions** — Council of Trent: Session IV (Scripture, Tradition, canon), Session VI (justification), Session VII (sacraments), Session XIII (Eucharist), Session XXII (Mass), Session XXIII (Orders), Session XXV (purgatory, saints).
- **Conciliar and papal teaching** — [_Dei Verbum_](https://www.vatican.va/archive/hist_councils/ii_vatican_council/documents/vat-ii_const_19651118_dei-verbum_en.html); [_Lumen Gentium_](https://www.vatican.va/archive/hist_councils/ii_vatican_council/documents/vat-ii_const_19641121_lumen-gentium_en.html); [_Unitatis Redintegratio_](https://www.vatican.va/archive/hist_councils/ii_vatican_council/documents/vat-ii_decree_19641121_unitatis-redintegratio_en.html); [_Ut Unum Sint_](https://www.vatican.va/content/john-paul-ii/en/encyclicals/documents/hf_jp-ii_enc_25051995_ut-unum-sint.html) (1995); [_Fides et Ratio_](https://www.vatican.va/content/john-paul-ii/en/encyclicals/documents/hf_jp-ii_enc_14091998_fides-et-ratio.html) (1998); _Dominus Iesus_ (2000); the **Pontifical Biblical Commission**, **_The Interpretation of the Bible in the Church_** (1993).
- **Catechism** — CCC 66–100, 120, 748–975, 1113–1134, 1362–1381, 1536–1600, 1987–2029.
- **Evangelical sources (reported, not endorsed)** — _The Fundamentals_ (1910–15); the Chicago Statement on Biblical Inerrancy (1978); the Lausanne Covenant (1974); the writings of Carl F. H. Henry, John Stott, and J. I. Packer; _Evangelicals and Catholics Together_ (1994, 1997).
- **Theological opinion** — dispensationalism and the various models of the atonement are cited as systems of opinion, not dogma.
