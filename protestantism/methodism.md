# Methodism — A Catholic Comparative Study

> **Scope and method.** This document presents the Methodist (Wesleyan) tradition from its own sources and then compares it with Catholic doctrine. It is written **for comparison and study only**, in the spirit of [protestantism/README.md](README.md). It endorses no Protestant position and treats Methodists as separated brethren who share the one Baptism (_Unitatis Redintegratio_ 22).

**Methodism** began not as a Church but as a renewal movement within the Church of England. Its founders, **John Wesley (1703–1791)** and his brother **Charles Wesley (1707–1788)**, sought to revive "scriptural holiness" through disciplined prayer, the sacraments, small-group accountability, and itinerant preaching. Within a century the movement had become a worldwide family of churches distinguished by its **Arminian** theology, its emphasis on **sanctification**, its missionary and social zeal, and its hymnody.

---

## 1. Historical Origins

### 1.1 Oxford, Georgia, and Aldersgate

At Oxford, the Wesley brothers and their companions formed the **Holy Club** (1729), whose methodical devotion earned them the nickname "**Methodists**." A failed missionary venture to Georgia (1735–1737) led John Wesley to a crisis of faith. On **24 May 1738**, at a Moravian meeting in **Aldersgate Street, London**, he experienced his heart "strangely warmed" — his evangelical conversion and the experiential center of the movement.

### 1.2 Societies, Classes, and Field Preaching

From 1739 Wesley preached in the open air, organized converts into **societies**, subdivided into **classes** for mutual accountability, and appointed **lay preachers** under his supervision. He remained a priest of the Church of England until his death and insisted the societies were not a separate church — yet the logic of the revival pulled them toward independence.

### 1.3 The Break with the Church of England (1784)

In **1784**, faced with the American colonies' need for ordained ministers after the Revolution, Wesley ordained **Thomas Coke** and others and sent them to America. The **Christmas Conference** at Baltimore (1784) organized the **Methodist Episcopal Church**. In Britain the break came after Wesley's death. The **Deed of Declaration** (1784) had already given Methodist chapels a legal constitution.

### 1.4 Divisions, Unions, and Holiness

American Methodism split over slavery in **1844** (Methodist Episcopal Church, South), reunited in **1939**, and merged with the Evangelical United Brethren in **1968** to form **The United Methodist Church**. African American Methodism organized separately: the **African Methodist Episcopal Church** (1816, under **Richard Allen**), the **African Methodist Episcopal Zion Church** (1821), and the **Christian Methodist Episcopal Church** (1870). In Britain, the **Methodist Church of Great Britain** was formed by union in **1932**.

Out of Methodist revival came the **Holiness movement** — camp meetings, the doctrine of **entire sanctification**, and bodies such as the **Church of the Nazarene** (1908) and the **Salvation Army** (founded by William and Catherine Booth, 1865), and it fed directly into **Pentecostalism** (see [pentecostalism.md](pentecostalism.md)).

> **A recent development to note.** In 2022–2024 the United Methodist Church experienced the departure of a large number of congregations (many joining the **Global Methodist Church**, formed 2022), and the 2024 General Conference removed the denomination's traditional prohibitions relating to same-sex marriage and the ordination of persons in same-sex relationships. These disciplinary changes bear on the moral and ecclesiological questions treated below and are reported here as they occurred.

## 2. Distinctive Doctrines as Methodism States Them

### 2.1 Arminian Soteriology and Prevenient Grace

Unlike Calvinism, Methodism holds:

- **conditional election** — God's election is based on foreseen faith, not an unconditional decree;
- **universal atonement** — Christ died for all;
- **resistible grace** — grace can be resisted;
- **prevenient grace** — God's grace precedes and enables human response, restoring the freedom of the will that sin had bound.

Wesley taught the "**via salutis**": prevenient grace, justifying grace, and sanctifying grace.

### 2.2 Justification and Sanctification

Wesley held **justification by faith** — a real, gracious forgiveness — but insisted that justification and sanctification be held together: "faith working by love." The distinctive Wesleyan doctrine is **entire sanctification** (Christian perfection): a second, instantaneous work of grace in which the believer is cleansed of the love of sin and perfected in love, though still liable to infirmity. **Assurance** of salvation is emphasized as the Spirit's witness.

### 2.3 The Sacraments and Worship

The **Articles of Religion** Wesley sent to America (1784) — a reduction of the Thirty-Nine Articles — retain the "two Sacraments ordained of Christ": **Baptism** (administered to infants and adults) and the **Lord's Supper**. Baptism is a genuine means of grace in the Wesleyan tradition, and Charles Wesley's Eucharistic hymns express a high sacramental devotion ("O the depth of love divine, the unfathomable grace"). Methodists differ on the mode of Christ's presence, with the United Methodist Church treating it as a mystery. Practice is generally an **open table**, extended to all who love Christ.

### 2.4 Polity: Connectionalism

Methodist polity is **connectional**: local churches are linked by circuits, districts, annual conferences, and a general conference. Bishops in the American tradition are elected **superintendents** rather than a distinct sacramental order, though they exercise real oversight. The **class meeting** and the **society** formed the movement's original engine of discipleship.

### 2.5 The "Wesleyan Quadrilateral"

Modern Methodist theology often describes its method as the **Wesleyan Quadrilateral** — Scripture, tradition, reason, and experience (a term coined by Albert Outler in the 1960s, not by Wesley). In practice this has been invoked to justify doctrinal development and change, and has become a point of internal contention.

## 3. The Catholic Assessment

### 3.1 What the Church Affirms in Common

The Catholic Church recognizes in Methodism a genuine Christian inheritance: the **Trinity**, the **creeds**, a love of **Scripture**, the validity of **infant Baptism** (in most Methodist bodies), faith in the **real presence** of Christ in the Supper, a passionate concern for **holiness of life**, a strong **missionary and charitable** impulse, and a rich **hymnody** and **devotional** tradition. The Wesleyan emphasis on **grace** — prevenient, justifying, and sanctifying — converges in important respects with the Catholic doctrine of grace (CCC 1996–2005).

### 3.2 Where the Church Has Defined Otherwise (Dogma)

> **On the Wesleyan doctrine of perfection.** The Catholic Church teaches the **universal call to holiness** (_Lumen Gentium_ 39–42; CCC 2013) and the possibility of heroic sanctity, but she distinguishes this from the claim that a "second work" of grace can remove the _concupiscence_ that remains in the baptized and make absolute perfection of love a stable possession. The Council of Trent defined that even the justified retain concupiscence, which is not sin but inclines toward it, and that no one can know with certitude that he has obtained the grace of final perseverance (Session VI, canons on justification; CCC 1264, 2015).

### 3.3 The 2006 Accession to the Joint Declaration

A notable ecumenical act: in **2006** the **World Methodist Council**, meeting in Seoul, formally **acceded to the _Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification_** (1999) with the Catholic Church and the Lutheran World Federation. This is the most advanced convergence between Catholics and Methodists on justification. It does not resolve the remaining differences on the sacraments, ministry, and the Church.

### 3.4 The Salvation Army and Non-Sacramental Bodies

Some Methodist-derived bodies, most notably the **Salvation Army**, abandoned the celebration of the sacraments (the Army ceased administering Baptism and the Lord's Supper in 1883, though it does not formally deny their validity). This raises a specific canonical question: an unbaptized Christian has not received the sacrament of Baptism, and the Catholic Church would require Baptism before reception into full communion. It also illustrates the distance that can open between a Wesleyan emphasis on **experience** and the objective means of grace.

## 4. Latin and Eastern Catholic Perspectives

The pursuit of **holiness** and **perfection** that animates Methodism finds its Catholic home in the doctrine of **divinization** — "the Son of God became man so that we might become God" (CCC 460, citing St. Athanasius) — and in the Church's rich ascetical and mystical tradition: the desert Fathers, the **hesychast** tradition of the **Jesus Prayer**, and the teaching on the **theological virtues** and the **gifts of the Holy Spirit**. In the East, the goal of the Christian life is **theosis**, and the means are the **sacraments**, **fasting**, and **unceasing prayer** — not a second, sacramentally independent "work." The Eastern Catholic and Orthodox witness thus supplies what a purely individual "sanctification experience" lacks: the ecclesial and sacramental context of growth in grace. See [church-history/eastern-catholic-churches.md](../church-history/eastern-catholic-churches.md) and [gifts-holy-spirit/README.md](../gifts-holy-spirit/README.md).

## 5. Ecumenical Relations

Catholic-Methodist dialogue has been among the most fruitful:

- the **International Methodist–Roman Catholic Dialogue** produced the **Denver Report** (1971), the **Nairobi Report** (1986), **_Speaking the Truth in Love_** (1994), and **_The Grace Given You in Christ_** (2006);
- the **World Methodist Council's accession to the _Joint Declaration_** (2006) marked real convergence on justification;
- Catholic and Methodist bodies collaborate in charitable and social work, and Pope Francis has met Methodist leaders in Rome and in 2019 in Geneva addressed the World Council of Churches.

The remaining obstacles concern the **sacraments**, **ministry and orders**, the **papacy**, and moral questions. The Church seeks the unity Christ willed (_Ut Unum Sint_ 4).

## Sources & Levels of Authority

- **Dogmatic definitions** — Council of Trent: Session VI (justification, assurance, concupiscence), Session VII (sacraments and Baptism), Session XIII (Eucharist), Session XXIII (Orders), Session XXV (purgatory).
- **Conciliar and papal teaching** — [_Lumen Gentium_](https://www.vatican.va/archive/hist_councils/ii_vatican_council/documents/vat-ii_const_19641121_lumen-gentium_en.html); [_Dei Verbum_](https://www.vatican.va/archive/hist_councils/ii_vatican_council/documents/vat-ii_const_19651118_dei-verbum_en.html); [_Ut Unum Sint_](https://www.vatican.va/content/john-paul-ii/en/encyclicals/documents/hf_jp-ii_enc_25051995_ut-unum-sint.html) (1995); [_Redemptionis Sacramentum_](https://www.vatican.va/roman_curia/congregations/ccdds/documents/rc_con_ccdds_doc_20040423_redemptionis-sacramentum_en.html) (2004); the _Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification_ (1999; World Methodist Council accession, 2006).
- **Catechism** — CCC 460, 748–975, 1113–1134, 1213–1284, 1362–1381, 1536–1600, 1987–2029.
- **Methodist sources (reported, not endorsed)** — the Articles of Religion (1784), Wesley's sermons and _Standard Sermons_, Charles Wesley's hymns, and the _Book of Discipline_.
- **Theological opinion** — the "Wesleyan Quadrilateral" is a twentieth-century theological construct, cited as such.
