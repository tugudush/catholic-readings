# The Reformed Churches — A Catholic Comparative Study

> **Scope and method.** This document presents the Reformed (Calvinist and Presbyterian) tradition from its own confessions and then compares it with Catholic doctrine. It is written **for comparison and study only**, in the spirit of [protestantism/README.md](README.md). It endorses no Protestant position and treats Reformed Christians as separated brethren who share the one Baptism (_Unitatis Redintegratio_ 22).

The **Reformed tradition** is the second great stream of the Reformation. Where Lutheranism flowed from Wittenberg, the Reformed churches flowed from **Zürich** under Huldrych Zwingli and from **Geneva** under John Calvin. Its confessional children include the Continental Reformed churches, the **Presbyterian** churches of the English-speaking world, and the **Congregationalist** churches of New England. Historically it has been the most theologically systematic, the most anti-sacramental, and the most politically assertive of the magisterial Protestant families.

---

## 1. Historical Origins

### 1.1 Huldrych Zwingli in Zürich (1484–1531)

**Huldrych Zwingli**, a humanist priest in Zürich, began preaching reform in 1519 and by 1525 had carried the city council with him. His program was more radical than Luther's in worship: he rejected the **Real Presence** — holding the bread and wine to be signs and memorials only — and presided over the removal of images, organs, and the Mass from the churches. He died in battle at Kappel in 1531.

### 1.2 John Calvin in Geneva (1509–1564)

**John Calvin**, a French lawyer turned theologian, published the **_Institutes of the Christian Religion_** in 1536 (final edition 1559) and organized the church of Geneva into a model of Reformed discipline: consistory, pastors, doctors, elders, and deacons. From Geneva, Reformed teaching spread to France (the **Huguenots**), the Netherlands, Scotland, England, and beyond. Its polity and doctrine were carried to the New World by the Puritans and Presbyterians.

### 1.3 The Reformed Confessions

Unlike Lutheranism, the Reformed tradition produced many confessions, not one book:

- the **Zwinglian** and **Bullinger** texts: the _First_ and _Second Helvetic Confessions_ (1536; 1566);
- the **Gallican Confession** (1559) and the **Belgic Confession** (1561);
- the **Heidelberg Catechism** (1563);
- the **Canons of Dort** (1619), which defined the Calvinist doctrine of predestination;
- the **Westminster Confession of Faith** (1646) and the **Westminster Shorter** and **Larger Catechisms** (1647), the classic Presbyterian symbols;
- the **Savoy Declaration** (1658) and the **1689 London Baptist Confession** (see [baptists.md](baptists.md)).

## 2. Distinctive Doctrines as the Reformed State Them

### 2.1 The Sovereignty of God and Double Predestination

The _Westminster Confession_ and the _Canons of Dort_ teach that God, from eternity, freely decreed the salvation of some and — with equal purpose — the reprobation of others, "for the praise of His glorious justice." The familiar **TULIP** mnemonic summarizes the five points of Dort:

- **T**otal depravity (original sin leaves the will utterly unable to turn to God);
- **U**nconditional election (election is not based on foreseen faith or merits);
- **L**imited atonement (Christ died for the elect only);
- **I**rresistible grace (the elect cannot finally resist God's saving grace);
- **P**erseverance of the saints (the elect cannot finally fall away).

### 2.2 Justification by Faith Alone, with a Reformed Accent

Like all Protestants, the Reformed hold justification by faith alone. Classic Calvinism adds the **covenant theology** of grace, and lays great weight on the certainty of election and the assurance of salvation.

### 2.3 The Lord's Supper: Spiritual, not Corporeal

Reformed teaching is divided historically between the **memorialism** of Zwingli (the Supper as a symbolic remembrance) and the **spiritual presence** of Calvin (the believer truly feeds on Christ, but spiritually, by faith, and not by any bodily presence of the Lord's flesh in the elements). Either way, the Reformed deny both **transubstantiation** and the Lutheran **sacramental union** (see [lutheranism.md](lutheranism.md)). The _Westminster Confession_ says the "outward elements" are not changed into the substance of Christ's flesh and blood.

### 2.4 The Sacraments Reduced, and Baptism

The Reformed recognize only **Baptism** (administered to infants) and the **Lord's Supper** (received spiritually). They reject the sacrificial character of the Mass, the reserved Sacrament, adoration, and the other five sacraments. Baptism is a **sign and seal** of the covenant, not, as the Catholic Church teaches, an instrument that confers regeneration and an indelible character.

### 2.5 Worship, Polity, and Discipline

The Reformed developed the **regulative principle of worship** — that only what Scripture commands may be done in worship — leading to iconoclasm, the stripping of altars, and simple preaching services. In polity they divided between **presbyterian** government (ruling elders and courts — session, presbytery, synod, general assembly) and **congregationalist** government (each local church self-governing), rejecting the episcopate and the papacy alike. A strict **Lord's Day sabbath** and church **discipline** were characteristic.

### 2.6 Rejection of Catholic Devotion

Purgatory, prayers for the dead, invocation of saints, Marian devotion, relics, images, and the celebration of the saints' days were abolished. The Second Commandment was read as forbidding sacred images.

## 3. The Catholic Assessment

### 3.1 What the Church Affirms in Common

The Reformed share with Catholics the **Trinity**, the **two natures of Christ**, the **creeds**, the Old and New Testaments, the **Ten Commandments**, the **Baptism** of infants in the Trinitarian name, the **Lord's Day**, and the hope of eternal life. Their love of Scripture, catechesis, and church discipline are genuine Christian goods (_Unitatis Redintegratio_ 3–4).

### 3.2 Where the Church Has Defined Otherwise (Dogma)

| Reformed position                                       | Catholic definition and source                                                                                                                                                                                              |
| :------------------------------------------------------ | :-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Double predestination; limited atonement                | God wills the salvation of all; predestination to grace is real, but reprobation is not a positive decree to damn anyone apart from foreseen sin. Council of Trent, Session VI, canons 15–17; 1 Timothy 2:4; CCC 1037, 600. |
| Total depravity; the will cannot cooperate              | By grace, the will is freed and can cooperate with God. Council of Trent, Session VI, canons 4–5; CCC 1989–1995, 2001–2002.                                                                                                 |
| Justification as forensic imputation                    | Justification is remission of sin and infusion of sanctifying grace. Council of Trent, Session VI, canons 9–16; CCC 1987–2029.                                                                                              |
| Two sacraments (Baptism, Supper)                        | Seven sacraments instituted by Christ. Council of Trent, Session VII, canons 1 and 2; CCC 1113–1134, 1210.                                                                                                                  |
| Baptism as a sign and seal only                         | Baptism is a sacrament that confers grace and an indelible character, and is necessary for salvation where its necessity is known. Council of Trent, Session VII, canons on Baptism; CCC 1257–1274.                         |
| The Supper: memorial or spiritual presence only         | Transubstantiation; the Real Presence of Christ's Body and Blood, Soul and Divinity. Council of Trent, Session XIII, canons 1–4; CCC 1373–1381.                                                                             |
| The Mass is not a sacrifice                             | The Mass is the same sacrifice of the Cross made present. Council of Trent, Session XXII; CCC 1362–1372.                                                                                                                    |
| Rejection of the papacy and episcopacy                  | Holy Orders is a sacrament; the pope is the successor of Peter by divine institution. Council of Trent, Session XXIII; Vatican I, _Pastor Aeternus_; CCC 880–887, 1536–1600.                                                |
| No invocation of saints, no purgatory, no sacred images | Defined at Trent, Session XXV; CCC 956, 1030–1032, 2683; on images, [sacramentals/sacred-images.md](../sacramentals/sacred-images.md).                                                                                      |

> **A note on "double predestination."** The Catholic Church permits a range of **theological opinion** on the mystery of predestination — the Thomist and Molinist schools differ — but she excludes the Calvinist construction on two points: universal salvific will and the refusal to make God the positive author of sin. The Council of Trent's canons on justification (especially canons 15–17) exclude predestination to evil; see also CCC 1037.

### 3.3 Ministry and Orders

Because the Reformed confessions deny the sacrament of Order and the sacrificial priesthood, and their ordinations lack the ancient form and intention, the **Catholic Church does not recognize Reformed ordinations as valid**. Reformed ministers are therefore not validly ordained priests; their Communion services are not valid Masses. Their **Trinitarian Baptism** is valid. See [sacraments/holy-orders.md](../sacraments/holy-orders.md).

### 3.4 Matters of Discipline and Opinion

Calvin's Geneva and its heirs imposed disciplines far stricter than anything required by Catholic dogma — sumptuary laws, mandatory church attendance, and (in some cases) capital penalties for heresy, as in the execution of Michael Servetus in 1553. These belong to the history of Reformed **discipline**, not to defined Catholic teaching on the one side or the other. Catholic **clerical celibacy** in the Latin Rite, by contrast, is a **discipline**, not a dogma, and the Eastern Catholic Churches ordain married priests.

## 4. Latin and Eastern Catholic Perspectives

The Eastern Churches — both Catholic and Orthodox — share with Catholics the **seven sacraments**, the **Real Presence**, the **sacrificial Liturgy**, the **veneration of icons** (defended dogmatically at the Second Council of Nicaea, 787), the **invocation of the saints**, and prayer for the dead. The Reformed iconoclasm and sacramentarianism are therefore rejected by the whole Catholic communion, East and West, and not only by Latin polemic. The Eastern witness is treated in [church-history/eastern-catholic-churches.md](../church-history/eastern-catholic-churches.md) and [church-history/orthodox-east-and-the-reformation.md](../church-history/orthodox-east-and-the-reformation.md).

## 5. Ecumenical Relations

Catholic dialogue with the Reformed tradition has been extensive but less convergent than with the Lutherans:

- the **International Reformed–Catholic Dialogue** produced **_Towards a Common Understanding of the Church_** (1990) and **_Communion: Church and Sacraments_** (2018);
- the **World Communion of Reformed Churches** and the **World Alliance of Reformed Churches** have engaged in bilateral conversations on justification, ecclesiology, and ethics;
- the **_Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification_** (1999) is a Lutheran-Catholic text; Reformed bodies have studied but not all have formally received it.

The deepest remaining obstacles concern the **Church**, the **sacraments**, and **ministry**, along with the doctrine of predestination and the practice of infant communion in the East. The Church seeks full communion while maintaining the faith once delivered to the saints (_Ut Unum Sint_ 18).

## Sources & Levels of Authority

- **Dogmatic definitions** — Council of Trent: Session VI (justification), Session VII (sacraments and Baptism), Session XIII (Eucharist), Session XXII (Mass), Session XXIII (Orders), Session XXV (purgatory, saints, images); Second Council of Nicaea (787) on images.
- **Conciliar and papal teaching** — [_Unitatis Redintegratio_](https://www.vatican.va/archive/hist_councils/ii_vatican_council/documents/vat-ii_decree_19641121_unitatis-redintegratio_en.html); [_Lumen Gentium_](https://www.vatican.va/archive/hist_councils/ii_vatican_council/documents/vat-ii_const_19641121_lumen-gentium_en.html); _Pastor Aeternus_ (Vatican I, 1870); [_Ut Unum Sint_](https://www.vatican.va/content/john-paul-ii/en/encyclicals/documents/hf_jp-ii_enc_25051995_ut-unum-sint.html); _Dominus Iesus_ (2000).
- **Catechism** — CCC 74–100, 748–975, 1210–1666, 1987–2029, 1030–1032.
- **Reformed sources (reported, not endorsed)** — the Second Helvetic Confession (1566), Belgic Confession (1561), Heidelberg Catechism (1563), Canons of Dort (1619), Westminster Confession (1646), Calvin's _Institutes_ (1559).
- **Theological opinion** — cited as such; the Thomist–Molinist debate over predestination is a matter of theological school, not dogma.
