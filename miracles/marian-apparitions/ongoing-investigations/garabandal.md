# Our Lady of Garabandal (1961–1965)

## Theological Introduction to Private Revelation
The alleged apparitions of Garabandal, in the mountains of Cantabria, northern Spain, are among the most debated cases of modern Marian devotion:
- As private revelation (CCC 67), the events are subordinate to the public Revelation of Christ; the Church has **never approved** them, and belief in them is not required and may be prudently withheld.
- The local bishop issued a negative judgment in 1967 (*non constat de supernaturalitate*), and the case has never received the approbation of the Holy See. It remains, however, a case that the Church has never formally condemned by name, and devotion continues privately among some of the faithful — hence its classification as an ongoing, unapproved phenomenon rather than a formally rejected one.

## The Alleged Apparitions
Between **June 18, 1961** and **1965**, four girls of the village of San Sebastián de Garabandal — **Conchita González (12)**, **Jacinta González (11)**, **Mari Cruz González (11)**, and **Mari Loli Mazón (12)** — reported apparitions of the Archangel Saint Michael and then of the Blessed Virgin Mary.

### Key Historical Details:
1. **The Early Phenomena**: The children reported hearing voices and seeing lights, followed by apparitions of Saint Michael the Archangel, who announced the coming of the Virgin. Reports of "ecstasies," including the children walking backward rapidly with heads thrown back — phenomena that impressed some observers and troubled others — accompanied the events.
2. **The Message**: Mary, under the title "**Our Lady of Mount Carmel**" of Garabandal, is said to have delivered a message of penance, prayer, the Eucharist, and the Rosary, together with warnings of coming chastisements if the world did not repent. The devotion is famous for its prophecies of a "Warning," a "Miracle," and a "Chastisement" — elements that have no magisterial endorsement.
3. **The Secret**: Conchita reported receiving a secret to be revealed at an appointed time, a claim that has contributed to both popular fascination and ecclesiastical reserve.
4. **The Crowds and the Church's Response**: The village drew thousands of pilgrims. The local Ordinary, Bishop Doroteo Fernández of Santander, and successive commissions investigated; in 1967 the bishop declared that the events could not be established as supernatural (*non constat de supernaturalitate*), prohibited clerical involvement, and directed the faithful away from the site.
5. **The Later Lives**: The seers have maintained their accounts; Conchita González later married and has lived an ordinary life. No Church approval has ever been granted, and no formal condemnation naming the events has been issued, leaving the case in a state of official non-approval.

## Current Status
- **No Approval**: Garabandal has never received any episcopal or pontifical approval, and its characteristic prophecies and "secrets" are not part of Catholic teaching.
- **No Formal Condemnation**: The negative 1967 declaration and the prohibition of public cult mean the Church has judged the events *not established* as supernatural, while not issuing a formal decree of condemnation against the seers.
- **Pastoral Guidance**: The faithful are free to regard the events with prudence; they are not bound to believe them, and the Church's norms (both the 1978 norms and the 2024 *Norms for Proceeding in the Discernment of Alleged Supernatural Phenomena*) remind the faithful that private revelations can never correct or supplement the public Revelation of Christ. Pending any new judgment, Garabandal remains under investigation in the broad sense of an unapproved phenomenon.
