# Our Lady of Necedah (1950–1995)

## Theological Introduction to Private Revelation
The alleged apparitions of Necedah, in Juneau County, Wisconsin, are among the earliest and most clearly **rejected** American Marian cases:
- As private revelation (CCC 67), the events are subordinate to the public Revelation of Christ, and the Church's pastors have the authority to judge and prohibit them.
- The Diocese of La Crosse investigated the events and formally condemned them within five years of their beginning, and the condemnation has never been reversed.

## The Alleged Apparitions to Mary Ann Van Hoof
Beginning in **1950** and continuing until her death in **1984** (with the movement continuing to **1995**), **Mary Ann Van Hoof** (1909–1984), a farmer's wife of Necedah, Wisconsin, claimed to receive apparitions of the Blessed Virgin Mary on her property.

### Key Historical Details:
1. **The Messages**: Van Hoof's messages, delivered at outdoor gatherings that at times drew tens of thousands, combined calls to prayer and penance with apocalyptic warnings, condemnations of the post-conciliar Church, and sharply polemical predictions. She claimed Mary had designated her farm as a place of pilgrimage.
2. **The "Sealed Letter" and Claims**: Van Hoof claimed supernatural gifts and revelations about coming catastrophes, and promoted devotion to "Our Lady of Necedah" with a distinctive prayer and scapular-like sacramentals — claims that invited ecclesiastical scrutiny.
3. **The Crowds**: The site became a major center of American traditionalist popular piety in the 1950s, drawing pilgrims from across the country, many of whom reported graces and healings.
4. **The Seer's Later Years**: Van Hoof died in 1984; the movement continued for another decade under followers before largely fading, though a small remnant devotion persists.

## The Rejection by the Church
- **The 1955 Condemnation**: On **December 8, 1955**, the Diocese of La Crosse — then under Bishop John P. Treacy, with the investigation led by his coadjutor and successor, Bishop John Grellinger — issued a formal decree declaring that the alleged apparitions of Necedah were **not of supernatural origin** and that "**no credence should be given**" to the claims of Mary Ann Van Hoof.
- **The Prohibition of Public Cult**: The diocese prohibited public veneration at the site and forbade the promotion of the devotion, ordering the faithful to refrain from participation.
- **Continued Vigilance**: Successive bishops of La Crosse upheld the condemnation, and the case was repeatedly brought to the attention of the Holy See; the negative judgment was never reversed.

## Current Status
- **Formally Rejected**: The local Ordinary definitively judged the events not to be supernatural and prohibited adherence and promotion — a formal rejection of the alleged apparitions.
- **Obedience Required**: The faithful are bound to refrain from participating in the prohibited gatherings and from spreading the condemned messages; the case is frequently cited as a model of firm but measured ecclesiastical discipline toward unapproved claims.
- **A Cautionary Case**: Necedah illustrates the pattern — apocalyptic prediction, polemic against ecclesiastical authority, and an unapproved "cult" — that the Church's discernment norms are designed to address with pastoral firmness.
