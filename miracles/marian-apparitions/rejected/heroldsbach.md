# Our Lady of Heroldsbach (1949)

## Theological Introduction to Private Revelation
The alleged apparitions of Heroldsbach, in the Diocese of Bamberg, Bavaria, Germany, were among the most prominent European cases of the post-war era, and they were **condemned** by the Church:
- As private revelation (CCC 67), the events are subordinate to the public Revelation of Christ; the Church's pastors have the authority to judge and prohibit them.
- The events of 1949, which drew vast crowds to a small Bavarian village, were investigated by the Archbishop of Bamberg, judged not to be of supernatural origin, and formally prohibited, with the devotion suppressed.

## The Alleged Apparitions to the Children
Beginning in **October 1949**, four girls of Heroldsbach — **Kunigunde Ruf (9)**, **Anna Schlüter (8)**, **Maria Hecht (7)**, and **Elisabeth Böhmer (6)** — reported apparitions of the Blessed Virgin Mary near the village church, in the Archdiocese of Bamberg.

### Key Historical Details:
1. **The Apparitions**: The girls reported seeing a luminous Lady, often on the grounds of the parish, with messages of prayer and penance; the phenomena included reported ecstasies and unusual behavior that drew both believers and skeptics.
2. **The Crowds**: Within weeks, Heroldsbach attracted crowds of tens of thousands of pilgrims, making it one of the most frequented alleged apparition sites in post-war Germany and straining the resources and patience of the local Church and state.
3. **The Investigation**: Archbishop **Joseph Otto Kolb** of Bamberg initiated an investigation; the children were examined repeatedly, and the phenomena were studied by theologians and physicians, with considerable controversy over the children's accounts and their handling.
4. **The Civil Dimension**: The Bavarian state intervened, restricting access to the site, and the parents of the seers and some clergy faced legal proceedings — adding a civil-persecution dimension similar to Marpingen's.
5. **The Verdict**: The Church judged that the events could not be established as supernatural and that the manner of their promotion was disorderly; public veneration was prohibited.

## The Condemnation by the Church
- **The 1952 Decree**: On **January 31, 1952**, Archbishop Kolb issued a decree declaring that the supernatural character of the alleged apparitions of Heroldsbach was **not established** (*non constat de supernaturalitate*), prohibiting public devotion at the site, and ordering the removal of devotional objects.
- **The Destruction of the Chapel**: A chapel erected by the seers' families was demolished on the archbishop's orders in 1952, a striking act of discipline that ended the public cult.
- **The 1954 Confirmation**: The Holy Office confirmed the archbishop's judgment in **1954**, and the prohibition was never lifted, though a small private devotion continued among followers for decades.

## Current Status
- **Formally Rejected**: The local Ordinary, with the confirmation of the Holy See, determined that the events were not established as supernatural and suppressed the public cult — a formal rejection of the alleged apparitions.
- **Obedience Required**: The faithful are bound to refrain from promoting the rejected devotion; Heroldsbach is cited as a model of firm episcopal discipline exercised promptly and decisively.
