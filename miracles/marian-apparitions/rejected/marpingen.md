# Our Lady of Marpingen (1876)

## Theological Introduction to Private Revelation

The alleged apparitions of Marpingen, in the Saarland region of Germany, were among the first great "Marian wave" cases of the modern era, and they were **forbidden** by the Church:

- As private revelation (CCC 67), the events are subordinate to the public Revelation of Christ; the Church's pastors have the authority to judge and prohibit them.
- The events of 1876 — which drew national attention and were dubbed the "German Lourdes" — were investigated by the local Ordinary and prohibited within months of their beginning, and the prohibition has never been lifted.

## The Alleged Apparitions to the Three Girls

In **July 1876**, three girls of Marpingen — **Margaretha Kunz (8)**, **Susanna Leist (7)**, and **Katharina Hubertus (6)** — reported seeing a luminous woman near a wood called the Härtel, whom they identified as the Blessed Virgin Mary.

### Key Historical Details:

1. **The Apparitions**: Beginning on July 3, 1876, the girls reported repeated apparitions of a radiant Lady at the wood, accompanied by reported healings and unusual lights. News spread rapidly, and crowds of pilgrims — at times numbering thousands — flocked to Marpingen within days.
2. **The "German Lourdes"**: The events occurred in the wake of the Franco-Prussian War and amid the Kulturkampf (the Bismarckian struggle against the Catholic Church in the new German Empire); the site became a symbol of popular Catholic devotion and of resistance to state pressure, and its fame spread across Europe.
3. **The Investigation**: The Bishop of Trier, Bishop Matthias Eberhard, initiated a careful investigation. The girls' testimony was examined repeatedly, and the phenomena were subjected to scrutiny by clergy and physicians.
4. **The Civil Dimension**: The Prussian state intervened, closing the site to pilgrims and prosecuting the parents of the seers and the local clergy; the children were placed in state custody and questioned under pressure — a coercive civil dimension that complicated the case.
5. **The Verdict**: After investigation, the Church judged that the events could **not be established as supernatural**. The bishop prohibited public veneration at the site, and the pilgrimage was suppressed.

## The Rejection by the Church

- **The 1876 Prohibition**: In **1876–1877**, Bishop Eberhard of Trier declared that the supernatural character of the events was not established, prohibited the public cult at Marpingen, and ordered the faithful to refrain from the pilgrimages. The site was closed to devotion.
- **No Approval, No Reversal**: No approbation has ever been issued for Marpingen, and the negative judgment has never been reversed, despite periodic revivals of devotion in the twentieth and twenty-first centuries (including a chapel built on the site in later years, which serves as a place of ordinary worship rather than an approved apparition shrine).
- **A Case of Restraint**: Marpingen is often studied as an example of the Church's prudence in the face of a mass devotional movement: the civil authorities' persecution was condemned, but the Church nonetheless declined to authenticate the phenomena.

## Current Status

- **Formally Rejected**: The local Ordinary determined that the events were not established as supernatural and prohibited public devotion — a formal rejection of the alleged apparitions.
- **Obedience Required**: The faithful are bound to refrain from promoting the rejected devotion; the case stands as a classic example of the distinction between legitimate popular piety and unauthenticated claims.
