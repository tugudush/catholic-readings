# Sacred Relics and Their Veneration

The Catholic Church venerates the **relics of the saints** — the bodily remains of the holy ones of God and the objects associated with their lives and deaths — as sacred signs of the communion of saints and as means of grace for the faithful. Relics are among the oldest and most cherished of the Church's sacramentals, attested from the earliest centuries of the Christian era and governed today by a precise body of theology, liturgy, and canon law.

This document explains what relics are, the theological foundation of their veneration, the three classes of relics, the discipline of their authentication and preservation, the law of the Church concerning their use, and the right manner of venerating them.

---

## 1. The Theology of Relic Veneration

### 1.1 Veneration, Not Adoration

The Church's veneration of relics is rooted in two truths: the saints are **temples of the Holy Spirit** and instruments of divine holiness, and their bodies, which were sanctified by grace and united to Christ, are **destined for the resurrection** ([CCC 988–1001](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/988)).

The veneration of relics is therefore **not adoration**. The Church distinguishes rigorously between:

- **_Latria_** (adoration, divine worship), which is due to God alone — Father, Son, and Holy Spirit;
- **_Dulia_** (veneration, honor), which is given to the saints and angels and, derivatively, to their images and relics;
- **_Hyperdulia_** (eminent veneration), which is given to the Blessed Virgin Mary above all other creatures.

Honor shown to a relic is referred to the **saint** whom it represents and, ultimately, to **God**, the source of the saint's holiness. Saint Thomas Aquinas states this clearly: "we do not give the honor of _latria_ to the saints, but only that of _dulia_… We honor the relics of the saints because of the saints themselves" ([Saint Thomas Aquinas, _Summa Theologiae_, III, q. 25, a. 6](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/f2c979a8-871d-4f46-a059-320d3b837a76/ref/)).

### 1.2 The Biblical and Patristic Foundation

The veneration of relics is ancient and grounded in Scripture:

- The bones of the prophet **Elisha** raised a dead man to life ([2 Kings 13:20–21](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/00a1de35-98f1-481a-956f-e98e2b52331d/ref/));
- The very shadow of **Saint Peter** healed the sick ([Acts 5:15–16](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/00a1de35-98f1-481a-956f-e98e2b52331d/ref/));
- **Handkerchiefs and aprons** that had touched the body of **Saint Paul** carried healing power ([Acts 19:11–12](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/00a1de35-98f1-481a-956f-e98e2b52331d/ref/)).

From the second century, the Church preserved and honored the relics of the martyrs, celebrating the Eucharist over their tombs and venerating their remains with the greatest reverence. The Fathers — Saint Jerome, Saint Augustine, Saint John Chrysostom, and Saint Ambrose — defended this practice against its pagan and heretical detractors and taught that the martyrs' relics are honored because the saints live in Christ and intercede for the Church ([CCC 956](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/956)).

### 1.3 The Council of Trent on Relics

The **Council of Trent**, in its twenty-fifth session (3–4 December 1563), defined the doctrine of the veneration of relics against the Protestant reformers:

> "The holy Synod enjoins on all bishops, and others who sustain the office and care of teaching, that, agreeably to the usage of the Catholic and Apostolic Church, received from the primitive times of the Christian religion, and with the consent of the holy Fathers, and to the decrees of the sacred Councils, they especially instruct the faithful diligently concerning the intercession and invocation of saints; the honor (honorem) paid to relics; and the legitimate use of images." (Council of Trent, Session XXV, Decree on the Invocation, Veneration, and Relics of Saints, and on Sacred Images)

The same decree anathematized those who deny that the relics of the saints are worthy of veneration and honor, while it carefully excluded every superstition and abuse.

## 2. The Three Classes of Relics

The Church distinguishes relics by their origin and degree of closeness to the saint:

| Class            | Definition                                                                                                                                | Examples                                                                                      |
| ---------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **First class**  | A portion of the **body** of the saint — a bone or fragment of bone, blood, hair, or, in the case of cremation, the totality of the ashes | A relic of a bone of Saint Peter; the blood of Saint Januarius; the body of a martyr          |
| **Second class** | An **object that belonged to or was used by** the saint during life                                                                       | Clothing, a book or manuscript, a walking stick, liturgical vestments, instruments of penance |
| **Third class**  | A piece of cloth (or similar material) **touched to a first- or second-class relic**                                                      | A cloth that has been applied to the tomb or relic of a saint                                 |

The 2017 Instruction of the Dicastery for the Causes of Saints, _Relics in the Church: Authenticity and Preservation_, refines this classification in its technical discipline by distinguishing **"significant" relics** — the entire body, notable parts of the body, and the sum total of cremated ashes — which require particular care, preservation, and vigilance by the diocesan bishop or the competent authority, and are to be kept in properly sealed urns or containers — from other relics of lesser significance (Dicastery for the Causes of Saints, Instruction _Relics in the Church: Authenticity and Preservation_, 2017, "Significant Relics").

---

## 3. The 2017 Instruction: Authenticity and Preservation

In **2017**, the **Dicastery (then Congregation) for the Causes of Saints** issued the Instruction **_Relics in the Church: Authenticity and Preservation_** to regulate the discipline of relics in the contemporary Church. Its principal norms include:

- **Authentication**: every relic exposed for the veneration of the faithful must be **authenticated** by competent ecclesiastical authority; doubtful or unauthenticated relics may not be exposed;
- **Sealing and certification**: significant relics are to be preserved in sealed urns or containers, accompanied by a certificate of authentication and the wax seal of the competent authority;
- **Prohibition of sale**: the sale of sacred relics is absolutely forbidden;
- **Prohibition of superstition and fraud**: relics may not be treated as talismans or objects of superstition, and fraudulent or doubtful relics are forbidden;
- **Preservation and vigilance**: the diocesan bishop, the competent Eastern Catholic authority, and the Dicastery itself exercise vigilance over the preservation and public veneration of relics;
- **Transfer and alienation**: the permanent transfer of highly venerated relics requires the permission of the Apostolic See.

The Instruction also urges that the faithful be catechized on the true meaning of relics — that the veneration of relics is a sign of the communion of saints, a profession of faith in the resurrection of the body, and a summons to imitate the holiness of the saints, and that it is directed to God through the saints (Dicastery for the Causes of Saints, Instruction _Relics in the Church: Authenticity and Preservation_, 2017, Introduction).

## 4. The Canon Law of Relics

The **Code of Canon Law** (1983) governs the discipline of relics with precision:

> **Canon 1190**
> §1. It is absolutely forbidden to sell sacred relics.
> §2. Relics of great significance and other relics honored with great veneration by the people cannot be alienated validly or transferred permanently in any way without the permission of the Apostolic See.
> §3. The prescript of §2 also applies to relics which, because of the veneration of the people, are considered significant. (Code of Canon Law, canon 1190)

Other relevant canons include:

- **Canon 1237 §2**: relics of martyrs or other saints are to be placed in a fixed altar that is to be dedicated, in accordance with the norms of the liturgical books — the ancient and enduring practice of sealing relics in the altars where the Eucharist is celebrated;
- **Canon 1287** (1917 Code, abrogated): the older discipline similarly forbade the sale of relics and required the authentication of those exposed for veneration — the principle that has been carried into the present law;
- The **canonical norms on the reservation and public veneration** of relics, which assign competence over these matters to the diocesan bishop and the Holy See.

The Church's law thus protects relics from **commerce** (they may never be sold), from **negligence** (they must be authenticated, sealed, and preserved), and from **superstition** (they may never be used as charms or objects of magical expectation).

## 5. The Veneration of Relics in the Liturgy and Popular Piety

### 5.1 In the Sacred Liturgy

- **Relics in the altar**: since the earliest centuries, the Church has celebrated the Eucharist over the tombs of the martyrs, and to this day relics of saints are placed in the altars at their dedication ([Canon 1237 §2](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/00a1de35-98f1-481a-956f-e98e2b52331d/ref/));
- **Feasts of the saints**: on their liturgical feasts, the relics of the saints are honored, carried in procession, and proposed for the veneration of the faithful;
- **Veneration of the Cross on Good Friday**: the Church venerates the relic of the True Cross on Good Friday, and the faithful reverence it with a kiss;
- **Public exposition**: the relics of a saint may be exposed for public veneration on their feast or on other appropriate occasions, always in a dignified manner and with the permission of the competent authority.

### 5.2 In Popular Piety

The **Directory on Popular Piety and the Liturgy** (2001) treats the veneration of relics among the expressions of popular piety, teaching that it must be ordered to the liturgy and to the imitation of the saints:

> "The veneration of relics, like the cult of the saints, is not an end in itself but a means of leading the faithful to God, of strengthening their faith in the resurrection, and of encouraging them to imitate the virtues of the saints." (Directory on Popular Piety and the Liturgy, 236–237)

The Directory urges that:

- The faithful be taught the **true meaning** of relics — signs of the communion of saints and of the resurrection of the body;
- Relics be venerated with **reverence and dignity**, not treated as curiosities or talismans;
- The veneration of relics **flow from and lead back to** the liturgy, especially the Eucharist;
- The authenticity of relics be **assured** and doubtful relics not be proposed for veneration.

### 5.3 Relics of the True Cross and of the Passion

Among the most venerated relics of the Church are those of the **True Cross** and the instruments of the Passion — the Crown of Thorns, the Holy Nails, the Holy Lance (see [church-history/holy-lance-relic.md](../church-history/holy-lance-relic.md)), and the Shroud of Turin. The Church honors these not as bodily relics of a saint but as **relics of Christ** — material objects connected with the Redeemer himself, which the faithful venerate with a devotion of supreme reverence (though still not with the adoration due to God alone). The feast of the **Exaltation of the Holy Cross** (14 September) and of the **Finding of the Holy Cross** (3 May, in the older calendar) commemorate the Church's veneration of the True Cross, and the relics of the Passion have been preserved and honored from the fourth century.

## 6. The Right and Wrong Veneration of Relics

### 6.1 Right Veneration

The faithful venerate relics rightly when they:

- Honor the relic **for the sake of the saint** and of God, the source of holiness;
- Kiss or touch the relic (or its case) **reverently**, with faith and prayer;
- Use relics as a **means of prayer and devotion**, asking the saint's intercession;
- Understand that any **healing or grace** connected with relics comes from **God**, through the prayer of the Church and the intercession of the saint — not from the relic as a thing;
- Preserve relics with **reverence**, keeping them clean, dignified, and properly authenticated.

### 6.2 Wrong Veneration

The Church forbids:

- **Superstition**: treating relics as charms, amulets, or lucky objects with automatic power ([CCC 2110–2111](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/2110));
- **Commerce**: the sale or trading of sacred relics (canon 1190 §1);
- **Fraud**: the fabrication, misattribution, or doubtful authentication of relics;
- **Irreverence**: the desecration, destruction, or profane handling of relics;
- **Excess**: treating relics as more important than the sacraments, or allowing private devotions to obscure the primacy of the Eucharist and the liturgy.

## 7. The Eastern Catholic Tradition

In the Eastern Catholic Churches, the veneration of relics (Greek, _leipsana_; Slavic, _moshchi_) is equally ancient and profound:

- The **relics of the saints** are placed in the **altar table** (the _antimension_ in the Byzantine tradition contains a relic of a martyr, sewn into the cloth upon which the Divine Liturgy is celebrated);
- The faithful **venerate icons and relics** with a kiss, and the relics are carried in procession on the feasts of the saints;
- The Byzantine tradition emphasizes that the saints, though departed, are **alive in Christ**, and that their relics are temples of the Holy Spirit and pledges of the resurrection — the same theology taught in the Latin Church ([Catechism of the Ukrainian Catholic Church, _Christ – Our Pascha_, 618](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/8034cb0d-dae1-4457-8790-75dfe8b78e74/ref/618)).

The veneration of relics is thus one of the many points on which the Latin and Eastern Catholic traditions are in perfect doctrinal harmony, differing only in ceremonial expression.

## 8. Levels of Authority in This Document

Following the conventions of [AGENTS.md](../AGENTS.md), this document distinguishes:

1. **Dogma** — the legitimacy and rightness of venerating the relics of the saints, defined by the Council of Trent (Session XXV) and grounded in Scripture and Tradition; the truth of the communion of saints and the resurrection of the body.
2. **Ecclesiastical discipline** — the classification of relics, the norms of authentication and preservation, the prohibition of sale, and the rules of public veneration, which are governed by the Code of Canon Law and the 2017 Instruction and may be adapted by competent authority.
3. **Pious practice** — particular devotions connected with individual relics (e.g., the liquefaction of the blood of Saint Januarius), which are permitted and encouraged but are not defined doctrine.
4. **Private revelation** — not invoked as a source for this doctrine.
