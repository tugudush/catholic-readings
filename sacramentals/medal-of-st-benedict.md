# The Medal of Saint Benedict

The **Medal of Saint Benedict** (sometimes called the **St. Benedict Medal** or the **Benedictine Medal**) is one of the oldest and most powerful sacramentals of the Catholic Church. It bears the image of Saint Benedict of Nursia, Father of Western monasticism and Patriarch of Western monks, together with a cross and a series of Latin inscriptions that express the Church's faith in the power of the Cross of Christ and her prayer for protection against the snares of the Evil One.

This document explains the medal's nature as a sacramental, its history, the meaning of every symbol and inscription, the prayers connected with it, the indulgences attached to it, and the right manner of using it in the spiritual life.

---

## 1. The Medal as a Sacramental

The Medal of Saint Benedict is a **sacramental**, not a sacrament. Like all sacramentals, it is a sacred sign instituted by the Church that disposes the faithful to receive grace and sanctifies the circumstances of life ([CCC 1667](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/1667)). It does not confer grace by its own power, does not "work" mechanically, and does not substitute for the sacraments. Its power is the power of the prayer of the Church, united to the merits of Christ and the intercession of the saints, and it is effective in proportion to the faith and devotion of the one who uses it.

> "Sacred signs which bear a resemblance to the sacraments. They signify effects, particularly of a spiritual nature, which are obtained through the intercession of the Church. By them men are disposed to receive the chief effect of the sacraments, and various occasions in life are rendered holy." ([CCC 1667](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/1667))

The medal is thus a **means of grace through the Church's prayer**, an external sign of confidence in the protection of Christ and of Saint Benedict, and a continual reminder of the Christian's share in Christ's victory over sin and death.

## 2. History and Origin of the Medal

### 2.1 The Ancient Foundation: Saint Benedict and the Cross

The devotion behind the medal is ancient, rooted in the life of Saint Benedict of Nursia (c. 480–547), the founder of Monte Cassino and author of the Rule of Saint Benedict. His biography, the _Dialogues_ of Saint Gregory the Great, records how the power of the Cross was displayed in Benedict's life: he shattered a poisoned cup by the Sign of the Cross, broke the enchantment of a deadly poisoned loaf when a raven carried it away at his word, and cast out demons and healed the sick in the name of Christ ([Saint Gregory the Great, _Dialogues_, Book II](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/f2c979a8-871d-4f46-a059-320d3b837a76/ref/)).

These very scenes — the cup and the raven — are depicted on the medal, and they explain why the Church has long associated the Benedictine devotion with protection against poison, evil, and the assaults of the devil.

### 2.2 The Rediscovery at Natternberg (1647)

The specific form of the medal developed gradually. According to the classic account of the Catholic Encyclopedia, during a trial for witchcraft at Natternberg, near the Abbey of Metten in Bavaria, in the year **1647**, the accused women testified that they had no power over the abbey, which was "under the protection of the cross." On investigation, a number of painted crosses surrounded by the mysterious letters now found on Benedictine medals were discovered on the walls of the abbey, but their meaning had been forgotten. At length, in an old manuscript written in **1415**, there was found a picture of Saint Benedict holding in one hand a staff ending in a cross and a scroll in the other, on which the words corresponding to the mysterious letters were written in full. Medals bearing the image of Saint Benedict, a cross, and these letters then began to be struck in Germany and soon spread over Europe ([Catholic Encyclopedia, "Medal of Saint Benedict"](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/f2c979a8-871d-4f46-a059-320d3b837a76/ref/Medal%20of%20Saint%20Benedict)).

### 2.3 Papal Approval: Pope Benedict XIV (1741–1742)

The medal was first approved by **Pope Benedict XIV** in his briefs of **23 December 1741** and **12 March 1742**, who granted the faculty to bless it and attached indulgences to its devout use ([Catholic Encyclopedia, "Medal of Saint Benedict"](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/f2c979a8-871d-4f46-a059-320d3b837a76/ref/Medal%20of%20Saint%20Benedict)). It has since been repeatedly enriched and recommended by the Holy See.

### 2.4 The Jubilee Medal of 1880

The medal in its most familiar form is the so-called **jubilee medal**, struck for the first time in **1880** to commemorate the **fourteenth centenary of Saint Benedict's birth**. The **Archabbey of Monte Cassino** possesses the exclusive right to strike this medal. On 31 December 1877, Pope Pius IX had granted to the habitual wearer of the jubilee medal the indulgences attached to a visit to the basilica, crypt, and tower of Saint Benedict at Monte Cassino; and by a decree of 27 February 1907 the Holy See granted a plenary indulgence for the souls in purgatory on the feast of All Souls to the faithful who, hindered from visiting a Benedictine church, devoutly visit any church or public oratory ([Catholic Encyclopedia, "Medal of Saint Benedict"](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/f2c979a8-871d-4f46-a059-320d3b837a76/ref/Medal%20of%20Saint%20Benedict)).

## 3. The Design of the Medal and the Meaning of Its Symbols

### 3.1 The Front (Obverse)

The front of the medal bears an image of **Saint Benedict** holding a **cross** in his right hand and the **Holy Rule** in his left hand. Around the image are a **cup** on one side (recalling the poisoned cup that shattered at his Sign of the Cross) and a **raven** on the other (recalling the raven that bore away the poisoned loaf). Above the cup and the raven is inscribed:

> **Crux Sancti Patris Benedicti**
> ("The Cross of the Holy Father Benedict")

Around the margin of the medal runs the legend:

> **Eius in obitu nostro praesentia muniamur**
> ("May we at our death be fortified by his presence")

### 3.2 The Reverse

The reverse of the medal bears a **cross**. Around it are placed the initial letters of a series of Latin prayers of protection:

- On the **vertical bar** of the cross, downward:

> **C S S M L** — _Crux Sacra Sit Mihi Lux_
> ("May the Holy Cross be my light")

- On the **horizontal bar** of the cross:

> **N D S M D** — _Non Draco Sit Mihi Dux_
> ("Let not the dragon be my guide")

- In the **four angles** of the cross:

> **C S P B** — _Crux Sancti Patris Benedicti_
> ("The Cross of the Holy Father Benedict")

- Around the **margin**, the initials of the distich:

> **V R S N S M V — S M Q L I V B**
> _Vade Retro Satana, Nunquam Suade Mihi Vana — Sunt Mala Quae Libas, Ipse Venena Bibas_
> ("Begone, Satan, never suggest to me thy vanities — evil are the things thou profferest, drink thou thy own poison")

- At the **top of the cross** usually stands the word **PAX** ("Peace") or the monogram **IHS** (the Holy Name of Jesus) ([Catholic Encyclopedia, "Medal of Saint Benedict"](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/f2c979a8-871d-4f46-a059-320d3b837a76/ref/Medal%20of%20Saint%20Benedict)).

### 3.3 The Meaning of the Inscriptions

The inscriptions are a single continuous prayer in miniature:

- **"May the Holy Cross be my light"** — the Christian's whole life is lived in the light of the Cross of Christ, the sign of our redemption;
- **"Let not the dragon be my guide"** — the prayer that the devil, the ancient serpent ([Revelation 12:9](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/00a1de35-98f1-481a-956f-e98e2b52331d/ref/)), never lead the soul astray;
- **"Begone, Satan, never suggest to me thy vanities"** — a firm rejection of the temptations of the Evil One;
- **"Evil are the things thou profferest, drink thou thy own poison"** — a declaration that the devil's offers are evil and that his malice recoils upon himself;
- **"The Cross of the Holy Father Benedict"** — the medal is placed under the protection of the great Patriarch of Western monks, who by the Sign of the Cross conquered poison, enchantment, and the powers of darkness;
- **"May we at our death be fortified by his presence"** — the prayer that, at the hour of death, the intercession of Saint Benedict and the protection of the Cross may assist the faithful soul, in the tradition of the dying Christian's confidence in the Cross and the prayers of the saints ([CCC 1020–1022](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/1020));
- **PAX** — peace, the fruit of the Cross and the greeting of the Risen Christ ([John 20:19–21](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/00a1de35-98f1-481a-956f-e98e2b52331d/ref/)); **IHS** — the Holy Name of Jesus, "the name which is above every name" ([Philippians 2:9](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/00a1de35-98f1-481a-956f-e98e2b52331d/ref/)).

## 4. Prayers Connected with the Medal

### 4.1 The Prayer of the Inscriptions

The inscriptions of the medal are themselves prayed as a brief formula of spiritual warfare:

    May the holy Cross be my light!
    Let not the dragon be my guide!
    Begone, Satan!
    Never suggest to me thy vanities!
    Evil are the things thou profferest!
    Drink thou thy own poison!

### 4.2 The Prayer of the Church to Saint Benedict

The Church prays to Saint Benedict in the Collect of his feast (11 July), asking that through his intercession we may be delivered from our sins and defended from the assaults of the enemy:

    V. The Cross of our holy Father Benedict.
    R. May the Cross of our holy Father Benedict be our salvation.

    V. May the holy Cross be my light!
    R. May the dragon never be my guide!

    V. Begone, Satan!
    R. Never tempt me with vanities!

    V. What thou offerest me is evil!
    R. Drink thou thy own poison!

    Let us pray.
    O God, who through the merits of the blessed Abbot Benedict
    hast been pleased to enrich thy faithful people
    with the gift of a special protection,
    grant, we beseech thee,
    that those who devoutly honor his memory
    may be delivered by his intercession
    from every evil of soul and body.
    Through Christ our Lord. Amen.

### 4.3 The Short Daily Invocation

A shorter daily invocation, suitable for use with the medal, is:

    By the Cross of Christ,
    and through the intercession of our holy Father Benedict,
    defend me, O Lord, from all evil
    and from the snares of the enemy. Amen.

> 📝 **A necessary clarification**: The longer prayer associated with the medal (sometimes called the "Saint Benedict exorcism") is a **private prayer of the faithful** and a prayer of the Church for protection. It is not a **solemn exorcism**. The solemn ("major") exorcism of a person is a liturgical act that **may be performed only by a priest with the express permission of the local bishop** and in accordance with the strict norms of the Church ([CCC 1673](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/1673)). The laity never perform solemn exorcisms; their use of the medal and its prayers is an act of personal devotion and spiritual protection.

## 5. Indulgences Attached to the Medal

The Medal of Saint Benedict has been enriched with **indulgences** by the Holy See:

- **Pope Benedict XIV** (1741–1742) first approved the medal and attached indulgences to its devout use;
- **Pope Pius IX** (31 December 1877) granted to the habitual wearer of the jubilee medal the indulgences that could be gained by visiting the basilica, crypt, and tower of Saint Benedict at Monte Cassino;
- By a decree of the Sacred Congregation of Indulgences (**27 February 1907**), a **plenary indulgence** for the souls in purgatory was granted on the feast of **All Souls** (from about two o'clock in the afternoon of 1 November to sunset of 2 November), as often as one, after confession and Holy Communion, visits any church or public oratory and prays there according to the intention of the pope, provided that the faithful soul is hindered by sickness, enclosure, or distance of at least a thousand steps from visiting a church or oratory of the Benedictines ([Catholic Encyclopedia, "Medal of Saint Benedict"](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/f2c979a8-871d-4f46-a059-320d3b837a76/ref/Medal%20of%20Saint%20Benedict)).

An indulgence is the remission before God of the temporal punishment due to sin already forgiven, granted by the Church from the treasury of the merits of Christ and the saints under defined conditions ([CCC 1471–1479](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/1471)). The indulgences of the medal presuppose the ordinary conditions for gaining any indulgence: the state of grace, the proper intention, and, where a plenary indulgence is concerned, sacramental confession, Holy Communion, and prayer for the pope's intentions.

## 6. How the Medal Is Obtained, Blessed, and Worn

### 6.1 Obtaining and Blessing the Medal

The Medal of Saint Benedict should be **blessed by a priest with the faculty** before being worn. Any priest may receive the faculties to bless these medals ([Catholic Encyclopedia, "Medal of Saint Benedict"](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/f2c979a8-871d-4f46-a059-320d3b837a76/ref/Medal%20of%20Saint%20Benedict)). The medal may be worn on the person, kept in the home, placed in a car or workplace, attached to a rosary, or set into the foundation of a new building; the Church does not restrict the manner of its use so long as it is treated with reverence as a sacramental.

### 6.2 Wearing the Medal

The medal is worn or kept as an external sign of:

- **Faith in the Cross of Christ**, our only salvation;
- **Confidence in the intercession of Saint Benedict**, patron of Europe, of monks, and of students ([saints/st-benedict.md](../saints/st-benedict.md));
- **A prayer for protection** against the snares of the devil and the dangers of body and soul;
- **A commitment to the Christian life** — prayer, the sacraments, and the works of mercy.

> 📝 **Pastoral note**: Like every sacramental, the medal is a means of grace, not a charm. It does not guarantee protection apart from faith, repentance, and a Christian life, and it never substitutes for the sacraments. The most powerful "protection" of the Christian remains the grace of Christ received in the sacraments, especially the Most Holy Eucharist.

## 7. The Medal in the Spiritual Life

The Medal of Saint Benedict is especially associated with the Church's prayer of **spiritual warfare**. Its inscriptions are a summary of the Christian's resistance to the devil: the prayer for the light of the Cross, the rejection of the "dragon" as a guide, and the command to Satan to depart. The Church teaches that the devil is a real, personal being who seeks the ruin of souls ([CCC 391–395](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/391)), but that his power is strictly limited by the providence of God and is definitively broken by the victory of Christ ([CCC 2852](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/2852)). The faithful are called to "resist the devil," who "prowls around like a roaring lion" ([1 Peter 5:8–9](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/00a1de35-98f1-481a-956f-e98e2b52331d/ref/)), and the medal is a venerable means of doing so in the manner of the saints.

The Benedictine tradition, faithful to the Rule of Saint Benedict with its warnings against the "wicked spirit" and its call to humble prayer, has always combined the use of such signs with the primary weapons of the spiritual life: prayer, fasting, the sacraments, obedience to the Church, and the works of mercy.

## 8. Levels of Authority in This Document

Following the conventions of [AGENTS.md](../AGENTS.md), this document distinguishes:

1. **Dogma** — the power of the Cross of Christ, the reality and defeat of the devil, the legitimacy of sacramentals, and the intercession of the saints.
2. **Magisterial teaching and approved practice** — the Church's approval of the Medal of Saint Benedict (Benedict XIV), its enrichment with indulgences, and its recommendation in Catholic devotional life.
3. **Pious tradition** — the historical details of the discovery at Natternberg and the 1415 manuscript, which are credible but not articles of faith.
4. **Private revelation** — not invoked as a source for this devotion, which rests on the public Revelation of the Cross and the Church's pastoral authority.
