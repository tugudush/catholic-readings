# The Other Approved Scapulars

Beyond the Brown Scapular of Our Lady of Mount Carmel — the oldest and most widespread of the Church-approved scapulars (see [sacramentals/brown-scapular.md](brown-scapular.md)) — the Catholic Church has approved a number of other scapular devotions, each with its own color, meaning, founding religious order, and history. These scapulars are all **sacramentals**: reduced forms of religious habits (or, in some cases, devotional badges), received through a rite of the Church, and worn as external signs of consecration, devotion, and prayer.

This document surveys the principal approved scapulars, their origins and approvals, and the general principles that govern all scapular devotion.

---

## 1. What a Scapular Is

The word **scapular** (from Latin _scapulae_, "shoulders") designates the rectangular piece of cloth worn over the shoulders, hanging down the front and back, which forms part of the habit of the religious orders. When the Church admits the faithful to share in the spiritual goods of an order by receiving a **small form of its habit**, that reduced form is called a scapular of devotion ([Catholic Encyclopedia, "Scapular"](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/f2c979a8-871d-4f46-a059-320d3b837a76/ref/Scapular)).

The **Directory on Popular Piety and the Liturgy** (2001) describes the scapular devotion in general terms:

> "The Scapular is a reduced form of the religious habit… imposed by a special rite of the Church… an external sign of the filial relationship with Mary [or, in general, with the order's patron] and of the primacy of spiritual life." (Directory on Popular Piety and the Liturgy, 205)

All scapulars share the same structure: they are **sacramentals**, they are received by a rite of the Church (usually administered by a priest), they signify a **consecration or a commitment** to a particular devotion, and they are worn as a **discreet sign** of that commitment in daily life.

## 3. The Blue Scapular of the Immaculate Conception

- **Devotion**: The Immaculate Conception of the Blessed Virgin Mary.
- **Origin**: Traditionally attributed to **Venerable Ursula Benincasa** (1547–1618), the foundress of the Theatine Sisters, in seventeenth-century Italy.
- **Appearance**: Blue woolen cloth, often bearing an image of the Immaculate Conception.
- **Approval**: The devotion received ecclesiastical approval under **Pope Clement XI**, traditionally dated to 1710, and was subsequently promoted and enriched with indulgences under later popes, including **Pope Pius IX**.
- **Associated order**: The **Theatines** (Clerics Regular of Divine Providence), with whose spiritual family the devotion originated; the Archconfraternity of the Immaculate Conception became associated with the scapular ([Catholic Encyclopedia, "Scapular"](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/f2c979a8-871d-4f46-a059-320d3b837a76/ref/Scapular)).

The Blue Scapular is a sign of consecration to the Immaculate Virgin and of confidence in her intercession, and it is frequently worn together with the Brown Scapular in the practice of the "scapular of the faithful" devotions.

---

## 4. The Red Scapular of the Passion

- **Devotion**: The Passion of our Lord Jesus Christ and the devotion to the Sacred Hearts of Jesus and Mary.
- **Origin**: The devotion is traced to revelations received in **1846** by **Sister Apollonia Andriveau** (sometimes given as Apolline Andriveau), a religious of the Congregation of the Sisters of Charity of St. Vincent de Paul, to whom our Lord is said to have revealed the form of the scapular and the promises attached to it.
- **Appearance**: Red woolen cloth, bearing on one side an image of Christ crucified with his instruments of the Passion, and on the other the Sacred Hearts of Jesus and Mary.
- **Approval**: The scapular was approved by **Pope Pius IX** in **1847** ([Catholic Encyclopedia, "Scapular"](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/f2c979a8-871d-4f46-a059-320d3b837a76/ref/Scapular)).

The Red Scapular is a sign of devotion to the Passion of Christ and of reparation for sin; the Church has attached to it indulgences for those who wear it and devoutly honor the Passion and the Sacred Hearts.

---

## 5. The Black Scapular of the Seven Dolors

- **Devotion**: The Seven Sorrows (Dolors) of the Blessed Virgin Mary — her sorrows at the prophecy of Simeon, the flight into Egypt, the loss of the Child Jesus, the meeting on the Way of the Cross, the Crucifixion, the taking down from the Cross, and the burial of her Son.
- **Origin**: The devotion to the Sorrows of Mary is closely associated with the **Servite Order** (Order of the Servants of Mary), founded at Florence in 1233, whose members honor the sorrowful Mother with special devotion and who have promoted the Black Scapular as a sign of that devotion.
- **Appearance**: Black woolen cloth, usually bearing an image of the Sorrowful Mother or of the Seven Sorrows.
- **Approval**: The Black Scapular has long been approved and enriched with indulgences in connection with the Servite Order, which celebrates the feast of the Seven Sorrows of the Blessed Virgin (15 September) and the solemn commemoration of the Sorrows on the Friday of Passion Week.

The Black Scapular is a sign of devotion to the sorrowful Mother and of participation in her compassion for her Son — a devotion solemnly recommended by the Church and practiced by many of the faithful, especially in times of trial.

---

## 6. The White Scapular of the Most Holy Trinity

- **Devotion**: The Most Holy Trinity.
- **Origin**: The devotion is connected with the **Trinitarian Order** (the Order of the Most Holy Trinity and of Captives), founded in the twelfth century by Saints John of Matha and Felix of Valois for the ransom of Christian captives.
- **Appearance**: White woolen cloth, usually bearing the Trinitarian emblem — a red and blue cross — or an image of the Most Holy Trinity.
- **Approval**: The White Scapular was approved in connection with the Trinitarian Order and the confraternities of the Most Holy Trinity.

The White Scapular is a sign of devotion to the Blessed Trinity and of the Christian's consecration to the three divine Persons, in whom "we live and move and have our being" ([Acts 17:28](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/00a1de35-98f1-481a-956f-e98e2b52331d/ref/)).

---

## 7. The Green Scapular of the Immaculate Heart of Mary

- **Devotion**: The Immaculate Heart of Mary.
- **Origin**: The Green Scapular (more precisely a **single devotional badge** rather than the two-piece cloth form) is traced to revelations received in **1840** by **Sister Justine Bisqueyburu** (1817–1903), a Daughter of Charity of St. Vincent de Paul, to whom the Blessed Virgin is said to have revealed the scapular and its promises of conversion and healing.
- **Appearance**: A single piece of green cloth, bearing on one side an image of the Immaculate Heart of Mary, and on the other the image of the Heart of Mary pierced by a sword.
- **Approval**: The devotion was approved by **Pope Pius IX** in **1863**, and indulgences were subsequently added by **Pope Leo XIII**.

The Green Scapular is especially associated with prayer for the **conversion of sinners** and for the **healing of the sick**. The faithful are encouraged to place it on the sick and to pray for them the prayer associated with the devotion: "Immaculate Heart of Mary, pray for us now and at the hour of our death." The Green Scapular, like all sacramentals, does not work mechanically; it is a sign of prayer and of confidence in Mary's intercession for the conversion and healing of souls.

---

## 8. The Scapular of the Sacred Heart

- **Devotion**: The Sacred Heart of Jesus and the Immaculate Heart of Mary.
- **Origin**: The devotion was spread by the **Daughters of the Sacred Heart**, a religious congregation founded at Antwerp in **1873**.
- **Appearance**: White woolen cloth bearing the image of the Sacred Heart of Jesus on one side and the Immaculate Heart of Mary on the other (the exact form varies), often with the words "Sacred Heart of Jesus, have mercy on us" and "Immaculate Heart of Mary, pray for us."
- **Approval**: The scapular was approved by the **Congregation of Sacred Rites** on **4 April 1900** ([Catholic Encyclopedia, "Scapular"](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/f2c979a8-871d-4f46-a059-320d3b837a76/ref/Scapular)).

The Scapular of the Sacred Heart is a sign of consecration to the Sacred Heart of Jesus and to the Immaculate Heart of Mary, in the spirit of the devotion revealed to Saint Margaret Mary Alacoque and approved by the Church (see [liturgy/sacred-heart-devotion.md](../liturgy/sacred-heart-devotion.md)).

## 9. General Principles of All Scapular Devotion

The Directory on Popular Piety and the Liturgy and the long tradition of the Church establish certain common principles that govern every scapular:

- **A sacramental, not a charm**: every scapular is a sacramental; it does not confer grace automatically and must never be treated as a talisman or lucky object;
- **A sign of commitment**: the scapular signifies a commitment — to a particular devotion, to prayer, to chastity according to one's state, and to the Christian life;
- **Received by a rite**: scapulars are received through a rite of the Church, normally administered by a priest, which should not be improvised and should be preceded by suitable catechesis (Directory on Popular Piety and the Liturgy, 205);
- **Worn as a sign**: the scapular is worn, ordinarily under the clothing, as a discreet and constant sign of the wearer's devotion;
- **Ordered to the liturgy**: scapular devotion flows from and leads back to the liturgy, especially the Eucharist and the sacraments, and never replaces them ([CCC 1675](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/1675));
- **Distinguishing approval from private revelation**: a scapular may be "approved" by the Church — meaning that the devotion is lawful, praiseworthy, and enriched with indulgences — without thereby authenticating every apparition, message, or promise connected with its origin. The faithful are free to receive approved scapulars; they are not required to believe the private revelations attached to their origins.

## 10. Enrollment, Blessing, and Wearing

Scapulars of devotion are received by a **rite of enrollment** or **investment**, normally administered by a priest (and, in some cases, by a deacon or, for certain scapulars, by a lay person delegated for the purpose). The rite includes:

- A brief instruction on the meaning and obligations of the scapular;
- The blessing of the scapular;
- The imposition of the scapular upon the recipient, with an appropriate formula of prayer;
- A prayer for the recipient, commending him or her to the devotion signified by the scapular.

Where the discipline of a particular scapular so provides, the enrollment may be **renewed** or the scapular **replaced** without losing the benefits of the original enrollment. The scapular itself should be blessed by a priest when it is newly acquired, and worn with reverence. In many places the faithful are enrolled in the Brown Scapular of Mount Carmel on the feast of Our Lady of Mount Carmel (16 July) and in the other scapulars on the feasts proper to their devotions.

## 11. Levels of Authority in This Document

Following the conventions of [AGENTS.md](../AGENTS.md), this document distinguishes:

1. **Dogma** — the legitimacy of sacramentals, the truth of the devotions signified (the Immaculate Conception, the Passion of Christ, the Seven Sorrows, the Most Holy Trinity, the Sacred and Immaculate Hearts), and the reality of Mary's intercession.
2. **Ecclesiastical discipline** — the approval of each scapular, the norms of enrollment and wearing, and the indulgences attached, all of which are governed by the Holy See and may be adapted by competent authority.
3. **Private revelation** — the alleged apparitions and messages connected with the origins of several scapulars (e.g., to Sister Apollonia Andriveau, to Sister Justine Bisqueyburu), which are credible for the faithful but are not articles of faith and do not bind belief.
4. **Theological opinion** — particular interpretations of the promises attached to the scapulars, which remain open to pious discretion.
