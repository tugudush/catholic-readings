# Speaking in Tongues (γένη γλωσσῶν)

The eighth of the nine charismatic gifts enumerated by St. Paul in [1 Corinthians 12:10](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/00a1de35-98f1-481a-956f-e98e2b52331d/ref/): "To another **various kinds of tongues**." This charism — often called _glossolalia_ (from the Greek γλῶσσα, _glōssa_, "tongue, language") — is the Spirit-given ability to speak in languages unknown to the speaker, whether human languages (as at Pentecost) or a transcendent mode of prayer beyond ordinary linguistic structures.

---

## 1. Biblical Foundation

### 1.1 The Pauline Text

> "To another **various kinds of tongues**, to another the interpretation of tongues."
> — [1 Corinthians 12:10 (NRSV-CE)](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/00a1de35-98f1-481a-956f-e98e2b52331d/ref/)

The Greek γένη γλωσσῶν (_genē glōssōn_) — literally, "kinds of tongues" or "families of languages" — uses the plural for both words, suggesting a diversity of linguistic manifestations under the Spirit's impulse. There is not one "tongue" but various kinds.

### 1.2 Pentecost: The Archetypal Manifestation

The definitive biblical model of speaking in tongues is the descent of the Holy Spirit at Pentecost:

> "All of them were filled with the Holy Spirit and began to speak in other languages, as the Spirit gave them ability. Now there were devout Jews from every nation under heaven living in Jerusalem. And at this sound the crowd gathered and was bewildered, because each one heard them speaking in the native language of each."
> — [Acts 2:4–6 (NRSV-CE)](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/00a1de35-98f1-481a-956f-e98e2b52331d/ref/)

At Pentecost, the tongues were **xenolalia** — the speaking of actual, identifiable human languages unknown to the speakers but understood by hearers from different nations. The purpose was **evangelization**: the proclamation of "God's deeds of power" ([Acts 2:11](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/00a1de35-98f1-481a-956f-e98e2b52331d/ref/)) in languages the hearers could understand. This is the reversal of Babel — where human languages were divided in judgment, now they are united in the Spirit for proclamation.

### 1.3 Tongues in Corinth: A Different Emphasis

In Corinth, the phenomenon appears to have taken a different form. Based on St. Paul's instructions in 1 Corinthians 14, the tongues at Corinth were:

- **Not understood by the hearers** without interpretation ([1 Cor 14:2](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/00a1de35-98f1-481a-956f-e98e2b52331d/ref/)): "For those who speak in a tongue do not speak to other people but to God; for nobody understands them, since they are speaking mysteries in the Spirit."
- **Prayer and praise directed to God**, not proclamation to humans: "The one who speaks in a tongue builds up himself" ([1 Cor 14:4](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/00a1de35-98f1-481a-956f-e98e2b52331d/ref/)).
- **A genuine gift** but problematic when exercised in the assembly without interpretation.
- **Potentially angelic or transcendent language**: "If I speak in the tongues of mortals and of angels..." ([1 Cor 13:1](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/00a1de35-98f1-481a-956f-e98e2b52331d/ref/)).

### 1.4 Tongues in the Acts of the Apostles

Beyond Pentecost, the Acts of the Apostles records instances of speaking in tongues:

- **Cornelius's household** (Acts 10:44–46): The Holy Spirit fell on the Gentiles, and they spoke in tongues and extolled God — a sign confirming that Gentiles were to be received into the Church.
- **The Ephesian disciples** (Acts 19:6): When Paul laid hands on them, "the Holy Spirit came upon them, and they spoke in tongues and prophesied."

In both cases, tongues function as a **confirmatory sign** — a visible and audible manifestation that the Holy Spirit had been poured out.

## 2. Greek Terminology

| Term                         | Greek                         | Meaning                                                                                                                                                                                            |
| ---------------------------- | ----------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Kinds / Families**         | γένη (_genē_)                 | Genera, kinds, classes, families — suggesting diversity and variety in the manifestation                                                                                                           |
| **Tongues / Languages**      | γλωσσῶν (_glōssōn_)           | Tongues, languages — both the physical organ of speech and, by extension, the language spoken                                                                                                      |
| **Various Kinds of Tongues** | γένη γλωσσῶν (_genē glōssōn_) | A diversity of linguistic manifestations given by the Spirit — potentially including known human languages (xenolalia), unknown prayer languages (glossolalia), and transcendent or angelic speech |

The term γλῶσσα is also used in the Septuagint (Greek Old Testament) for languages and for the speech of foreign nations. In the New Testament, it takes on the specialized charismatic sense at Pentecost and in Corinth.

## 3. Patristic Commentary

### 3.1 St. John Chrysostom (c. 347–407)

In his [_Homily 29 on 1 Corinthians_](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/4a72892b-0a56-4371-a138-ff4aea2b0093/ref/), St. John Chrysostom observes that some in the Corinthian church had the ability to speak in tongues, while others had the related ability to interpret. He notes — with characteristic pastoral realism — that tongues were widely present in Corinth, but that their exercise had become a source of disorder and spiritual pride.

Chrysostom emphasizes that tongues are **inferior to prophecy** when uninterpreted, because they do not edify the hearers. He does not deny the authenticity of the gift but insists on its proper ordering: tongues are for private prayer and for edification of the assembly only when accompanied by interpretation.

### 3.2 St. Augustine of Hippo (354–430)

St. Augustine, in his [_Homilies on the First Epistle of John_](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/4a72892b-0a56-4371-a138-ff4aea2b0093/ref/), reflects on the phenomenon of tongues. He suggests that in the apostolic age, the gift of tongues was a sign for unbelievers — a visible manifestation that the Spirit had been given to all nations. In his own time, Augustine notes, the gift had become less common, but he interprets this as a **fulfillment** rather than a cessation: the Church herself, speaking all the languages of the world, is the enduring sign of Pentecost.

Augustine's interpretation is influential in Catholic thought: while the charismatic gift of tongues may be given to individuals as the Spirit wills, the **ecclesial reality** of Pentecost — the Church speaking the Gospel in every tongue — is the permanent and universal fulfillment of the gift.

### 3.3 Patristic Caution

The Fathers were generally cautious about tongues. Unlike prophecy, teaching, and works of mercy — which clearly edify the Church — uninterpreted tongues were of ambiguous value. The consistent patristic instinct was to **subordinate** tongues to the intelligible proclamation of the Gospel.

## 4. Thomistic Theology

### 4.1 St. Thomas Aquinas on 1 Corinthians 14

In his [_Commentary on 1 Corinthians_ 14.2](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/4a72892b-0a56-4371-a138-ff4aea2b0093/ref/), St. Thomas Aquinas devotes sustained attention to the gift of tongues. His analysis centers on the principle of **intelligibility**:

1. **Tongues without interpretation do not profit the hearers**: "The one who speaks in a tongue utters mysteries in the Spirit, but the mind is unfruitful" ([1 Cor 14:14](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/00a1de35-98f1-481a-956f-e98e2b52331d/ref/)). Because the speaker's own intellect does not grasp the meaning (or the hearers do not understand the language), the mind is not edified.
2. **Tongues with interpretation become comparable to prophecy**: When the tongue is interpreted, the message becomes intelligible, and the Church is built up. In this case, tongues-with-interpretation can be as valuable as prophecy.
3. **Tongues are a sign for unbelievers, not believers**: Citing [1 Corinthians 14:22](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/00a1de35-98f1-481a-956f-e98e2b52331d/ref/), Aquinas notes that tongues function as an external sign confirming the Spirit's presence — useful for drawing unbelievers to faith. But for believers already assembled, intelligible prophecy is more profitable.

### 4.2 The Thomistic Hierarchy of Charisms

Aquinas implicitly establishes a hierarchy among the communicative charisms, based on the principle of intelligibility and edification:

| Rank | Charism                        | Reason                                                                           |
| ---- | ------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1    | **Prophecy**                   | Intelligible divine communication that directly edifies the hearers              |
| 2    | **Tongues + Interpretation**   | Tongues become edifying when interpreted — equivalent to prophecy                |
| 3    | **Word of Wisdom / Knowledge** | Intelligible teaching that builds up the Church in understanding                 |
| 4    | **Uninterpreted Tongues**      | Genuine but of limited edification — builds up the speaker only, and imperfectly |

### 4.3 The Fruitful Mind

Aquinas's concern is that the **mind** (νοῦς, _nous_) be engaged in the charismatic act. "I will pray with the spirit, but I will pray with the mind also; I will sing praise with the spirit, but I will sing praise with the mind also" ([1 Cor 14:15](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/00a1de35-98f1-481a-956f-e98e2b52331d/ref/)). The charisms are not given to bypass the intellect but to elevate it. Tongues without mental engagement — without understanding — are of limited spiritual value.

## 5. Catholic Understanding Today

### 5.1 Magisterial Teaching

The Catholic Church does not have an extensive body of magisterial teaching specifically on the gift of tongues. The Catechism does not separately enumerate it. However, the general principles governing charisms apply:

- Tongues are a genuine charism when exercised authentically and in good order ([_Lumen Gentium_ 12](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/4a72892b-0a56-4371-a138-ff4aea2b0093/ref/)).
- Charisms, including tongues, are given for the common good and must be exercised in harmony with ecclesial authority ([_Iuvenescit Ecclesia_ 5–7](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/4a72892b-0a56-4371-a138-ff4aea2b0093/ref/)).
- Tongues without interpretation should not be exercised in the liturgical assembly, following St. Paul's explicit directive: "If anyone speaks in a tongue, let there be only two or at most three, and each in turn; and let one interpret. But if there is no one to interpret, let them be silent in church and speak to themselves and to God" ([1 Cor 14:27–28](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/00a1de35-98f1-481a-956f-e98e2b52331d/ref/)).

### 5.2 The Catholic Charismatic Renewal

The Catholic Charismatic Renewal, which began in 1967 (the "Duquesne Weekend") and has since spread globally, has brought the gift of tongues to renewed prominence in Catholic life. The Renewal has received cautious but genuine support from the Magisterium:

- **Pope St. Paul VI** (1975): Welcomed participants in the Catholic Charismatic Renewal and encouraged them to remain rooted in the Church's sacramental life and obedience.
- **Pope St. John Paul II** (1998): Spoke of the Charismatic Renewal as a "gift of the Spirit for the Church" and encouraged its integration into the life of parishes.
- **Pope Francis** (2014, 2017): Has repeatedly expressed appreciation for the Charismatic Renewal, calling it "a current of grace in the Church and for the Church." In a 2014 address, he described his own initial skepticism giving way to appreciation for the good fruits of the Renewal.

The Church has not made a dogmatic judgment on the specific nature of glossolalia (whether it is always xenolalia, a prayer language, or both). The pastoral approach is to test the fruits: does the exercise of tongues lead to deeper prayer, greater charity, and fidelity to the Church, or to disorder, spiritual pride, and division?

### 5.3 Manifestations Today

In Catholic charismatic experience, the gift of tongues typically manifests in two forms:

| Form                                     | Description                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          |
| ---------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Personal Prayer Language**             | A non-cognitive, Spirit-given mode of prayer used in private devotion — "speaking mysteries in the Spirit" ([1 Cor 14:2](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/00a1de35-98f1-481a-956f-e98e2b52331d/ref/)). The speaker does not understand the words rationally but experiences them as a mode of deep prayer, praise, and intercession. |
| **Public Utterance with Interpretation** | A tongue spoken aloud in the assembly, followed by an interpretation — functioning analogously to prophecy, as St. Paul describes in [1 Corinthians 14:5](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/00a1de35-98f1-481a-956f-e98e2b52331d/ref/) and 27–28.                                                                                     |

### 5.4 Discernment and Caution

The Church urges several cautions regarding tongues:

- **Not a necessary sign of baptism in the Spirit**: The Catholic Church does not teach that speaking in tongues is the necessary or normative evidence of the outpouring of the Holy Spirit. The fruits of the Spirit ([Galatians 5:22–23](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/00a1de35-98f1-481a-956f-e98e2b52331d/ref/)) and growth in charity are the true signs.
- **Not to be sought for its own sake**: St. Paul explicitly prioritizes intelligible gifts: "I would rather speak five words with my mind, in order to instruct others also, than ten thousand words in a tongue" ([1 Cor 14:19](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/00a1de35-98f1-481a-956f-e98e2b52331d/ref/)).
- **Order in the assembly**: Tongues in public worship must follow the Pauline rules: limited number, one at a time, with interpretation — or silence ([1 Cor 14:27–28](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/00a1de35-98f1-481a-956f-e98e2b52331d/ref/)).
- **Not a basis for spiritual elitism**: The presence or absence of the gift of tongues says nothing about a person's holiness or standing before God.

## 6. Tongues and the Liturgy

The Church's liturgical law and long-standing pastoral practice reflect St. Paul's principles:

- The **Roman Missal** and the **Code of Canon Law** do not provide for glossolalic utterances within the Eucharistic liturgy. The liturgy has its own inspired language — the sacred texts of Scripture and the Church's prayer — which is the normative mode of the Spirit's speech in the assembly.
- Charismatic prayer groups, which often include the exercise of tongues, are understood as **paraliturgical** — complementary to the liturgy but not replacing or disrupting it.
- St. Paul's rule remains the gold standard: "All things should be done decently and in order" ([1 Cor 14:40](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/00a1de35-98f1-481a-956f-e98e2b52331d/ref/)).

## 7. Distinction from Related Phenomena

| Phenomenon                     | Distinction                                                                                                                                             |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Xenolalia (Pentecost)**      | Speaking in identifiable human languages unknown to the speaker; the hearers understand naturally. This was the mode at Pentecost.                      |
| **Glossolalia (Corinth)**      | Speaking in a language not identifiable as any known human tongue — "tongues of angels" — not understood by hearers without charismatic interpretation. |
| **Ecstatic Utterance (Pagan)** | Unintelligible speech in non-Christian religious contexts (e.g., the Delphic oracle, Dionysian cults). Distinguished by context, content, and fruits.   |
| **Psychological Phenomenon**   | Non-supernatural vocalization produced by psychological suggestion or emotional states. Distinguished by fruits and ecclesial discernment.              |
| **Interpretation of Tongues**  | The complementary charism that renders tongues intelligible to the assembly. Tongues + interpretation = functional equivalent of prophecy.              |

## 8. Practical & Pastoral Considerations

- The gift of tongues is a **genuine charism** of the Holy Spirit, attested in Scripture and the Church's living experience. It should not be dismissed or mocked — nor should it be elevated to a place of disproportionate importance.
- St. Paul's rule for public worship remains binding: no uninterpreted tongues in the assembly. This is not a denial of the gift but a recognition that intelligible edification is the goal of all charismatic expression in the Church.
- The proper place for uninterpreted tongues is **private prayer** — "speaking to themselves and to God" ([1 Cor 14:28](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/00a1de35-98f1-481a-956f-e98e2b52331d/ref/)).
- The ultimate model of Spirit-filled speech is the **Eucharistic liturgy** itself — the Word proclaimed intelligibly in the vernacular, and the Word made present substantially on the altar. All charisms are ordered to this summit.
- Those who exercise the gift of tongues should cultivate the **greater gifts** — faith, hope, and above all charity ([1 Cor 12:31–13:13](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/00a1de35-98f1-481a-956f-e98e2b52331d/ref/)). Tongues are a means; love is the end.
