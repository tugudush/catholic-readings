# Receiving Holy Communion in a Civil Marriage

A pastoral and canonical study of a question frequently asked by Catholics who have married only civilly: _Can I receive Holy Communion?_ The answer depends on several distinct factors — the canonical status of the marriage, the state of grace of the person, the manner of life the couple is actually living, the question of public scandal, and the prudential judgment of the confessor or pastor. This document sets out each factor in turn, following the Church's own teaching and discipline.

---

## 1. The Question and the Short Answer

A baptized Catholic who married only in a civil ceremony is not validly married in the eyes of the Church (Code of Canon Law, c. 1108, 1117). The couple is therefore, canonically speaking, unmarried, and sexual relations between them fall outside the context of marriage (CCC 2353, 2390).

Whether such a person may receive Holy Communion depends on whether the person is in a **state of grace**, has made a **valid confession with a firm purpose of amendment**, is living in **complete continence** where required, is not giving **public scandal**, and has the **approval of a confessor or pastor** applying the Church's principles to the particular case. The short answer, therefore, is: **possibly, but not automatically.**

> "Anyone conscious of a grave sin must receive the sacrament of Reconciliation before coming to communion" (CCC 1385; c. 916).

The ordinary and preferable remedy is not indefinite reliance on continence but the **convalidation** of the marriage, by which the couple is brought into full sacramental standing (see [Convalidation](convalidation.md)).

---

## 2. The Canonical Situation: Civil Marriage without Canonical Form

For a baptized Catholic, marriage is a sacrament, and its valid celebration ordinarily requires **canonical form** — the exchange of consent before a priest or deacon delegated by the Church, and two witnesses (c. 1108 §1; CCC 1623–1624). This requirement binds every person baptized in the Catholic Church or received into it, even those who have ceased to practice (c. 1117).

A civil ceremony — before a mayor, judge, or other civil official — does not satisfy this requirement unless a dispensation from canonical form has been granted by the competent authority. Consequently:

| Situation                                               | Canonical status in the Church                                 |
| ------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Civil marriage of a Catholic, no dispensation from form | A **civil union lacking canonical form**; not a valid marriage |
| Couple living together in such a union                  | Treated as canonically unmarried                               |
| Children born of such a union                           | Legitimate (c. 1137) and in no way culpable                    |

The civil union may be recognized by the state and may involve deep mutual fidelity, shared life, and children; but for the Church it does not constitute the sacrament of Matrimony. The Church's pastors owe such couples charity and respect (CCC 1650; _Familiaris Consortio_ 84), while the objective canonical situation remains what it is until the marriage is validated.

### 2.1 The Two Controlling Texts: CCC 1650 and _Familiaris Consortio_ 84

For couples in irregular unions — including civilly married couples raising children — two magisterial texts govern the Church's entire pastoral approach, and they must always be read together:

- **_Catechism of the Catholic Church_, § 1650** states the objective situation. A Catholic who has entered a civil union that is not a valid marriage is in a situation that objectively contradicts the union of love between Christ and the Church signified and effected by the Eucharist; so long as that situation persists, the person ordinarily cannot be admitted to sacramental Communion. The Catechism adds the condition for reconciliation: it "can be granted only to those who repent of having violated the sign of the Covenant of Christ and his fidelity, and who are ready to live in complete continence, that is, by refraining from the acts proper to married couples."

- **_Familiaris Consortio_ 84** states the pastoral path. Pope John Paul II directs pastors to exercise careful discernment and great charity toward couples who cannot separate without serious harm — and he names **the upbringing of the children** among those serious reasons. Such couples are called to undertake "the duty to live in complete continence," and the same paragraph insists that they are **not separated from the Church**: they should listen to the word of God, attend the sacrifice of the Mass, persevere in prayer and penance, contribute to works of charity, and **bring up their children in the Christian faith**.

Read together, these texts hold two truths at once: the objective impossibility of sacramental Communion while the couple lives as spouses outside a valid marriage, and the couple's real, ongoing communion with the Church's life of word, worship, and charity — for their own salvation and for the sake of their children. Neither text may be cited against the other: CCC 1650 without _Familiaris Consortio_ 84 becomes mere exclusion, and _Familiaris Consortio_ 84 without CCC 1650 becomes mere accommodation.

## 3. The Objective Moral Question: Sexual Relations outside Marriage

Because the Church does not recognize a civil-only marriage as valid for a baptized Catholic, sexual relations within such a union are **objectively outside the context of marriage**.

> "Fornication is carnal union between an unmarried man and an unmarried woman. It is gravely contrary to the dignity of persons and of human sexuality" (CCC 2353).

> "The sexual act must take place exclusively within marriage. Outside of marriage it always constitutes a grave sin and excludes one from sacramental communion" (CCC 2390).

Pope John Paul II, in _Familiaris Consortio_ (1981), §82, stated that pastors, while treating such couples with great charity, "will regrettably not be able to admit them to the sacraments."

This is the **objective** moral truth. **Subjective culpability** is a separate question. The _Catechism_ teaches that "imputability and responsibility for an action can be diminished or even nullified by ignorance, inadvertence, duress, fear, habit, inordinate attachments, and other psychological or social factors" (CCC 1735). A couple who married in good faith, unaware of the Church's form requirement, may have acted with diminished culpability (CCC 1860). Once the teaching is known, however, the question becomes what one does with that knowledge — and the loving path is to seek regularization of the marriage.

## 4. The Discipline of Receiving Holy Communion

### 4.1 The State of Grace (c. 916; CCC 1385)

The Church requires that a person conscious of grave sin receive the Sacrament of Penance before approaching Holy Communion, except for the narrowly defined case of a grave reason, no opportunity for confession, and an act of perfect contrition including the resolution to confess as soon as possible (c. 916).

A confession is valid and fruitful only when it includes **true contrition and a firm purpose of amendment** — a real intention not to commit the sin again and to avoid the occasions of it (CCC 1451–1452; Trent, Session XIV, ch. 4). A verbal promise to change, made without any real intention, would not suffice. The confessor must assess the sincerity of the purpose in the particular case.

### 4.2 Those Who Are Not to Be Admitted (c. 915)

Canon 915 states that those who "obstinately persist in manifest grave sin" are not to be admitted to Holy Communion. This applies to a person whose public, externally visible way of life is itself a grave sin — for example, a couple publicly living together in a sexual relationship outside a valid marriage, with no sign of amendment. Where the person's life no longer presents such a manifest contradiction, and scandal is not involved, the norm of c. 916 (state of grace through confession) governs.

### 4.3 The Question of Public Scandal

Even where a person is in a state of grace, the reception of Communion must not cause **public scandal** — that is, it must not lead others to think that the Church approves of a publicly irregular situation (CCC 2284). Where the couple's situation is publicly known, the avoidance of scandal may require that they refrain from Communion, or that the situation be regularized before reception, in accordance with the judgment of the pastor.

## 5. The Path of Complete Continence

The Church's pastoral practice recognizes a specific path for couples in irregular unions who cannot separate without serious harm — for example, because of children — while they seek to regularize their situation. That path is **complete continence**: the couple lives together as brother and sister, abstaining entirely from sexual relations.

This principle is set out by Pope John Paul II in _Familiaris Consortio_ (1981), §84, which teaches that couples in such situations, when they cannot separate, are "called to live in complete continence, that is, in abstinence from the acts proper to married couples," and that in this way they "can have access to the sacraments of Penance and the Eucharist." The same teaching is reflected in the _Catechism_ (CCC 1650) and was reaffirmed by the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith in its **Letter to the Bishops of the Catholic Church concerning the Reception of Holy Communion by the Divorced and Remarried Members of the Faithful** (September 14, 1994).

The application of this principle to a civilly married couple is analogous: since the couple is canonically unmarried, complete continence removes the ongoing grave matter (sexual relations outside marriage), so that — with a valid confession, a firm purpose of amendment, and the avoidance of scandal — the question of Communion can be brought to the confessor.

### 5.1 What Complete Continence Means

| Included                                                            | Not required                      |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| Abstinence from sexual intercourse and all deliberately sexual acts | Separation from one's spouse      |
| Avoidance of occasions that would lead to sexual relations          | Abandoning the family or children |
| A firm resolve, renewed in prayer and grace                         | Pretending the marriage is valid  |

Complete continence is not the same as refusing all affection. It means abstaining from **acts proper to married couples** — that is, from sexual and deliberately intimate acts — while continuing to live together, raise the children, and care for one another in charity.

### 5.2 What Complete Continence Does Not Do

- It does **not** make the civil marriage canonically valid.
- It does **not**, by itself, automatically entitle one to Holy Communion; the other conditions (state of grace, confession, no scandal) and the confessor's judgment still apply.
- It does **not** dispense from the obligation to seek convalidation where possible.
- It does **not** make past sins unforgiven — but a valid confession, with contrition and firm purpose, does forgive them.

### 5.3 The Pastoral Elements of _Familiaris Consortio_ 84

_Familiaris Consortio_ 84 does not leave continent couples standing at the threshold of the Church. It names the concrete means by which they remain united to her while they await convalidation:

- **Listening to the word of God** — regular reading of Sacred Scripture and attentive participation in the Liturgy of the Word;
- **Attending the sacrifice of the Mass** — being present at Sunday Mass with the family even when not yet receiving Communion;
- **Persevering in prayer and penance** — daily family prayer, the Rosary, and acts of penance offered for the grace of fidelity;
- **Contributing to works of charity and justice** — serving the parish and the poor as a family;
- **Bringing up the children in the Christian faith** — baptism, catechesis, and the daily witness of the parents' fidelity to each other and to the Church.

For couples with children, this last element is central. The continence of the parents is itself a witness to the children of the seriousness of marriage, and the family's life of prayer and worship makes the home a domestic church (CCC 1656–1657). The Church's expectation is not that the couple withdraw until the situation is fixed, but that they live the Christian life fully in the ways that remain open to them.

## 6. The Role of the Confessor and Pastor

The application of these principles to a particular person is not something the person should decide privately, nor is it a matter a general rule can settle in the abstract. The Church entrusts the assessment to the **internal forum** — the person's conscience as examined with a confessor — while the **external forum** concerns the publicly verifiable situation.

In practice, the person should:

1. **Speak candidly with a priest or confessor**, disclosing that the marriage is civil only, that the couple lives together, that they have children, and that they intend to live in complete continence.
2. **Receive the Sacrament of Penance** with genuine contrition for any past sins and a firm purpose of amendment.
3. **Follow the confessor's guidance** about receiving Communion, avoiding scandal, and the manner of life in the interim.
4. **Pursue convalidation** as the ordinary and preferred remedy.

> "The person should not decide this matter solely by relying on a private promise or on a general rule. The situation should be discussed candidly with an individual priest, who can examine both the internal forum — the person's conscience and confession — and the external forum — the publicly verifiable canonical situation."

---

## 7. Children Are Not an Obstacle

Children born of a civil union are **legitimate** (c. 1137) and bear no guilt whatsoever for their parents' canonical situation. They are not an obstacle to the reception of the sacraments by their parents; indeed, the welfare of the children is one of the serious reasons recognized by the Church for a couple remaining together while living in complete continence (_Familiaris Consortio_ 84). The Catholic party also carries the serious duty, in all that is within his or her power, to have the children baptized and raised in the Catholic faith (c. 1125 §1).

_Familiaris Consortio_ 84 names the upbringing of the children explicitly among the serious reasons that may make separation impossible, and it addresses such parents directly: they are to bring up their children in the Christian faith, and the Christian community is to help them do so. In this way the couple's continence is not a private burden but a service to the family: it preserves the truth of marriage for the children, keeps the home anchored in the word of God, the Mass, prayer, and charity, and prepares the whole family for full sacramental life once the marriage is convalidated. The children, far from being a reason to abandon the Church's discipline, are its most urgent beneficiaries.

---

## 8. In the Meantime: Spiritual Communion

While the couple is not yet able to receive Holy Communion, they may remain united to Christ through **spiritual Communion** — an act of faith, love, and desire for the Eucharist made when sacramental reception is not possible or not prudent (Council of Trent, Session XIII, ch. 8). See [Spiritual Communion](../liturgy/spiritual-communion.md) for the meaning, practice, and prayer.

Spiritual Communion is not a substitute for sacramental reception or for Confession, and it must be accompanied by a sincere desire to resolve whatever prevents worthy reception. But it is a genuine act of devotion that sustains the soul on the way to full communion.

---

## 9. The Ordinary Remedy: Convalidation

The definitive resolution of the situation is the **convalidation** of the marriage — bringing the civil union into full sacramental standing by renewing consent in canonical form before a priest or deacon and two witnesses (c. 1156–1160). Where renewal of consent is impossible, the bishop may sometimes grant **radical sanation** (_sanatio in radice_, c. 1161–1165), healing the marriage at its root without a new ceremony. The full canonical and pastoral treatment is found in [Convalidation](convalidation.md).

After convalidation, the couple may confess, receive the Eucharist regularly, and live the full sacramental life of marriage. The Church desires this for every couple, and the process is ordinarily simple for couples already living together with children.

---

## 10. Eastern Catholic Considerations

The Eastern Catholic Churches (_sui iuris_ Churches) share the same sacramental theology of marriage and the same doctrine of the Real Presence, but they have their own canonical provisions in the _Code of Canons of the Eastern Churches_ (CCEO, c. 776–866) and their own liturgical traditions, including the Office of Crowning (_Stephánoma_). Eastern Catholics in a civil-only marriage should follow the discipline and pastoral guidance of their own priest or eparchy; the principles of the state of grace, complete continence, and the avoidance of scandal apply equally, while the procedural path may differ.

## 11. Levels of Authority

| Element                                                               | Authority                                                                                    |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| The Real Presence of Christ in the Eucharist                          | Dogma (CCC 1374; Council of Trent, Session XIII)                                             |
| The requirement of the state of grace for Communion                   | Doctrine and universal discipline (c. 916; CCC 1385)                                         |
| The obligation of canonical form for the valid marriage of a Catholic | Doctrine and universal discipline (c. 1108, 1117)                                            |
| The gravity of sexual relations outside marriage                      | Doctrine (CCC 2353, 2390)                                                                    |
| The path of complete continence in irregular unions                   | Pastoral discipline applying doctrine (_Familiaris Consortio_ 84; CCC 1650; 1994 CDF Letter) |
| The judgment whether a particular person may receive Communion        | Prudential application by the confessor or pastor in the individual case                     |

The general principle is firm; the application to any particular person belongs to the pastoral care of the priest, who alone can weigh the full circumstances.
