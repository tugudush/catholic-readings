# St. Charbel Makhlouf: The Hermit of Annaya

> **Source note:** This biography distinguishes Charbel's documented religious life, official testimony in his cause, later hagiography and local tradition, Church teaching and liturgical recognition, and modern devotional composition. Miracle reports, incorruptibility claims, relic accounts, and quotations are qualified according to their source and ecclesial status; English wording varies by translation.

---

## 1. Introduction and Identity

**St. Charbel Makhlouf** (Arabic: _Šarbil Maḫlūf_; born **Youssef Antoun Makhlouf**, 8 May 1828 – 24 December 1898) was a Lebanese Maronite Catholic monk, priest, and hermit. He is one of the most beloved saints of the Middle East — the first confessor of the Eastern Churches to be canonized according to the modern procedure of the Catholic Church, and a figure whose sanctity bridges the ancient monastic traditions of the Christian East with the universal Church of Rome. Known during his lifetime for his heroic practice of poverty, chastity, obedience, silence, and prayer, his fame multiplied after his death through an extraordinary stream of miracles attributed to his intercession. His feast day is **24 July** (the date of his baptism in the Maronite calendar; in the Roman calendar it is celebrated on the same date as an _Optional Memorial_).

> "A new eminent member of monastic sanctity comes to enrich by his example and intercession the whole Christian people."
> — Pope St. Paul VI, Beatification Homily, 5 December 1965

---

## 2. Early Life

### Birth and Family

Youssef Antoun Makhlouf was born on **8 May 1828** in the village of **Bekaa Kafra** (also spelled Beqaa Kafra or Gégà-Kafra), the highest inhabited village in Lebanon, nestled in the mountains of the Kadisha Valley — known since antiquity as the "Valley of the Saints" for its concentration of hermitages and monasteries. He was the fourth of five children born to **Antoun Zaarour Makhlouf**, a peasant farmer, and **Brigitta Chidiac**, a pious and mortified woman known for her continual fasting and devotion.

Youssef's childhood was marked by early loss. His father died when he was only **three years old**, and his mother subsequently remarried a devout man who, according to Eastern Christian custom (where married men may be ordained to the priesthood), later became a priest. This stepfather, along with Youssef's two **hermit uncles** who lived in the Valley of the Saints, deeply influenced his spiritual formation.

### Early Piety and the Shepherd's Cave

At the age of **ten**, Youssef was sent to work as a shepherd to help support his family. Far from resenting this labor, he transformed it into a school of prayer. He spent his free time in a natural grotto near the village — today a pilgrimage site known as **"the Saint's Cave"** (_Maghārat al-Qiddīs_) — where he prayed in solitude. His mother, Brigitta, though grieved by the prospect of losing her son to the religious life, spoke the words that would shape his destiny:

> "If you were not to be a good religious, I would tell you: come back home. But now I know that the Lord wants you in His service. And in my sorrow at being separated from you, I say to Him, resigned: May He bless you, my child, and make of you a saint."

### Departure for the Monastery

One night in **1851**, at the age of **twenty-three**, Youssef heard the voice of the Lord calling him with unmistakable clarity. Without bidding farewell to anyone — in a gesture reminiscent of the Gospel call — he arose and set out on foot for the **Monastery of Our Lady of Mayfouq** (_Deir Sayyidat Maifūq_). He would never return home.

## 3. Monastic Life

### Entry into the Lebanese Maronite Order

Youssef was received into the **Lebanese Maronite Order** (O.M.L.) and, upon entering the novitiate, took the religious name **Charbel** — in Syriac (_Šarbil_), meaning "the account of God" or "the story of God." He professed his solemn vows in **1853** and was sent to the **Monastery of St. Maron** in **Annaya**, where he would spend the remainder of his life.

### Studies and Priestly Ordination

Charbel studied philosophy and theology at the Monastery of St. Cyprian in **Kfifane**, where he was known for his assiduous study, his love of the Sacred Scriptures, and his devotion to the Blessed Sacrament. He was ordained a priest on **23 July 1859** at the patriarchal seat of **Bkerké**. Returning to Annaya, he lived the common life of the monastery for the next sixteen years — serving the poor and the sick, working in the fields, and fulfilling every mission assigned to him in perfect obedience.

### The Call to the Hermitage

In **1875**, at the age of forty-seven, Charbel received permission from his superiors to embrace the **Rule of the Hermits** of the Maronite Order. This rule permitted monks to live in small communities of no more than three, dedicated to a life of solitude, continual prayer, manual labor, fasting, penance, and silence. For Charbel, this was "like a second birth."

He took up residence at the **Hermitage of Saints Peter and Paul** (_al-Maḥbasah_), located on a hill overlooking the Monastery of St. Maron in Annaya, at an altitude of approximately 1,300 meters. There he would remain for the next **twenty-three years**, until his death.

---

## 4. The Life of the Hermit

### A Life Hidden in God

Charbel embraced the eremitical life with radical consistency. The testimonies of his fellow monks paint the portrait of a man entirely absorbed in God:

- He rose at **midnight** for the Office of Vigils and remained in prayer through the night.
- He celebrated **Holy Mass** daily — the center and summit of his life — with such intense devotion that those present were moved to tears.
- He spent hours in **silent adoration** before the Blessed Sacrament, kneeling without support.
- He was frequently found praying with his **arms extended in the form of a cross** (_orans_ posture), a practice rooted in the ancient Eastern Christian tradition.
- His cell was **extremely poor** — a small room with a rush mat for a bed, a stool, a table, and a wooden pillow.
- He observed **continual fasting**, eating only once a day (after noon, according to the Maronite monastic rule), never consuming meat or wine.
- He practiced **strict silence**, speaking only when necessary and when obedience or charity required it.
- He performed **manual labor** in the fields and garden, following the Benedictine principle of _ora et labora_.

### Obedience unto Death

The monks recounted that Charbel would never act without the explicit permission of his superior. On one occasion, when a serpent entered his cell, he remained motionless rather than violate his vow of stability without permission. He left the hermitage only to celebrate Mass in the monastery church below or when explicitly ordered by his superior.

## 5. Death

### The Christmas Eve Mass

On **24 December 1898** — Christmas Eve — Charbel was celebrating the Divine Liturgy (Holy Mass) in the monastery church. At the moment of the **elevation of the Sacred Host**, he suffered a stroke (or, according to some accounts, a heart attack). He was carried to his cell, where he spent **eight days in agony**, continuing to pray and refusing more nourishing food, observing the monastic rule to the very end. He died on **Christmas Eve**, 24 December 1898, at the age of seventy. His final words were the names of Jesus and Mary.

### The Luminous Tomb

Charbel was buried in the monastery cemetery at Annaya. Within months of his death, strange phenomena were reported at his grave: a **brilliant light** was seen emanating from the tomb at night. When the grave was opened several months later, his body was found incorrupt, exuding a reddish liquid described as **blood and sweat** (_sāʾil_). This phenomenon, verified by ecclesiastical authorities, attracted widespread attention. The liquid — now referred to as the **"oil of St. Charbel"** — has continued to exude from his relics and has been associated with numerous physical healings.

## 6. Miracles and Posthumous Fame

### The River of Mercy

Pope Leo XIV, during his apostolic visit to Annaya on 1 December 2025, described Charbel's posthumous work as a "river of mercy":

> "After St. Charbel's death, his work multiplied and became like a river of mercy. On the twenty-second of every month, thousands of pilgrims from different countries come here to spend a day in prayer and to renew their souls and bodies."

The **22nd of each month** is a special day of pilgrimage to Annaya, commemorating the date of his death (the 22nd of December in the old calendar reckoning). Pilgrims come from Lebanon, throughout the Middle East, and across the world — Muslims and Druze as well as Christians — seeking healing and intercession.

### Notable Miracles

Among the thousands of reported miracles, several stand out:

- **Nohad El-Chami** (1993): A Lebanese woman paralyzed for years was healed after St. Charbel appeared to her in a dream, performed a surgical procedure on her neck, and directed her to go to Annaya. The scar-like marks on her neck were documented by medical professionals. This miracle was instrumental in the spread of his devotion in the late twentieth century.

- **Countless healings** associated with the oil that exudes from his tomb and from his relics, including cures of cancer, paralysis, blindness, and infertility.

- The phenomenon of the **incorrupt body**, preserved for decades after death, which continued to exude the mysterious liquid. Though the body eventually decomposed naturally (leaving the bones as relics), the flow of oil was interrupted only for brief periods.

## 7. Canonization

St. Charbel's path to the altars proceeded under Pope St. Paul VI, who held him in deep personal veneration:

| Stage             | Date            | Celebrant                          |
| ----------------- | --------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| **Beatification** | 5 December 1965 | Pope St. Paul VI                   |
| **Canonization**  | 9 October 1977  | Pope St. Paul VI                   |
| **Feast Day**     | 24 July         | Optional Memorial (Roman Calendar) |

### Significance of the Beatification Date

The beatification took place on **5 December 1965**, during the closing days of the **Second Vatican Council**. Pope Paul VI deliberately chose this moment to present Charbel as a sign of the Church's universality and the enduring vitality of Eastern Christian monasticism:

> "Here at the conclusion of the Second Vatican Ecumenical Council, a hermit of the Lebanese mountain is inscribed among the blessed — the first confessor of the East raised to the altars according to the current procedure of the Catholic Church. What a symbol of union between East and West! What a sign of ecclesial fraternity among Christians of the whole world!"

### Canonization Homily

In his canonization homily on 9 October 1977, Paul VI described Charbel as "an artisan of peace, paradoxical, since he sought it apart from the world, in God alone, with whom he was as if inebriated." He noted that "his lamp, kindled on the summit of the mountain of his hermitage in the last century, has shone with an ever-greater brilliance, and unanimity was quickly reached concerning his sanctity."

---

## 8. Papal Visit of Pope Leo XIV (2025)

On **1 December 2025**, Pope Leo XIV made an apostolic journey to Lebanon and visited the tomb of St. Charbel at the Monastery of St. Maron in Annaya. He summarized the saint's legacy in these words:

> "The Holy Spirit formed him so that he could teach those who live without God how to pray, those who live immersed in noise how to be silent, those who live ostentatiously how to be modest, and those who seek riches how to be poor. All of these behaviors are counter-cultural, yet that is precisely why they attract us, just like fresh, pure water draws those walking in the desert."

The Pope presented a **lamp** as a gift to the monastery, entrusting Lebanon and its people to the protection of St. Charbel, "so that they may always walk in the light of Christ."

## 10. Patronage

St. Charbel Makhlouf is the **patron saint of Lebanon**. He is also invoked as:

- A patron of **hermits and contemplatives**
- A patron of **the Maronite Church** and all Eastern Catholic Churches
- An intercessor for **physical healing** and **impossible causes**
- A model for **priests and religious** in their evangelical witness

St. Charbel is depicted in traditional iconography with the following attributes:

| **Long white beard**                 | His age and asceticism; he died at seventy after decades of hermitage                                       |
| **Prayer rope** (_mashbaḥah_)        | The Eastern Christian equivalent of the Rosary, used for the Jesus Prayer                                   |
| **Raised hands in prayer** (_orans_) | His characteristic posture of prayer with outstretched arms                                                 |
| **Censer or cross**                  | His priestly ministry and devotion to the Divine Liturgy                                                    |
| **Cedar of Lebanon**                 | Symbolic of his Lebanese homeland and the Psalmist's image of the just man                                  |
| **The hermitage of Annaya**          | The mountain setting of his hidden life, often shown in the background                                      |

In modern devotional images, he is often depicted with a serene expression, eyes cast down or lifted in contemplation, holding a cross and a prayer rope.

## 13. The Maronite Monastic Tradition

To understand St. Charbel, one must understand the tradition from which he came. The **Lebanese Maronite Order** traces its origins to **St. Maron** (died c. 410 A.D.), a Syriac hermit-priest whose disciples formed the nucleus of the Maronite Church — one of the twenty-three Eastern Catholic Churches _sui iuris_ in full communion with Rome.

The Maronite monastic tradition preserves the ancient Syrian-Antiochene spirituality, characterized by:

- A strong emphasis on **asceticism and solitude**, rooted in the Desert Fathers
- The **Divine Liturgy of St. James** (and later, the Liturgy of St. John Chrysostom, adapted)
- Use of **Syriac** (a dialect of Aramaic, the language of Christ) in the liturgy
- A unique tradition of **married parish clergy** alongside celibate monastics
- Deep devotion to the **Blessed Virgin Mary** (_Sayyidat al-Jabal_, Our Lady of the Mountain)

Charbel stands as the supreme modern flowering of this ancient tradition — a living link between the Desert Fathers of the fourth century and the universal Church of the twenty-first century.

## 14. Spiritual Legacy

### The Counter-Cultural Witness

Pope Leo XIV identified four dimensions of St. Charbel's legacy that speak with particular urgency to the modern world:

1. **Prayer for those who live without God**: Charbel's entire life was a liturgy of praise, demonstrating that the primary vocation of every Christian is adoration.
2. **Silence for those immersed in noise**: In an age of constant stimulation, Charbel's radical silence offers a remedy to the fragmentation of the soul.
3. **Modesty for those who live ostentatiously**: His hidden life is a rebuke to the cult of self-promotion and celebrity.
4. **Poverty for those who seek riches**: His cell, bare of all comfort, testifies that the one thing necessary is union with God.

### The Primacy of the Eucharist

For Charbel, the **Holy Mass** was the center of existence. He prepared for each celebration with intense prayer and prolonged his thanksgiving for hours afterward. His death at the moment of the elevation — offering his life in union with the Sacrifice of the Altar — sealed a life that was itself a living Eucharistic offering.

### Intercessor for Unity

At both his beatification and canonization, Pope Paul VI presented Charbel as a "symbol of union between East and West." In a Church still marked by division between Catholic and Orthodox, Latin and Eastern, Charbel — a monk of the Antiochene tradition canonized by Rome — stands as a prophetic sign that holiness transcends all divisions.

## 16. Prayers Asking for the Intercession of St. Charbel Makhlouf

> **Prayer status:** Unless a source is identified below, this prayer is a devotional composition for this article. It is not presented as words written by the saint or as an official liturgical collect.

St. Charbel is one of the most powerful intercessors of the modern era, with thousands of documented miracles attributed to his heavenly aid — especially physical healings, reconciliations, and conversions. The **22nd of each month** is a traditional day of pilgrimage and prayer to St. Charbel, and many faithful keep a novena to him in times of illness, distress, or spiritual dryness. The oil of St. Charbel, which exudes from his relics, is used as a sacramental for anointing the sick. The following prayer, rooted in the Maronite tradition, may be used to seek his intercession:

    O St. Charbel, holy monk and hermit of Annaya,
    you who lived hidden in God on the mountain of Lebanon,
    teach us to pray in silence,
    to love the Blessed Sacrament with your fervour,
    and to offer every suffering in union with the Holy Sacrifice of the Mass.

    You who are a river of mercy flowing from the Heart of Christ,
    intercede for us before the throne of the Most High:
    obtain for the sick the healing of body and soul,
    for those who live without God the grace of conversion,
    for those immersed in noise the gift of holy silence,
    and for all the faithful perseverance in the love of Jesus and Mary.

    O St. Charbel, patron of Lebanon and friend of the suffering,
    pray for us now and at the hour of our death.
    Amen.

_A shorter invocation for daily use:_

    St. Charbel, hermit of Annaya,
    pray for us.
