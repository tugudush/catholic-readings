# St. Anselm of Canterbury: The Magnificent Doctor, Monk of Bec

> **Source note:** This biography distinguishes Anselm's own writings, the testimony of his secretary and biographer Eadmer, contemporary and near-contemporary records, later hagiography and local tradition, Church teaching and liturgical recognition, and modern devotional composition. Dates, contested episodes, and quotations are qualified according to their source and certainty. Where a claim is disputed — notably the date of his canonization and the meaning of his most famous argument — the dispute is stated rather than resolved by assertion. English wording of his works varies by translation.

---

## 1. Introduction and Identity

**St. Anselm of Canterbury** (Latin: _Anselmus Cantuariensis_; Italian: _Anselmo d'Aosta_; 1033 – 21 April 1109) was a **Benedictine monk, prior and abbot of Bec in Normandy, Archbishop of Canterbury, and Doctor of the Church**. Since the eighteenth century the Church has honoured him with the title **"Magnificent Doctor"** (_Doctor Magnificus_), and Pope Benedict XVI used that same title in his letter for the ninth centenary of Anselm's death in 2009.

He is remembered above all for two things. The first is the programme he named in the original title of his most famous book: **_fides quaerens intellectum_**, "faith seeking understanding." The second is the pair of arguments that flowed from that programme — the "one argument" (_unum argumentum_) of the _Proslogion_ for the existence of God, and the account of why God became man in _Cur Deus Homo_, which gave Western theology its classic vocabulary of **satisfaction**.

Anselm stands at a hinge in Western history. He was formed in the monastic school of Bec in the generation after Lanfranc, wrote as a monk for monks, and yet became the first Latin theologian after the Fathers to attempt a sustained, ordered, purely rational exposition of the contents of faith. That combination — a monastic heart and a dialectician's method — explains both his influence and the later argument over what to call him.

## 2. Historical Context: Monks, Schools, and the Freedom of the Church

### A Church under reform

Anselm's life coincided with the movement historians call the **Gregorian reform**, named for Pope St. Gregory VII (1073–1085). Its aims were the moral and canonical renewal of the clergy, the abolition of simony and clerical concubinage, and above all the recovery of the Church's independence from lay rulers — the programme the sources call **_libertas Ecclesiae_**, "the freedom of the Church."

### The Investiture Controversy

The concrete flashpoint was **investiture**: the practice by which kings and lords installed bishops and abbots by handing them the ring and staff, the symbols of their spiritual office. To the reformers this meant that ecclesiastical office was being treated as a piece of property granted by a layman. The resulting struggle between the papacy and the Western emperors and kings — the **Investiture Controversy** — dominated Anselm's public life and sent him into exile twice.

### Two ways of doing theology

The eleventh and twelfth centuries also saw Latin theology divide into two broad styles, which Pope Benedict XVI described in his catechesis on [Monastic Theology and Scholastic Theology](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/30829e5f-67ce-48dd-9ea5-43ba62fd63b2/ref/page1):

- **Monastic theology**, practised by monks and abbots within the cloister, ordered to nourishing charity and the contemplation of God; its exemplars are St. Bernard of Clairvaux and, in the previous generation, Anselm.
- **Scholastic theology**, practised by the _magistri_ of the urban cathedral schools, "anxious to show the reasonableness and soundness of the Mysteries of God," using the disciplined instruments of dialectic, question, and disputation.

Anselm genuinely belongs to the first and genuinely prepared the second. This is why the labels attached to him in popular usage need care (§8.7).

## 3. Early Life in Aosta (1033–c. 1059)

### Birth and family

Anselm was born at **Aosta**, a town on the Italian side of the Alps, in **1033 or early 1034**, the first child of a noble family. His father **Gundulf** was a Lombard; his mother **Ermenberga** came from a Burgundian family. Eadmer, his biographer, describes the father as a harsh and improvident man and the mother as a woman of deep piety who gave her son his first religious formation.

### The dream and the mountain

Eadmer records a childhood dream that Anselm later recounted himself. Believing that God must dwell on the snow-capped peaks above his native valley, the boy dreamed that he was summoned to the court of the great King, seated at his feet, and fed white bread. The image of God "at an inaccessible height" stayed with him: Benedict XVI, in his [letter for the ninth centenary](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/04b3f153-249b-473c-80ad-24d835a83d18/ref/page1), observes that to the boy who grew up among the mountains "it seemed impossible to imagine anything greater than God."

### Vocation delayed

At about fifteen Anselm asked to be admitted to a monastery. His father refused, and the refusal held even when the boy, gravely ill, begged for the habit. After his recovery his mother died, and Anselm entered a period of moral dissipation and neglect of study that he later acknowledged with candour.

### Departure (c. 1056)

He left home around 1056, crossed the Mont Cenis pass on foot, and spent nearly three years wandering through Burgundy and France. Around **1059 or 1060** he arrived in Normandy at the **Abbey of Bec**, drawn by the reputation of its prior, **Lanfranc of Pavia**, later Archbishop of Canterbury. The meeting changed his life: under Lanfranc he resumed study with intensity, and the monastic vocation he had sought as a boy was rekindled.

## 4. Bec: Monk, Prior, and Abbot (c. 1060–1093)

### Profession and ordination

Anselm took the monastic habit at about the age of twenty-seven and was ordained priest. Benedict XVI describes the years that followed as a life of asceticism and study in which he "rediscovered at a far higher level the same familiarity with God which he had had as a child" ([General Audience, 23 September 2009](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/203b4bb6-7bfd-4225-a4fc-aa2411a348f5/ref/page1)).

### Prior of Bec (1063)

When Lanfranc was appointed abbot of Caen in 1063, Anselm — after barely three years in the monastery — was made **prior** and given charge of the cloister school. The sources note his gentle method as an educator: he compared the young to small plants that thrive better outside a greenhouse and allowed them a "healthy" freedom.

### The two earliest works

It was at Bec, in response to requests from his own monks, that Anselm composed the two works on which his fame rests:

- The **_Monologion_** (1076–1077), written as an example of meditating on the reasonableness of faith. It argues to God's existence from the degrees of goodness and perfection found in creatures, and then treats the divine attributes and the Trinity — the latter by analogy with the human mind, its self-knowledge, and its self-love.
- The **_Proslogion_** (1077–1078), whose original title was **_Fides quaerens intellectum_**. It seeks a single argument that would be sufficient by itself to establish that God exists and is the highest good.

### Abbot of Bec (1078)

On the death of the founding abbot Herluin in 1078, Anselm was elected **abbot of Bec**. Under his governance Bec became one of the most influential monasteries and schools of the Latin West, and Anselm was repeatedly drawn into negotiations and visitations in England, where the abbey held lands and where Lanfranc now sat at Canterbury.

### Eadmer

His constant companion in the last period of his life was **Eadmer**, a monk of Christ Church, Canterbury, who served as his chaplain and secretary and wrote both a _Life of Anselm_ (_Vita Sancti Anselmi_) and a history of the English Church in his time (_Historia Novorum in Anglia_). Nearly everything known about Anselm's temperament and conversation comes through Eadmer's testimony — a near-contemporary witness of great value, though a devoted one.

## 5. Archbishop of Canterbury and the Investiture Controversy (1093–1109)

### The vacant see

Archbishop Lanfranc died in 1089. King **William II Rufus** left the see of Canterbury vacant for four years, drawing its revenues into the royal treasury while the Church in England fell into disorder. When Anselm came to England in 1092, public expectation fastened on him at once; he fled rather than appear to seek the office.

### Consecration (1093)

He was chosen archbishop in **March 1093** and consecrated bishop on **4 December 1093**. Eadmer records that when the king tried to invest him with the pastoral staff with his own hand, Anselm resisted, and the bishops present had to force his fingers open to receive it. The episode set the tone of his episcopate: he would not accept his spiritual office as a gift of the crown.

### The pallium and the Council of Rockingham (1095)

William Rufus had not recognised Pope Urban II. Anselm insisted on receiving the **pallium** — the symbol of metropolitan authority — from the pope, and received it in 1095 from a papal legate. At the **Council of Rockingham** in March 1095 the king's bishops attempted to have Anselm deposed or his appeal to Rome denied. He refused to submit, appealed to the Apostolic See, and the council collapsed without a verdict.

### First exile (1097–1100) and the Council of Bari (1098)

In October 1097 Anselm left England for Rome. Pope Urban II received him with honour and, at the **Council of Bari** in October 1098, asked him to set out the Latin case on the procession of the Holy Spirit against the objections of the Greek bishops present. Anselm argued the **_Filioque_** from Scripture and from reason; he later developed the case at length in his treatise _De Processione Spiritus Sancti_. He returned to England in September 1100, recalled by the new king **Henry I**.

### Second exile (1103–1106)

The second conflict turned on homage and investiture. Anselm refused to do homage to the king for his archbishopric, since the archbishopric was not the king's to give; Henry demanded the return of Canterbury lands and pressed for his own candidate. Anselm went to Rome in 1103 and remained in exile, supported by Pope Paschal II, until a settlement was negotiated. He returned to England in 1106.

### The settlement of 1107

The **concordat reached at Westminster in August 1107** resolved the immediate dispute: Henry I renounced investiture with ring and staff, while Anselm allowed bishops and abbots to do homage to the king for their temporal possessions before consecration. He spent his last two years governing his see in peace — visiting, reforming, and providing for the monks and clergy of his province.

> **Doctrinal weight:** Anselm's stand on investiture belongs to the Church's **discipline and prudential judgement** in a particular historical crisis, not to defined dogma. The underlying principle — that spiritual authority is not the creature of the State — was vindicated by the Church's later practice, but the specific arrangement of 1107 was a political settlement, later superseded. Anselm was also a man of his age in his relations with the English crown and with the papacy; the legend of a solitary prophet against the world should not replace the historical record.

---

## 6. Death

Anselm died at Canterbury on **21 April 1109**, on the Wednesday of Holy Week, in about his seventy-sixth year. Eadmer records his burial in the cathedral, near the tomb of Lanfranc. Benedict XVI, in the 2009 centenary letter, described the event as the occasion for the whole Church to renew "the memory of one of the most luminous figures in the tradition of the Church and in the history of Western European thought itself."

## 7. Doctor of the Church, Cult, and Relics

### The documentation of his sainthood

Anselm was venerated as a saint from an early date, but the often-repeated claim that he was **canonized by Pope Alexander VI in 1494** is not secure. The Bollandist editors of the _Acta Sanctorum_ read Alexander VI's letters of that year as an order for an inquiry into Anselm's life and miracles rather than as a bull of canonization. The same account records that St. Thomas Becket sought Anselm's canonization from Pope Alexander III in 1163, without the pope then proceeding. The cautious formulation is therefore: **his cult is immemorial and his sanctity has never been doubted, but a formal canonization is not securely documented.**

### Doctor of the Church (1720)

What is documented is his creation as **Doctor of the Church by Pope Clement XI in 1720**, and the accompanying decree of the Congregation of Rites of 3 February 1720 raising the rank of his feast and ordering the Creed to be recited at his Mass. The title conferred on him in the Church's usage is **_Doctor Magnificus_**, the "Magnificent Doctor."

### Tomb and relics

Anselm was buried in Canterbury Cathedral near Lanfranc's tomb, and his body was long believed to rest in the chapel that bears his name there. Claims about the later elevation of his relics are not uniformly supported by the documentary record, and the fate of the medieval shrine at the Reformation — like that of so many English shrines — leaves the present location of his remains uncertain. Authenticity should not be asserted beyond what the documents support.

## 8. Spirituality, Theology, and Contribution

### 8.1 _Fides quaerens intellectum_

Anselm's whole intellectual project is contained in that phrase, which he used as the original title of the _Proslogion_. In the first chapter he states the principle in the first person:

> "For I do not seek to understand that I may believe, but I believe in order to understand. For this also I believe — that unless I believed, I should not understand."

The formula is not his invention; it distils Augustine's _crede ut intellegas_ ("believe that you may understand"), itself a reading of Isaiah 7:9 in the Septuagint ("unless you believe, you shall not understand"). Benedict XVI, in his [catechesis on Anselm](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/203b4bb6-7bfd-4225-a4fc-aa2411a348f5/ref/page1), presents this as the posture of a monk whose thought was guided by "profound mystical experience": understanding is not the price of belief but its fruit, and its goal is not mastery of the mystery but a deeper entry into it.

The principle is not an eccentric medieval opinion; it is the Church's own account of theology. The International Theological Commission's [_Theology Today_](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/90c2d321-f38d-40f7-8a9a-d50d88968c0d/ref/59) describes the theological task as the believing intelligence striving "to appreciate and explore the intelligibility of the Word of God — _fides quaerens intellectum_ — and to offer a reasoned account of the truth of God." The First Vatican Council taught the same in [_Dei Filius_, chapter IV](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/e1a07ee5-0122-4396-a9db-862ea3b90918/ref/4): reason "enlightened by faith" does "achieve by God's gift some understanding, and that most profitable, of the mysteries," though it can never "penetrate these mysteries" as it penetrates the objects proper to itself. John Paul II took up the same inheritance in [_Fides et Ratio_ 16–17 and 42](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/06811b47-ea16-42d9-a748-524adbab66da/ref/17).

> **Doctrinal weight:** That faith is antecedent to theological understanding, and that reason enlightened by faith attains a real but imperfect understanding of revealed mysteries, is Catholic **doctrine** (Vatican I, _Dei Filius_ IV). The precise psychology of how grace and reason cooperate in that movement remains largely a matter of **theological opinion**.

### 8.2 The "one argument" of the _Proslogion_

Anselm sought a single argument (_unum argumentum_) needing no other proof. Its object is "**_aliquid quo maius cogitari non potest_**" — that than which nothing greater can be thought. In _Proslogion_ 2 he argues that even one who denies God's existence (the "fool" of Psalm 14/53) understands the phrase; but if that than which nothing greater can be thought existed only in the mind, something greater — namely the same being existing also in reality — could be thought, which is a contradiction; therefore it exists in reality. _Proslogion_ 3 adds that such a being cannot even be thought not to exist, and _Proslogion_ 4 identifies this as the one argument that suffices.

The argument drew an immediate objection from the monk **Gaunilo of Marmoutier**, whose _Liber pro insipiente_ ("On Behalf of the Fool") argued that by the same logic one could prove the existence of a perfect lost island. Anselm replied in a short treatise usually called _Quid ad haec respondeat editor ipsius libelli_ ("What the Author of this Booklet Answers to These Things").

**Its later reception is a history of disagreement, and this matters for how the argument should be presented:**

- **St. Thomas Aquinas** denied that it is a demonstration for us, since God's existence is not self-evident to the human mind; he built his own case on other foundations (_Summa Theologiae_ I, q. 2, a. 1).
- **St. Bonaventure** and later **Duns Scotus** accepted qualified versions of the argument.
- **Immanuel Kant** rejected it on the ground that existence is not a predicate that adds anything to a concept.
- **Modern philosophy** has revived it in modal form (Norman Malcolm, Alvin Plantinga), while others continue to reject it.
- **Karl Barth**, in his 1931 book _Fides quaerens intellectum_, read the _Proslogion_ not as a philosophical proof but as a description of the grammar of faith — an influential reading, though contested by historians of medieval philosophy.

> **Doctrinal weight:** The existence of God is **dogma**. The validity of Anselm's argument is a **philosophical question**, on which the Church has pronounced nothing. No Catholic is obliged to accept it, and no one is permitted to treat its rejection as heresy. The arguments for God's existence are exercises of reason offered in service of the faith, not articles of it.

### 8.3 _Cur Deus Homo_ and the satisfaction of offended honour

In _Cur Deus Homo_ (begun in England, 1094–1098) Anselm addresses the question the title poses: by what necessity did God become man? His method is announced in the preface, where he proposes to proceed **_remoto Christo, quasi numquam aliquid fuerit de illo_** — "Christ being set aside, as if nothing had been known of him" — that is, to show by reason alone that the Incarnation and Redemption are fitting and necessary, as a thought-experiment for the sake of argument, not as a denial of revelation.

The argument, in outline:

1. Sin is not merely a failure to do what is right; it is a **failure to render God the honour due to him**, and it therefore incurs a **debt** that man cannot pay.
2. That debt cannot be paid by man, because man owes all that he is already; nor can a mere creature make satisfaction of infinite worth to offended infinite majesty.
3. Therefore the one who makes satisfaction must be **both God and man** — able to offer something of infinite value, and belonging to the race that owes the debt.
4. The God-man offers to God, freely and out of love, a life of perfect obedience crowned by a death he did not owe; this **satisfaction** restores the order of justice and, by way of superabundance, obtains the restoration of the human race.
5. Anselm explicitly rejects the older "rights of the devil" framework, in which the ransom was owed to Satan.

Two qualifications are essential and are frequently lost in popular summaries. First, Anselm's necessity is a **necessity of fittingness within God's free decision to save**, not a constraint on God's freedom considered absolutely; the _Cur Deus Homo_ takes pains to deny that God was obliged to save anyone. Second, **satisfaction is not punishment**. Anselm's category is the restoration of right order and honour, not the infliction of a penalty on a substitute. The later Reformed doctrine of **penal substitution** is a distinct development, using forensic categories of imputation that Anselm does not use.

St. Thomas Aquinas received Anselm's achievement but widened it: in _Summa Theologiae_ III, q. 48 he sets out five complementary ways of understanding the efficacy of Christ's Passion — as **merit**, **satisfaction**, **sacrifice**, **redemption**, and **efficient causality**. The Church has never defined one theory of the atonement; it has defined the fact that Christ's death is a true sacrifice offered for our salvation.

> **Doctrinal weight:** That Christ truly suffered and died for our sins, and that his Passion is a sacrifice and satisfaction of infinite value, is **Catholic doctrine** (cf. the Council of Trent, Session XXII). The **satisfaction theory as a systematic explanation** is a **theological opinion** — a highly influential one, received into the Church's preaching and hymnody, but not a definition, and not exclusive of other legitimate accounts.

### 8.4 Freedom, grace, and predestination

Anselm's most under-appreciated contribution is his analysis of freedom. In **_De Libertate Arbitrii_** he defines free will not as the power to choose either good or evil, but as **_the power of preserving rectitude of will for its own sake_**. His argument is characteristically sharp: if freedom consisted in the ability to sin, then God would not be free, and the blessed in heaven, who cannot sin, would have lost their freedom. Freedom is therefore a power _for_ the good, which sin weakens rather than exercises.

In **_De Concordia_** (1107–1108), his last work, he treats the compatibility of divine foreknowledge and predestination with human freedom and the cooperation of grace with the will: grace precedes, accompanies, and follows the good act, and the will remains genuinely free under it.

> **Doctrinal weight:** Anselm's definition of freedom and his account of the mode of cooperation between grace and free will are **theological opinions**. The Church has defined that grace is necessary and that human freedom is real, but not the precise metaphysical mechanism; the later _De auxiliis_ controversy shows how much room remains for legitimate disagreement here.

### 8.5 The _Filioque_ and the treatise on the procession of the Holy Spirit

The defence of the Latin doctrine of the double procession at Bari in 1098 became a book: **_De Processione Spiritus Sancti_** (1102). Anselm argues that the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son as from **one principle** (_tamquam ab uno principio_), because Father and Son are one God, and that the Greek objection confuses the distinction of the divine persons with a division of the divine essence. The treatise became a standard Western statement of the case and is still cited in Catholic–Orthodox discussion; the Eastern Christian objection that the Latin approach rationalises the mystery is one Anselm would have denied, since he held the procession to be revealed and only its grammar to be argued.

> **Doctrinal weight:** The _Filioque_ as the Latin Church professes it is **doctrine**; its insertion into the Creed was an **ecclesiastical and disciplinary act**, and the question of its canonical legitimacy in the Creed is a distinct matter from its dogmatic truth. This distinction is central to the modern Catholic–Orthodox dialogue. See [church-history/greek-orthodox-church.md](../church-history/greek-orthodox-church.md).

### 8.6 Mariology and the fittingness of the Virgin's purity

In _Cur Deus Homo_ II.16 Anselm writes that it was supremely fitting that the Virgin from whom the God-man took flesh should be adorned with "a purity than which no greater can be conceived under God." The text is often cited as a landmark in the development of the Church's understanding of the Blessed Virgin's immunity from all sin — a doctrine defined as dogma by Pope Pius IX in _Ineffabilis Deus_ (1854). Anselm's sentence is a witness to the trajectory of the Church's faith, not the definition itself, and he did not address the questions later settled by the definition.

### 8.7 "Father of Scholasticism"? The question of the label

Popular presentations — and a good deal of social-media quotation — call Anselm the "father of Scholasticism." The label needs three qualifications.

1. **It is a simplification.** Scholastic theology as a method — the _quaestio_, the disputed question, the commentary on Peter Lombard's _Sentences_, the university faculty — belongs to the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, after Anselm. Its representative figures are Abelard, Lombard, Albert the Great, Bonaventure, and Aquinas.
2. **The Church's own reading places him in the monastic stream.** Benedict XVI's catechesis on monastic and scholastic theology sets Anselm with the monks: theology pursued within prayer, ordered to charity and contemplation, not the public disputation of the schools. Anselm himself wrote for his own monks, not for a faculty.
3. **Yet he prepared the schools.** His insistence that the contents of faith can be exhibited as a coherent _rationes necessariae_ — and his willingness in _Cur Deus Homo_ to argue _sola ratione_ — gave later scholasticism both its confidence and its method. In that limited sense the title is defensible as a description of influence, not of membership.

> **Attribution caution:** When the phrase "father of Scholasticism" appears in devotional posts, it is sometimes intended as praise and sometimes as an accusation — for some polemicists "Scholasticism" names the moment the Church allegedly replaced the Gospel with Aristotle. Neither reading is history. Anselm's _fides quaerens intellectum_ is a monastic, prayerful, and thoroughly Catholic principle, and the later Reformed orthodox theologians who most attacked "scholasticism" (Turretin, Voetius, the Westminster and Second London divines) themselves used scholastic method extensively.

### 8.8 The spiritual physiognomy

Benedict XVI's summary in the 2009 centenary letter is worth quoting, because it gathers the elements that a single quotation cannot:

> "Anselm's exemplary monastic experience, his original method of rethinking the Christian mystery, his penetrating theological and philosophical doctrine, his teaching on the inviolable value of the conscience and on freedom as responsible adherence to truth and goodness, his enthusiastic work as a pastor of souls, totally dedicated to promoting the 'freedom of the Church', have never ceased to inspire in the past the liveliest interest."
> — Pope Benedict XVI, Letter to Cardinal Giacomo Biffi, 15 April 2009

| **_De Processione Spiritus Sancti_**                                                     | 1102          | The Latin case for the _Filioque_, developed from the Council of Bari.                                                                                                      |

| **_De Concordia Praescientiae et Praedestinationis et Gratiae Dei cum Libero Arbitrio_** | 1107–1108     | His last work: foreknowledge, predestination, grace, and free will.                                                                                                         |

### Patronage

| Patronage or association                            | Historical, devotional, or vocational connection                                                                                          |

| **Challand-Saint-Anselme**, Aosta Valley            | A town named for him, with local patronal observance.                                                                                     |
| **Theologians and students of theology**            | A devotional association drawn from his programme of _fides quaerens intellectum_; not an official universal patronage.                   |
| **Those seeking wisdom and understanding in faith** | Devotional extension of his charism; properly understood as a request for the gift of understanding, not for intellectual mastery of God. |

### Feast days

| Celebration                                     | Date     | Rite, calendar, or notes                                                                           |
| ----------------------------------------------- | -------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **St. Anselm, Bishop and Doctor of the Church** | 21 April | Latin Church; **optional memorial** in the General Roman Calendar; the anniversary of his death.   |
| Benedictine observance                          | 21 April | Kept with greater rank in Benedictine calendars, as befits a Doctor of the Order.                  |
| Eastern Catholic and Orthodox calendars         | —        | Not generally commemorated: a Western medieval saint who lived after the separation of 1054.       |

The feast of a saint honours God's grace in him and situates his memory within the Church's worship; it is not the worship of the saint, which belongs to God alone.

### Iconography

Anselm is conventionally depicted in the **black Benedictine habit**, and, as archbishop, with **mitre, pallium, and pastoral staff**. His stable attributes include a **book** (his writings), and in some traditions a **ship**, recalling his crossing to England and a vision recorded by Eadmer. Other representations show him **instructing or admonishing a wrongdoer**, **exorcising a possessed monk**, or **with the Blessed Virgin appearing to him**. These attributes belong to the history of art and devotional convention; they are not eyewitness descriptions, and their presence in an image is not evidence for the historical episodes they evoke.

## 12. Prayers St. Anselm Wrote

**Status of these texts:** The following excerpts are from Anselm's own _Proslogion_. They are given in English translation; wording varies between the older translation of Sidney Norton Deane (1903) and the modern rendering of Sister Benedicta Ward, among others. They are presented as Anselm's own composition, not as liturgical texts of the Church.

### The opening of the _Proslogion_ (chapter 1)

     Come now, little man,
     turn aside for a while from your daily employment,
     escape for a moment from the tumult of your thoughts.
     Put aside your weighty cares,
     let your burdensome distractions wait,
     free yourself awhile for God
     and rest awhile in him.

### The principle of faith seeking understanding (_Proslogion_ 1)

     For I do not seek to understand that I may believe,
     but I believe in order to understand.
     For this also I believe:
     that unless I believed, I should not understand.

### The form of the argument (_Proslogion_ 2)

     We believe that you are something than which nothing greater can be thought.

### The petition of the seeker (_Proslogion_ 1)

     Teach me to seek you,
     and reveal yourself to me as I seek;
     for unless you teach me, I cannot seek you,
     and unless you reveal yourself, I cannot find you.

**Attribution caution:** The prayers and meditations circulating under Anselm's name in the _Orationes sive Meditationes_ are a mixed collection. Three prayers to the Blessed Virgin and several meditations are securely his; others were added by later compilers and are properly called **pseudo-Anselmian**. The _Meditatio Redemptionis Humanae_, often printed with his works, is generally regarded as the work of a disciple. A prayer found in a devotional anthology under his name should not be quoted as his without a primary-text check.

## 13. Prayers Asking for the Intercession of St. Anselm of Canterbury

> **Prayer status:** The prayers below are **devotional compositions written for this article**. They are not words written by St. Anselm, and they are not official liturgical collects. Asking a canonized saint to intercede is a request for prayer within the communion of the Church; it is entirely distinct from the worship owed to God alone.

St. Anselm spent his life on a single project: to let faith become understanding, and to let understanding return as adoration. He sought God as "that than which nothing greater can be thought," and found that the search itself was a prayer. Because he was a monk before he was a scholar, and a bishop before he was a controversialist, the faithful ask his intercession for the difficult work of thinking faithfully, for those whose faith and reason seem to be at war, for scholars and students, and for all who suffer for the freedom of the Church.

    O blessed Anselm, monk of Bec and Magnificent Doctor,
    you who would not seek to understand in order to believe,
    but believed in order to understand,
    obtain for us the grace of a faith that thinks
    and a thought that adores.

    You sought the God than whom nothing greater can be conceived,
    and found that the seeking was already a prayer;
    teach us to seek him with an honest mind
    and to rest in him with a humble heart.
    Where our reason is proud, humble it;
    where our faith is slack, quicken it;
    where we are tempted to set the two at war,
    reconcile them in the one truth who is God.

    You endured exile twice rather than surrender
    the freedom of the Church to the power of the world;
    strengthen all who are pressed to trade the things of God
    for the favour of the powerful,
    and keep the Church free to preach her Lord.

    St. Anselm, Magnificent Doctor, lover of wisdom and friend of God,
    pray for us now and at the hour of our death.
    Amen.

_A shorter invocation for daily use:_

    St. Anselm of Canterbury,
    teach us to believe that we may understand,
    and pray for us.

## 14. Patristic and Magisterial Witness

> "One of the most luminous figures in the tradition of the Church and in the history of Western European thought itself."
> — Pope Benedict XVI, Letter to Cardinal Giacomo Biffi, 15 April 2009

> "A monk with an intense spiritual life, an excellent teacher of the young, a theologian with an extraordinary capacity for speculation, a wise man of governance and an intransigent defender of _libertas Ecclesiae_ … Anselm is one of the eminent figures of the Middle Ages who was able to harmonize all these qualities, thanks to the profound mystical experience that always guided his thought and his action."
> — Pope Benedict XVI, General Audience, 23 September 2009

> "The believing intelligence … strives to appreciate and explore the intelligibility of the Word of God — _fides quaerens intellectum_ — and to offer a reasoned account of the truth of God."
> — International Theological Commission, _Theology Today: Perspectives, Principles and Criteria_ (2012), 59

> "Now reason, if it is enlightened by faith, does indeed when it seeks persistently, piously and soberly, achieve by God's gift some understanding, and that most profitable, of the mysteries, whether by analogy from what it knows naturally, or from the connection of these mysteries with one another and with the final end of humanity; but reason is never rendered capable of penetrating these mysteries in the way in which it penetrates those truths which form its proper object."
> — First Vatican Council, _Dei Filius_, chapter IV

> "I believe in order to understand."
> — St. Anselm, _Proslogion_ 1, cited as the Church's own posture of faith in John Paul II, _Fides et Ratio_ 16–17

## 15. Attribution Cautions

Several sayings and claims attached to Anselm in popular use require care.

| Common claim or quotation                                                  | Status and correct handling                                                                                                                                                                                                                     |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| "Theology is faith seeking understanding."                                 | A **paraphrase**, not a verbatim sentence. Anselm's Latin is _fides quaerens intellectum_, the original title of the _Proslogion_. Quote the Latin, or mark the English as a rendering.                                                         |
| "Anselm, father of Scholasticism."                                         | A **popular label**, not an ecclesial title. It compresses a real influence into a misleading membership (see §8.7). His formal epithet is _Doctor Magnificus_.                                                                                 |
| "Anselm was canonized by Alexander VI in 1494."                            | **Disputed.** The Bollandists read the 1494 letters as commissioning an inquiry into his life and miracles, not as a canonization; Becket's petition to Alexander III in 1163 did not result in canonization. State the cult, not a false date. |
| "Anselm proved that God exists, so unbelief is irrational."                | **Overstated.** The validity of the _Proslogion_ argument is a contested philosophical question on which the Church has defined nothing; Aquinas denied it is a demonstration for us, and Kant rejected its logic.                              |
| "Anselm invented the idea that Christ was punished in our place."          | **Incorrect.** His category is **satisfaction** of offended honour, not the infliction of punishment on a substitute. Penal substitution is a later Reformed development with different juridical premises.                                     |
| Prayers printed under his name in devotional books.                        | The _Orationes sive Meditationes_ is a **mixed collection** containing pseudo-Anselmian material, and the _Meditatio Redemptionis Humanae_ is probably not his. Verify against a critical edition before quoting.                               |
| "Anselm taught that reason alone can establish the whole of Christianity." | **Half true, and easily misread.** His argument _remoto Christo_ is a thought-experiment showing the fittingness of the Incarnation, set inside a confession of faith; it does not claim that reason replaces revelation.                       |
