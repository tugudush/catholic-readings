# St. Longinus: The Soldier Who Pierced Christ's Side

> **Note:** This document was composed with the assistance of Magisterium AI. Citations should be verified against primary sources where possible.

---

## 1. Introduction

**St. Longinus** (1st c. A.D.) is the traditional name given to the Roman soldier who, standing guard at the foot of the Cross, **pierced the side of our crucified Lord with a lance** (John 19:34), from which flowed blood and water — a moment the Church has always understood as signifying the birth of the sacraments of Baptism and the Eucharist from the pierced Heart of Christ. He is venerated as a **martyr** who converted upon witnessing the cosmic signs at the death of Jesus and, according to tradition, was healed of failing eyesight when the Precious Blood touched his eyes. His feast day is commemorated on **15 March** in the Roman Martyrology (associated with Caesarea in Cappadocia) and **16 October** in certain calendars.

The Roman Martyrology (15 March) records:

> "At Caesarea, in Cappadocia, the martyrdom of St. Longinus, the soldier who is said to have pierced our Lord's side with a lance."[^6]

> "He opened His side with a spear, and at once there came out blood and water."
> — John 19:34

## 2. The Scriptural Account

The only biblical witness to the piercing of Christ's side is the **Gospel of John**:

> "But one of the soldiers pierced his side with a spear, and at once there came out blood and water. He who saw it has borne witness — his testimony is true, and he knows that he tells the truth — that you also may believe." (John 19:34–35)

Several points are significant in the sacred text:

- **The soldier is unnamed.** The Evangelist does not provide his name, rank, or origin. The tradition of calling him "Longinus" developed in later centuries.
- **John emphasizes eyewitness testimony.** The solemn affirmation ("his testimony is true") indicates that the Evangelist regarded this event as theologically momentous, a direct fulfillment of Zechariah 12:10: "They shall look on him whom they have pierced."
- **Blood and water** — the Church Fathers unanimously saw in this double stream a sign of the two great sacraments: **Baptism** (water) and the **Eucharist** (blood), both born from the side of the New Adam as Eve was drawn from the side of the first Adam.

The soldier who performed this act is distinct from the **centurion** mentioned in Mark 15:39 ("Truly this man was the Son of God!"), though later tradition sometimes conflates the two figures. The centurion witnessed the earthquake and darkness and confessed Christ's divinity; the soldier with the lance performed the physical act of piercing — but according to the tradition that developed, both realizations belong to the same man, Longinus.[^1]

## 3. The Name "Longinus" — Historical Origins

### The Linguistic Puzzle

The name _Longinus_ (Greek: Λογγῖνος, _Longinos_) most likely derives from the Greek word λόγχη (_lonchē_), meaning "lance" or "spear." This suggests that the name was originally not a personal name at all, but a descriptive title — "the lance-bearer" — which later tradition transformed into a proper name.[^3]

### Earliest Documentary Evidence

The earliest known visual representation of Longinus appears in the **Rabbula Gospels**, a Syriac illuminated manuscript dated to **586 A.D.**, now in the Laurentian Library in Florence. In the crucifixion miniature, the soldier thrusting the lance into Christ's side has the name **LOGINOS** (ΛΟΓΙΝΟΣ) written above his head in Greek characters. Whether this inscription is original to the 586 manuscript or a later addition remains debated, but it demonstrates that the Longinus tradition was established by at least the sixth century.[^3]

### The Apocryphal Gospel of Nicodemus

The apocryphal _Acts of Pilate_ (also called the _Gospel of Nicodemus_), in its later recensions, gives the name **Longinus** to the soldier who pierces Christ's side. This work, though not canonical Scripture, was widely read in the early Church and helped cement the name in Christian imagination.[^1]

### The Pilgrim Antoninus of Piacenza (c. 570)

The pilgrim St. Antoninus of Piacenza, visiting the holy places of Jerusalem around 570 A.D., records seeing in the basilica of Mount Sion "the crown of thorns with which Our Lord was crowned and **the lance with which He was struck in the side**." This is the earliest explicit mention of the lance as a venerated relic.[^3]

## 4. The Conversion and the Miracle of Sight

The most beloved element of the Longinus tradition concerns his **conversion** — and particularly the healing of his eyes.

### The Two Catalysts

According to the _Golden Legend_ of Blessed James of Voragine (c. 1260), which provides the most complete medieval biography of the saint, Longinus was moved to faith by two experiences at the crucifixion:[^2]

1. **The cosmic portents**: He witnessed the darkening of the sun (Mark 15:33), the earthquake (Matthew 27:51), and the rending of the Temple veil — signs that proclaimed the death of no ordinary man.
2. **The healing of his sight**: But what "influenced him most," the _Golden Legend_ relates, was that his eyesight had been failing — whether through age or infirmity. When he thrust the lance into Christ's side, **the blood of the Saviour ran down the lance and touched his eyes, and immediately he saw clearly**.[^2]

This story gave rise to St. Longinus's traditional patronage of those with eye ailments and diseases of sight. The spiritual symbolism is profound: the man who pierced the Lord's side was the first to see — both physically and spiritually — the truth that "blood and water" proclaim.

### The Confession

Seeing the portents and healed of his blindness, Longinus **believed in Christ**. The centurion's confession in Mark 15:39 — "Truly this man was the Son of God!" — became, in the developed tradition, Longinus's own cry of faith.

## 5. After the Crucifixion — Apostolic Instruction and Monastic Life

The _Golden Legend_ narrates that after his conversion, Longinus:

- **Renounced his military service**, abandoning the soldier's life that had brought him to the foot of the Cross.
- **Was instructed by the Apostles**, receiving the fullness of Christian teaching from those who had walked with the Lord.
- **Embraced a monastic life** in **Caesarea of Cappadocia** (in modern-day Turkey), where by his words and example he "won many souls to Christ."[^2]

This Cappadocian association is significant. Caesarea was a major centre of early Christianity — the see of St. Basil the Great in the following centuries — and the region produced some of the Church's greatest Fathers. That tradition places Longinus there suggests an early and deep-rooted local cult.

The Roman Martyrology likewise associates his martyrdom with Caesarea in Cappadocia, confirming the Cappadocian locus of his veneration.[^6]

---

## 6. The Martyrdom

The _Golden Legend_ provides a dramatic, detailed account of Longinus's passion. When he was brought to trial for refusing to offer sacrifice to the Roman gods, the governor ordered a series of tortures:[^2]

### The Tortures

- **His teeth were knocked out** and **his tongue was cut off** — mutilations designed to silence a preacher who had won many converts.
- Yet, miraculously, **Longinus did not lose his power of speech**. He continued to confess Christ aloud.

### The Idols Destroyed

Seizing an axe, Longinus **broke the pagan idols into fragments**, crying out:

> "Now we shall see whether they are gods!"

At that moment, **a pack of demons issued forth from the shattered idols** and entered into the governor and his attendants. Gibbering and howling, they fell at Longinus's feet.

Longinus asked the demons: "Why do you take up your abode in idols?" They answered:

> "Where the name of Christ is not heard and the sign of His cross is not imposed, there is our dwelling-place."[^2]

### The Governor's Blindness and Healing

The governor continued to rave and was now **struck blind**. Longinus told him:

> "Know that you can only be cured when you have put me to death. But as soon as I shall have surrendered my life by your act, I will pray for you, and I will obtain for you health both of body and of soul."[^2]

### The Beheading

The governor immediately ordered Longinus to be **beheaded**. As soon as the sentence was carried out, the governor was healed — and, according to the tradition, he too believed and honoured the martyr.[^2]

This narrative, while clearly belonging to the genre of hagiographical romance, conveys genuine spiritual truths: the power of the Name of Christ over demons, the witness of the martyr that wins over even his persecutor, and the paradox that death is the gateway to healing.

## 7. The Mantua Tradition

A distinct and notable medieval tradition claims that Longinus did not remain in Cappadocia but travelled to the West:

- According to this legend, Longinus came to **Mantua** (in northern Italy) shortly after Jesus' death, **preached there for some years**, and ultimately **suffered martyrdom** in that city.[^1]
- He is said to have brought with him a **portion of the Precious Blood shed upon the Cross** — a relic still preserved and venerated at Mantua.
- The **body of the saint** is also claimed to rest there.[^1]

This Mantua tradition competes with the Cappadocian tradition, and modern scholarship regards both as legendary rather than historical. Nevertheless, the Mantua cult generated a substantial body of literature and art, and the relic of the Precious Blood remains an object of local devotion.

## 10. In Art and Iconography

### Traditional Attributes

St. Longinus is typically depicted with:

- A **lance or spear** — his primary attribute, the instrument both of the piercing of Christ's side and of the miracle of his healing
- **Military attire** — Roman soldier's garb, sometimes with the armour of a centurion
- His **hand raised to his eyes** or his eyes directed toward the Cross — reflecting the healing of his sight
- Sometimes shown **mounted on horseback** at the foot of the Cross

### The Rabbula Gospels (586 A.D.)

The earliest known artistic representation of Longinus is in the crucifixion miniature of the Syriac Rabbula Gospels (Laurentian Library, Florence). The soldier piercing Christ's side is labelled **LOGINOS** in Greek, making this the earliest iconographic witness to the tradition.[^3]

### Later Western Art

Longinus appears in countless crucifixion scenes throughout Western art history, including works by **Fra Angelico**, **Matthias Grünewald** (the Isenheim Altarpiece), and **Peter Paul Rubens**. In Baroque art, he is often shown on horseback, dramatically gesturing toward the crucified Christ — the moment of piercing frozen in time as the moment of conversion.

### Eastern Iconography

In Eastern Orthodox and Eastern Catholic iconography, St. Longinus is honoured as a **martyr** and is often shown holding a **cross-topped lance**. Icons of the Crucifixion in the Byzantine tradition routinely include the soldier with the lance as a standard element.

## 11. The Holy Lance — Relic and Legend

The weapon with which Longinus pierced Christ's side became one of the most venerated and controversial relics in Christendom. Known as the **Holy Lance** (Latin: _Lancea Sacra_) or the **Spear of Destiny** (German: _Heilige Lanze_), its history is treated in full separately at [church-history/holy-lance-relic.md](../church-history/holy-lance-relic.md). Key highlights include:

- **Jerusalem (6th century)**: The lance was venerated at the church of the Holy Sepulchre, attested by Cassiodorus and Gregory of Tours.[^3]
- **Constantinople (7th century)**: After the Persian capture of Jerusalem in 615, the broken-off point of the lance was taken to Constantinople and deposited in the church of Hagia Sophia.[^3]
- **Paris (13th century)**: The lance point was given by Baldwin II to King St. Louis IX of France and enshrined in the Sainte-Chapelle alongside the Crown of Thorns. It disappeared during the French Revolution.[^3]
- **Vienna (20th century)**: The Vienna Lance, housed in the Hofburg Palace treasury, was seized by Adolf Hitler in 1938 and moved to Nuremberg. It was recovered by U.S. forces under Lt. Walter Horn in 1945 and returned to Austria. Scientific testing in the 20th century dated this particular lance to the **7th or 8th century**, suggesting it likely belonged to **Charlemagne** rather than a 1st-century Roman soldier.

For the full history, including Hitler's obsession, General Patton's fascination, the dramatic WWII recovery, and the metallurgical investigation, see [church-history/holy-lance-relic.md](../church-history/holy-lance-relic.md).

## 13. Prayer for Intercession

St. Longinus, Roman soldier who stood at the foot of the Cross, thrust the lance into the Saviour's side, and was healed of blindness by the Precious Blood that flowed down the shaft, the faithful seek his intercession for clarity of spiritual sight, for healing of diseases of the eye, for soldiers facing the trials of military life, and for the grace to recognize Christ in the midst of suffering. The following prayer may be used to seek his intercession:

    O glorious St. Longinus, soldier and martyr,
    you who stood guard at the crucifixion of the Lord
    and, in the very act of piercing His sacred side,
    received the healing of your failing sight
    through the blood and water that flowed from His Heart —
    obtain for us the grace to see clearly
    with the eyes of faith.

    You who abandoned the soldier's life
    to follow the King you had pierced,
    intercede for all who serve in the armed forces,
    that they may serve with honour and justice,
    and that in the darkness of conflict
    they may recognize the face of Christ.

    Courageous martyr who shattered idols with an axe
    and confessed the Name of Jesus before the governor,
    teach us to destroy every false god in our own hearts
    and to bear witness without fear.

    St. Longinus, healed by the Precious Blood,
    pray for us now and at the hour of our death.
    Amen.

_A shorter invocation for daily use:_

    St. Longinus, soldier of the Cross,
    pray for us.
