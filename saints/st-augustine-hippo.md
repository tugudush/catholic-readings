# St. Augustine of Hippo: Doctor of Grace

> **Source note:** This biography distinguishes Augustine's own testimony, contemporary historical evidence, later tradition, Church teaching, and modern devotional composition. References to Augustine's works use book, chapter, and section numbers where available; English wording varies by translation.

---

## 1. Introduction and Identity

**St. Augustine of Hippo** (Latin: _Aurelius Augustinus Hipponensis_; 13 November 354 - 28 August 430 A.D.) was a bishop, theologian, philosopher, preacher, and Christian writer of Roman North Africa. He served as Bishop of Hippo Regius, near modern Annaba in Algeria, from approximately 395/396 until his death. The Church venerates him as a **Father and Doctor of the Church**, traditionally calling him the **Doctor of Grace**.

Augustine's importance is not limited to the number of his books. His life joins several histories: the conversion of a brilliant but divided pagan-era intellectual, the growth of Latin Christianity in Africa, the formation of Catholic teaching about grace and the Church, the development of Christian biblical interpretation, and the pastoral work of a bishop who preached, corresponded, arbitrated disputes, trained clergy, and cared for the poor. Pope Benedict XVI called him the greatest Father of the Latin Church and emphasized that his influence reached both Christian theology and the cultural history of the West ([General Audience, 9 January 2008](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/be11462a-ad4d-490a-97a1-4d1925b12ae0/ref/page1)).

The governing image of Augustine's spirituality is the restless heart:

> "You have made us for yourself, O Lord, and our heart is restless until it rests in you."
>
> - St. Augustine, _Confessions_, I.1.1

This is not a claim that every desire is automatically holy. Augustine's teaching is that the human person is made for God, and that created goods become disordered when they are loved apart from the supreme Good (_Confessions_, I.1; _City of God_, XIX.1-4). His conversion did not end the need for conversion; it began a lifelong movement of grace, study, prayer, sacramental life, and pastoral service.

## 2. Early Life and the Search for Wisdom

### Birth and family

Augustine was born on **13 November 354** at **Thagaste** (modern Souk Ahras, Algeria), a Roman town in the province of Numidia. His father, **Patricius**, was not yet a Christian during Augustine's childhood and was baptized only near the end of his life. His mother, **St. Monica**, was a committed Catholic Christian. Augustine's own account presents her not as a sentimental background figure but as a persistent woman of prayer, prudence, and practical courage (_Confessions_, III.11-12; IX.8-13).

Augustine was marked as a catechumen in childhood, but he was not baptized then. When he became seriously ill, Monica sought baptism for him; when the danger passed, the baptism was postponed. Augustine later reflected that the postponement was connected with the penitential discipline of the ancient Church and his mother's fear that post-baptismal sin would be treated with less seriousness (_Confessions_, I.11). This early sacramental history helps explain why his later theology took sin, conversion, and grace with such gravity.

### Education at Thagaste, Madaura, and Carthage

Augustine studied grammar and rhetoric in the schools of **Thagaste**, **Madaura**, and **Carthage**, not at a modern university. His education gave him mastery of Latin language, public speaking, classical literature, and the techniques of argument. It also exposed him to the discipline and humiliations of late Roman schooling. In _Confessions_ I.14-16, he remembers both his hatred of forced study and the providence by which God used that education for a greater purpose.

Financial difficulties interrupted his studies for a period after his father's death, but his patronage and family support enabled him to continue. At Carthage, the young rhetorician pursued intellectual distinction while also entering into a long relationship with an unnamed woman. They had a son, **Adeodatus** ("gift of God"), born in 372. Augustine speaks of this relationship with honesty in _Confessions_ IV.2 and VI.15, neither glamorizing his sin nor denying the genuine affection involved.

### The pear theft

One of Augustine's most memorable stories is not a triumph but a petty crime. As a teenager, he and a group of companions stole pears from a neighboring tree, not because they were hungry but because the act itself was forbidden and the group enjoyed doing wrong together. They threw the pears to pigs and kept almost none of the fruit (_Confessions_, II.4-9).

Augustine later used the incident as an examination of disordered desire. The story illustrates his conviction that sin is not merely the pursuit of a useful object; it can be a will that turns away from the good because it wants to make itself the measure of the good. He also recognized the social dimension of sin: companionship can either strengthen virtue or make wrongdoing appear attractive.

### Cicero and the awakening of philosophical desire

At about eighteen, Augustine read Cicero's _Hortensius_, a philosophical exhortation now lost except for fragments. The book awakened in him a love of wisdom and a desire for truth that he later identified as a decisive turning point (_Confessions_, III.4). He was disappointed that the book did not name Christ, but its call to seek wisdom prepared him to ask more serious questions about happiness, evil, the soul, and God.

### Manichaeism, astrology, and skepticism

Augustine became a **Manichaean hearer** for roughly nine years, from about 373 to 382/383. Manichaeism promised a rational explanation of evil through a cosmic conflict between principles of light and darkness. Augustine was attracted by its confidence, its criticism of Catholic biblical interpretation, and its promise to answer difficult questions without requiring simple faith. He eventually found that its leaders could not satisfy his questions about astronomy, moral responsibility, and the nature of God (_Confessions_, III.6-7; V.3-10).

His meeting with the celebrated Manichaean teacher **Faustus** was especially disappointing. Augustine admired Faustus's reputation but found that Faustus could not answer the technical and philosophical questions Augustine brought to him (_Confessions_, V.3-7). Augustine also pursued astrology for a time, then rejected its fatalism after examining its claims and hearing the arguments of the physician Vindicianus.

After leaving Manichaeism, Augustine passed through a period of Academic skepticism. This was not indifference to truth but a temporary refusal to assent to claims he considered unproved. His later dialogue _Against the Academics_ shows how seriously he continued to engage the question of whether truth can be known.

## 3. Conversion in Milan

### Rome and Milan

In 383 Augustine left North Africa for **Rome**, hoping to find students who would respect a rhetoric teacher and pay reliably. He soon moved to **Milan**, where in 384 he obtained the public position of professor of rhetoric. The appointment brought prestige, but it did not settle his interior life. The successful teacher remained divided between his intellectual desire for truth and his attachment to ambition, sexual disorder, and social advancement.

### Monica's perseverance

Monica followed Augustine to Italy. In _Confessions_ III.11-12, Augustine records that she received a symbolic assurance from a Catholic bishop who told her, in substance, that a son of so many tears could not perish. The passage is Augustine's recollection of his mother's consolation, not a license to treat every private prediction as certain prophecy. Monica's perseverance became one of the great stories of Christian maternal intercession, while Augustine's conversion remained his own response to grace.

### St. Ambrose and Catholic exegesis

Augustine began attending the preaching of **St. Ambrose**, Bishop of Milan, partly to study his rhetoric. He soon discovered that Ambrose's sermons were doing more than display eloquence. Ambrose's spiritual and allegorical reading of difficult Old Testament passages addressed Augustine's objections to the Catholic faith (_Confessions_, V.13-14; VI.3-4).

This encounter changed Augustine's idea of Christian teaching. He had assumed that Catholic Christianity could not withstand philosophical scrutiny; Ambrose showed him that Christian Scripture had intellectual depth and that the literal sense was not always exhausted by a crude surface reading. The Christian faith did not ask him to abandon reason but to purify the way he used it.

### Neoplatonism and the humility of the Incarnation

Around 386 Augustine read Latin translations of works associated with Plotinus and other Neoplatonist authors. These books helped him understand God as immaterial, immutable, and the source of all being. They also helped him understand evil as a privation or disorder of the good rather than a substance equal to God.

Yet Augustine later judged that Neoplatonism did not give him the humility and healing he needed. The philosophers helped him glimpse the Word, but they did not lead him to the Word made flesh in the humility of Jesus Christ (_Confessions_, VII.9-21). Reading St. Paul's letters then united philosophical insight to the grace of Christ, the cross, and the transformation of the will.

### The struggle for continence

Augustine's conversion was preceded by a long moral struggle. He prayed for chastity, but not yet immediately: "Give me chastity and continence, but not yet" (_Confessions_, VIII.7.17). He describes himself as torn between the call of truth and the habits he had cultivated. The conflict was not solved by self-confidence. It ended when he learned to ask God for the grace to obey what God commands (_Confessions_, VIII.11-12; X.29).

### The garden and Romans 13

In August 386, while in a garden near Milan, Augustine heard what he understood as a child's voice repeating, **"Tolle, lege! Tolle, lege!"** ("Take up and read! Take up and read!"). He opened Paul's Letter to the Romans and read Romans 13:13-14:

> "Let us live honourably as in the day, not in revelry and drunkenness, not in debauchery and licentiousness, not in quarreling and jealousy. Instead, put on the Lord Jesus Christ, and make no provision for the flesh, to gratify its desires."

The passage answered the precise division of his will. Augustine wrote that he needed no further reading at that moment: "instantly at the end of this sentence, a light as it were of serenity infused into my heart, all the darkness of doubt vanished away" (_Confessions_, VIII.12.29). The episode is not a magical technique for selecting Bible verses at random. It is Augustine's account of Scripture becoming the instrument through which grace brought a long-prepared will to decision.

### Baptism and the Milanese Easter

Augustine was baptized by St. Ambrose at the Easter Vigil of **24-25 April 387** in Milan, together with Adeodatus and his friend **Alypius** (_Confessions_, IX.6). The baptism was the sacramental beginning of a new Christian life, not simply the public conclusion of an intellectual argument.

### Monica at Ostia

After baptism, Augustine and Monica prepared to return to Africa. At Ostia, they shared a profound conversation about eternal life. Augustine describes them as rising through created things and, for a brief moment, touching divine Wisdom "by the total concentration of our hearts" (_Confessions_, IX.10.23-26). The experience did not make them escape the world; it deepened their hope for the life to come.

Monica died at Ostia in 387. Augustine's mourning is both intimate and theological: he admits his tears, remembers her virtues, and asks God to forgive her through Christ (_Confessions_, IX.11-13). His prayer for his mother is one of the earliest and most moving Christian literary examples of intercession for the dead.

## 4. Priesthood and the Episcopate of Hippo

### A community of prayer and study

After Monica's death, Augustine returned to North Africa. He spent time at Thagaste, where he formed a community devoted to prayer, study, and common Christian life. His intention was not to establish a private philosophical club but to seek God with friends under the discipline of the Gospel. The early community became the seed of the Augustinian tradition.

### Ordination as a priest

In 391, while visiting Hippo Regius, Augustine was unexpectedly chosen and ordained a priest by Bishop **Valerius**. Augustine had hoped to live a secluded life of study, but he accepted ordination as a call to serve the local Church. Valerius gave him space to establish a monastery for clergy, and Augustine began preaching and writing while preparing for episcopal ministry.

The change from lay intellectual to priestly pastor was decisive. Augustine's thought was never again merely private speculation. Scripture, doctrine, preaching, the sacraments, discipline, the poor, widows, religious communities, and the unity of the Church became the concrete setting of his theology.

### Bishop and shepherd

Augustine became coadjutor to Valerius around 395 and succeeded him as Bishop of Hippo around 396. He served the see for approximately thirty-four years. He preached frequently, sometimes daily, heard cases and correspondence, attended councils, trained clergy, founded and governed communities, and continued an extraordinary literary output.

Possidius, Augustine's friend and biographer, portrays a bishop who used Church resources for mercy. During a crisis, Augustine permitted sacred vessels to be sold or exchanged to ransom captives. The story, preserved in the _Life of Augustine_, ch. 24, shows how Augustine understood ecclesial property: it exists for worship, but worship itself cannot be separated from charity toward persons in grave need.

Augustine also insisted that a bishop must be willing to be corrected. His late work _Retractations_ reviewed his earlier books, identified passages that needed qualification, and corrected errors of memory or argument. This habit of revising his own work is a model of intellectual humility, not a sign that his whole teaching was uncertain.

## 6. Pastoral Theology and Controversies

### Manichaeism and the goodness of creation

Against Manichaean dualism, Augustine defended the goodness of creation and taught that evil has no independent substance. Evil is a privation, a wound or disorder in a good created nature. This account enabled him to affirm both God's omnipotence and the reality of human moral responsibility (_Confessions_, VII.12-16; _City of God_, XI.9-22).

### Donatism and the unity of the Church

The Donatist controversy in North Africa concerned the holiness and unity of the Church, especially the validity of sacraments celebrated by clergy accused of betrayal during persecution. Augustine argued that Christ is the true source of sacramental grace and that the Church's unity cannot be reduced to the moral perfection of one faction (_On Baptism, Against the Donatists_; _Letter_ 93 to Vincentius).

The later scholastic formula _ex opere operato_ is not simply a slogan Augustine used in that exact form. It expresses a principle already visible in his sacramental theology: the efficacy of a sacrament comes from Christ and his promise, not from the personal holiness of the minister. This must not be used to excuse an unworthy minister's sin; it protects the faithful from believing that Christ's gifts fail whenever a human minister fails.

### Pelagianism and the primacy of grace

The Pelagian controversy concerned original sin, the wounded human will, the necessity of grace, infant baptism, and the relation between divine grace and human freedom. Augustine opposed the idea that a person could attain salvation by unaided moral effort. He insisted that even the beginning of faith is a gift of grace, while also defending the reality of responsible human consent (_On Nature and Grace_; _On Grace and Free Will_; _On Rebuke and Grace_).

The African bishops' teaching against Pelagianism was expressed in councils, including the Council of Carthage in 418, and was received through subsequent papal interventions. Augustine's technical language about predestination should be read in the context of these controversies. Later theological schools have debated how best to explain his formulations; his particular arguments should not be confused with the whole of Catholic doctrine or with every later Augustinian interpretation.

### Faith and reason

Augustine did not treat faith and reason as enemies. He used the formulas **_crede ut intelligas_** (believe in order to understand) and **_intellige ut credas_** (understand in order to believe) to describe their mutual service (_Sermon_ 43.9). Pope Benedict XVI summarized the same Augustinian balance by calling faith and reason "the two forces that lead us to knowledge" ([General Audience, 30 January 2008](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/f9731769-c62a-4e01-b6a1-e911147f31a7/ref/page1)).

### Scripture, charity, and the Church

Augustine's rule for interpretation is profoundly pastoral: a reading of Scripture that does not build up the double love of God and neighbour has missed the purpose of Christian understanding (_On Christian Doctrine_, I.39). His theology of the Church, sacraments, grace, and charity is therefore not detached from Christian life. Doctrine should lead to worship, conversion, mercy, and communion.

### How Augustine's books should be read

The _Confessions_ is not merely a modern autobiography. It is an address to God in which Augustine interprets his life as a history of grace. _The City of God_ is not merely a political manifesto, and it does not claim that the earthly city can be replaced by a perfect Christian empire. _On the Trinity_ is not a psychological reduction of God to the human mind. Each work uses human experience, history, and argument as limited instruments for contemplating divine revelation.

## 8. Letters, Sermons, and the Rule of Life

More than three hundred letters connected with Augustine survive, including letters he wrote and letters addressed to him. They reveal a bishop answering real people rather than an author writing only for later scholars: widows, monks and nuns, bishops, civil officials, soldiers, theologians, catechumens, and ordinary Christians. His letters are therefore essential sources for the pastoral and social Augustine.

| Letter       | Recipient                        | Theme and significance                                                                                                                            |
| ------------ | -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| _Letter_ 21  | Valerius                         | Augustine's early relationship with the Bishop of Hippo and the demands of priestly service.                                                      |
| _Letter_ 54  | Januarius                        | The relationship between universal Christian faith, local customs, fasting practices, and ecclesial unity.                                        |
| _Letter_ 82  | Jerome                           | Biblical interpretation, translation, the authority of Scripture, and the humility required in scholarly disagreement.                            |
| _Letter_ 93  | Vincentius                       | The Donatist controversy, pastoral correction, and the difficult question of how the Church should seek the return of those separated from her.   |
| _Letter_ 130 | Proba                            | The nature of Christian prayer, the Lord's Prayer, humility, perseverance, and the danger of praying for merely earthly success.                  |
| _Letter_ 166 | Jerome                           | Questions concerning the origin of the soul and the limits of what can be asserted without certainty.                                             |
| _Letter_ 189 | Boniface                         | Christian responsibility in public office, the legitimate defense of peace, and the moral limits of warfare.                                      |
| _Letter_ 211 | The community of religious women | Common life, obedience, correction, charity, possessions, and the spiritual principles associated with the _Praeceptum_ or Rule of St. Augustine. |
| _Letter_ 231 | Darius                           | Augustine's request that readers pray for him and that his books lead people toward Christian perfection rather than admiration of the author.    |

The document commonly called the **Rule of St. Augustine** is closely related to the _Praeceptum_ and to the tradition surrounding _Letter_ 211. Augustine did not found the medieval Order of St. Augustine in its later institutional form, but his vision of common prayer, common possessions, correction in charity, and unity of heart became the rule of life for many communities.

Augustine's preaching survives in hundreds of sermons and homilies. The _Expositions of the Psalms_ and _Tractates on the Gospel of John_ show him moving repeatedly from Scripture to the concrete life of the congregation. In _Sermon_ 272, preached to the newly baptized during the Easter season, he teaches the Eucharistic identity of the Church: those who receive the mystery are called to become what they receive as members of Christ's Body. The sermon is a classic example of Augustine's sacramental and ecclesial preaching.

## 9. Death, Relics, and Legacy

### The Vandal siege

In 429-430, the Vandals under King **Genseric** invaded North Africa and besieged Hippo Regius. The Vandals were associated with Arian Christianity, which denied the Catholic confession of the full divinity of the Son. Augustine's final months unfolded amid war, displacement, illness, and uncertainty about the future of the city and the African Church.

### Augustine's final illness

Possidius records that Augustine asked for the penitential psalms to be written where he could see them during his illness. He withdrew for prayer and tears, using the Psalms as the Church's language of repentance and hope (_Life of Augustine_, ch. 31). This final scene echoes the whole of _Confessions_: the bishop who had spent his life teaching others to pray returned to the mercy of God with the words of Scripture.

Augustine died on **28 August 430**, at about seventy-five years of age, while Hippo was still under siege. He was buried at Hippo. His relics were later transferred from North Africa to Sardinia and eventually to **Pavia, Italy**, where they are venerated in the Basilica of San Pietro in Ciel d'Oro. Pope John Paul II prayed before them in 2004, and Pope Benedict XVI visited the basilica in 2007.

### The enduring legacy

Augustine shaped Latin Christian theology on grace, sin, the Church, the sacraments, Scripture, the Trinity, history, and the interior life. His influence extends through the Middle Ages, the Reformation-era debates, Catholic theology, Christian philosophy, spiritual autobiography, biblical preaching, and the religious communities that follow his rule.

His authority is that of a Father and Doctor, not a substitute for the Magisterium. Augustine's writings are read within the Church's living tradition, alongside Scripture, the councils, papal teaching, and the later development of doctrine. His own _Retractations_ teaches readers to distinguish a genuine search for truth from attachment to one's first formulation.

## 12. The Augustinian Family

Augustine did not establish the medieval religious orders that later bore his name, but his common life and rule became a foundational pattern for many communities, including:

- The **Order of St. Augustine** (Augustinians, O.S.A.).
- The **Canons Regular of St. Augustine**.
- The **Order of Preachers** (Dominicans, O.P.), which follows the Rule of St. Augustine.
- The **Premonstratensians**, **Trinitarians**, and other communities whose canonical histories use the Augustinian rule or tradition.
- The **Augustinian Recollects** (O.A.R.) and other reform branches of the Augustinian family.

The shared Augustinian ideal is a unity of heart in God: common prayer, common life, charity, humility, study, and service of the Church. Its canonical expression differs from one institute to another.

## 13. Prayers Augustine Himself Wrote

The following passages are authentic prayer material from Augustine's own works. They are not all formal liturgical prayers, and their wording differs among English translations. A prayer addressed to Augustine is different: it asks a glorified member of the Church to intercede with God; it is not worship of Augustine.

### The opening prayer of the _Confessions_

In _Confessions_ I.1.1, Augustine begins his autobiography by praising God and asking how a person can seek the One who is already the source of every good desire:

    Great are you, O Lord, and greatly to be praised;
    great is your power,
    and your wisdom is without measure.

    You have made us for yourself,
    and our heart is restless until it rests in you.

### The prayer of the _Soliloquies_

In _Soliloquies_ I.2, Augustine asks God to teach him how to pray and to free him. The passage belongs to an early dialogue written soon after his conversion:

    O God, Framer of the universe,
    grant me first rightly to invoke you;
    then to show myself worthy to be heard by you;
    lastly, deign to set me free.

The surrounding prayer invokes God as the Father of truth, wisdom, blessedness, intelligible light, awakening, and illumination.

### "Let me know myself; let me know you"

In _Soliloquies_ II.1, Augustine expresses the purpose of his interior search in a brief prayer:

    God, always the same,
    let me know myself,
    let me know you.

This is not a call to self-absorption. Augustine seeks self-knowledge in order to recognize dependence on God and to return to the Truth who heals the soul.

### "Late have I loved you"

The famous prayer in _Confessions_ X.27.38 is an act of repentance and wonder:

    Late have I loved you,
    O Beauty ever ancient, ever new;
    late have I loved you!

    You were within,
    and I was in the external world and sought you there.

    You touched me,
    and I am on fire for your peace.

### "Give what you command"

In _Confessions_ X.29.40, Augustine turns his account of temptation into a direct prayer for grace:

    You command continence;
    give what you command,
    and then command what you will.

This prayer does not remove human responsibility. It expresses Augustine's central conviction that the grace by which God commands also heals and strengthens the will to obey.

### Prayer for Monica

In _Confessions_ IX.13.33-34, Augustine prays for his deceased mother. He asks God not to judge her apart from divine mercy in Christ:

    Forgive her, O Lord, forgive her, I beseech you;
    enter not into judgment with her.

    Hearken unto me,
    through that Medicine of our wounds
    who hung upon the tree.

He then remembers the prayerful conversation at Ostia and entrusts Monica to God's mercy. This is authentic Augustinian prayer and an important witness to Christian intercession for the dead.

### Augustine asking others to pray for him

Augustine did not present himself as spiritually self-sufficient. In _Letter_ 231.6, he asked Darius and other readers to pray for him:

    When, however, you find me in these books,
    pray for me that I may not fail,
    but be perfected.
    Pray, my son; pray.

### Attribution cautions

- The popular **"Prayer Before Study"** beginning "Creator ineffable, who from the treasures of your wisdom" is traditionally associated with **St. Thomas Aquinas**, not Augustine. It should not be presented as an Augustinian prayer.
- **"Breathe in me, O Holy Spirit"** is widely attributed to Augustine, but its attribution lacks secure primary-text evidence. It may be used devotionally if labeled **traditionally attributed to St. Augustine**, not as a certain quotation.
- The phrase **"Take and read"** belongs to Augustine's conversion story in _Confessions_ VIII.12. It is not itself a prayer composed by him.

## 14. Prayers Asking for the Intercession of St. Augustine

> **Prayer status:** The prayers in this section are modern devotional compositions unless otherwise identified. They ask St. Augustine's intercession and do not attribute these words to him.

The prayers in this section are modern devotional compositions unless otherwise identified. They ask St. Augustine to pray with and for the faithful; they do not attribute these words to him.

### Prayer for conversion, truth, and purity

    O God of mercy and truth,
    you drew St. Augustine from error,
    healed his divided heart,
    and made him a servant of the Gospel.

    Through his intercession,
    lead us from whatever keeps us far from you.
    Give light to those who seek the truth,
    strength to those who struggle with sin,
    and humility to those who study your mysteries.

    St. Augustine,
    son of Monica and Bishop of Hippo,
    pray for those who have wandered from the Father's house.
    Obtain for us the grace to receive the sacraments faithfully,
    to love Christ's Church,
    and to persevere in the truth with charity.

    Teach us to seek God above every created good,
    to confess our sins without despair,
    and to trust the grace that transforms the will.
    Pray for us now and at the hour of our death.
    Amen.

### Prayer for students, teachers, writers, and pastors

    Glorious St. Augustine,
    lover of wisdom and faithful shepherd,
    pray for students who seek understanding,
    teachers who serve the truth,
    writers who desire integrity,
    and pastors who bear the burdens of God's people.

    Ask the Lord to purify our intellects from pride,
    our speech from vanity,
    and our work from the desire for human praise.
    Help us to join study with prayer,
    knowledge with charity,
    and conviction with patience.

    May our restless hearts find their rest in God,
    and may everything we learn lead us to love him more.
    St. Augustine of Hippo,
    pray for us.
    Amen.

### Papal prayer before Augustine's relics

On 11 November 2004, Pope John Paul II prayed before Augustine's relics. The prayer is not Augustine's own, but it is a useful modern example of asking his intercession:

    O great Augustine, our father and teacher,
    teach us to read history in the light of divine Providence.
    Guide us toward the inner person
    in whom the One who alone can restore peace
    awaits the restless heart.

    Obtain for us a burning love for the Church,
    the Catholic mother of the Saints,
    and lead us with all the blessed
    to the glory of the heavenly homeland.
    Amen.

The complete prayer is preserved in [Pope John Paul II's prayer before the relics of St. Augustine](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/966b0832-b2fe-4e70-b2af-1527f2f3052f/ref/page1).

_Short invocation for daily use:_

    St. Augustine of Hippo,
    Doctor of Grace,
    pray for us.

## 15. Patristic and Magisterial Witness

> "I would like to return to the meditations on the Fathers of the Church and speak today of the greatest Father of the Latin Church, St. Augustine."
>
> - Pope Benedict XVI, [General Audience, 9 January 2008](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/be11462a-ad4d-490a-97a1-4d1925b12ae0/ref/page1)

> "Faith and reason" are "the two forces that lead us to knowledge."
>
> - St. Augustine, _Against the Academics_, III.20.43, quoted by Pope Benedict XVI in the [General Audience of 30 January 2008](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/f9731769-c62a-4e01-b6a1-e911147f31a7/ref/page1)

> "The hidden force of his tireless search was assuredly the same force that had guided him on the path of his conversion: love for the truth."
>
> - Pope John Paul II, [_Augustinum Hipponensem_](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/8cae03c8-966a-4b10-818d-a57f6a256de2/ref/II.5), II.5

> "Late have I loved you, O Beauty ever ancient, ever new, late have I loved you!"
>
> - St. Augustine, _Confessions_, X.27.38

The Catechism uses Augustine's opening insight to describe the human longing for God: "The desire for God is written in the human heart" (CCC 27), and it quotes the restless-heart passage in its treatment of the search for God (CCC 30). The invocation of Augustine's intercession belongs to the Church's doctrine of the communion of saints, not to the worship owed to God alone (CCC 956, 2683).
