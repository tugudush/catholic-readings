# Prayers of St. Francis of Assisi

## A Short Introduction to St. Francis

**St. Francis of Assisi** (1181/1182 – 3 October 1226), born Giovanni di Pietro di Bernardone, was the son of a wealthy cloth merchant of Assisi in Umbria. Raised for chivalry and dreams of military glory, he was taken prisoner in the war with Perugia, fell ill, and returned home unsettled. His conversion came by way of the lepers — whom he had once found bitter to look upon, and whom he then embraced and served — and by the voice from the crucifix in the ruined chapel of San Damiano, which told him to repair God's house, "which, as you see, is falling into ruin." When he renounced his inheritance publicly before the Bishop of Assisi, he was given the Gospel as his rule: the Lord's sending of the disciples without gold, silver, or sandals (Matthew 10:9–10).

From that beginning grew the **Order of Friars Minor**, approved orally by Pope Innocent III and confirmed by Pope Honorius III in the bull _Solet Annuere_ (29 November 1223), together with the Poor Clares and the Secular Franciscans. Poverty, humility, and the literal following of Christ marked his whole life. In September 1224, on the mountain hermitage of La Verna, he received the **stigmata** — the first recorded in Christian history — and bore those wounds for the two years that remained to him. Near-blind and gravely ill, he composed the _Canticle of Brother Sun_, one of the earliest poems of the Italian vernacular. He died at the Portiuncula on the evening of 3 October 1226, was canonized by Pope Gregory IX on 16 July 1228, and is commemorated on **4 October**. Pope Pius XII named him patron of Italy in 1939, and Pope John Paul II declared him patron of ecology in 1979.

Characteristically, Francis left very little writing, and what he left is almost entirely prayer. The texts in this booklet are drawn from that small body of work, and are presented with their manuscripts and circumstances so that the reader may know which prayers are his and which only carry his name.

## 1. The Prayer Before the Crucifix (San Damiano, c. 1205–1206)

According to the early lives, Francis composed this prayer while praying before the painted crucifix in the ruined chapel of San Damiano, from which he heard the call to repair God's house. It is the earliest datable text from his hand, and it is built almost entirely out of Gospel petitions.

    Most High and glorious God,
    enlighten the darkness of my heart,
    and give me right faith,
    certain hope,
    and perfect charity,
    with insight and knowledge, Lord,
    that I may carry out
    your holy and true command.
    Amen.

---

## 2. The Praises of God Most High (La Verna, 1224)

In September 1224 at La Verna, Francis wrote out his praises of God on a small parchment and gave it to Brother Leo as a blessing. The parchment, in Francis's own hand and still preserved at Assisi, carries these praises on one side and the blessing of Brother Leo on the other. They are not petition but pure doxology — a heaped-up series of "You are" clauses.

    You are holy, Lord, the only God,
    and your deeds are wonderful.
    You are strong. You are great. You are the Most High.
    You are almighty. You, holy Father, King of heaven and earth.
    You are three and one, Lord God, all good.
    You are good, all good, supreme good,
    Lord God, living and true.
    You are love. You are wisdom.
    You are humility. You are endurance.
    You are rest. You are peace.
    You are joy and gladness.
    You are justice and moderation.
    You are all our riches, and you suffice for us.
    You are beauty. You are gentleness.
    You are our protector. You are our guardian and defender.
    You are courage. You are our haven and our hope.
    You are our faith, our great consolation.
    You are our eternal life, great and wonderful Lord,
    God almighty, merciful Savior.

## 3. The "Absorbeat" — A Prayer to Obtain Love

This brief prayer, preserved among Francis's shorter writings, asks for the charity that consumed him. Its opening word, _Absorbeat_, supplies its customary name.

    Lord, I beg you,
    let the sweet and burning power of your love
    absorb my mind,
    that I may die for love of your love,
    you who deigned to die
    for love of my love.

---

## 4. The Canticle of Brother Sun (1225)

In the spring of 1225, suffering from severe illness and near-blindness, Francis composed the _Cantico delle Creature_ — the earliest great poem of the Italian vernacular. It is a hymn of praise offered through the created order, and it ends with the verse welcoming "Sister Death," which his companions read to him at the last. The English below is a working rendering; translators differ considerably, and no single English version is normative.

    Most High, all-powerful, good Lord,
    yours are the praises, the glory, the honour, and all blessing.
    To you alone, Most High, do they belong,
    and no one is worthy to speak your name.

    Praised be you, my Lord, with all your creatures,
    especially Sir Brother Sun,
    who is the day, and through whom you give us light.
    He is beautiful and radiant with great splendour,
    and bears a likeness of you, Most High One.

    Praised be you, my Lord, through Sister Moon and the stars:
    in heaven you formed them clear and precious and beautiful.

    Praised be you, my Lord, through Brother Wind,
    and through the air, cloudy and serene, and every kind of weather,
    through which you give sustenance to your creatures.

    Praised be you, my Lord, through Sister Water,
    who is very useful and humble and precious and chaste.

    Praised be you, my Lord, through Brother Fire,
    through whom you light the night;
    he is beautiful and playful and robust and strong.

    Praised be you, my Lord, through our Sister Mother Earth,
    who sustains and governs us,
    and produces various fruits with coloured flowers and herbs.

    Praised be you, my Lord, through those who give pardon for your love,
    and bear infirmity and tribulation.
    Blessed are those who endure in peace,
    for by you, Most High, they shall be crowned.

    Praised be you, my Lord, through our Sister Bodily Death,
    from whom no living being can escape.
    Woe to those who die in mortal sin;
    blessed are those whom death finds in your most holy will,
    for the second death shall do them no harm.

    Praise and bless my Lord, and give him thanks,
    and serve him with great humility.

## 5. The Blessing of Brother Leo

On the reverse of the La Verna parchment, Francis wrote out the priestly blessing of Numbers 6:24–26 for Brother Leo, signing it with the _tau_ — the sign of the cross shaped like a T, which he loved as a mark of penance and election. It is the blessing the Church still gives, and it may be prayed over anyone in need of it.

    The Lord bless you and keep you.
    The Lord make his face shine upon you, and be gracious to you.
    The Lord lift up his countenance upon you,
    and give you peace.

    Amen.

---

## 6. The Collect of His Feast (4 October)

The Church's own prayer asking Francis's intercession is the Collect of his Mass. His celebration is a **Memorial** in the General Roman Calendar and a **Feast** in Italy, since Pope Pius XII proclaimed him patron of Italy on 18 June 1939. From the _Messale Romano_, "San Francesco d'Assisi, patrono d'Italia":

    O Padre, che hai concesso a san Francesco
    di essere immagine viva di Cristo povero e umile,
    fa' che, camminando sulle sue orme,
    possiamo seguire il tuo Figlio
    e unirci a te in carità e letizia.
    Per il nostro Signore Gesù Cristo, tuo Figlio, che è Dio,
    e vive e regna con te, nell'unità dello Spirito Santo,
    per tutti i secoli dei secoli.

    Amen.

A working English rendering of the same collect (the approved English wording varies by translation):

    Father, you granted Saint Francis
    to be a living image of Christ, poor and humble;
    grant that, walking in his footsteps,
    we may follow your Son
    and be united to you in charity and joy.
    Through our Lord Jesus Christ, your Son,
    who is God, and lives and reigns with you,
    in the unity of the Holy Spirit,
    one God, for ever and ever.

    Amen.

## 7. A Prayer Asking His Intercession

St. Francis embodied the radical poverty and joyous love of Christ to such a degree that he received the sacred stigmata — the very wounds of the Crucified. The faithful seek his intercession for the grace of simplicity, for peace in a divided world, for love of all God's creation, and for the courage to live the Gospel without compromise. This prayer is a devotional composition inspired by the Franciscan tradition; it is not a text written by the saint.

    O seraphic St. Francis, mirror of Christ's poverty
    and herald of the Great King,
    you who were so conformed to Jesus crucified
    that you bore His sacred wounds in your own flesh,
    obtain for us a heart detached from worldly goods
    and burning with love for God alone.

    St. Francis, lover of all God's creatures,
    teach us to see the Creator's beauty in every living thing;
    St. Francis, instrument of Christ's peace,
    make us channels of that peace
    where there is hatred, injury, doubt, and despair.

    St. Francis of Assisi, little poor man of God,
    pray for us now and at the hour of our death.

    Amen.

A shorter invocation for daily use:

    St. Francis of Assisi,
    pray for us.

## 8. The "Peace Prayer" — Commonly Attributed to St. Francis

The best-known prayer in the world under Francis's name is not his. "Lord, make me an instrument of your peace" was first published anonymously in 1912 in the French Franciscan bulletin _La Clochette_, and was attributed to Francis only from about 1916 onward. Pope John Paul II, praying it at Assisi in 1986, introduced it as a prayer "commonly attributed to Saint Francis" — a popular attribution, not a claim of authorship. It is printed here because it is widely loved and is often wanted for prayer, but it should not be quoted as his own words.

    Lord, make me an instrument of your peace.
    Where there is hatred, let me sow love;
    where there is injury, pardon;
    where there is doubt, faith;
    where there is despair, hope;
    where there is darkness, light;
    and where there is sadness, joy.

    O Divine Master, grant that I may not so much seek
    to be consoled as to console,
    to be understood as to understand,
    to be loved as to love.
    For it is in giving that we receive,
    it is in pardoning that we are forgiven,
    and it is in dying that we are born to eternal life.

    Amen.

## A Note on This Print Edition

- **The sources of these texts.** Sections 1–3 and 5 are Francis's own, preserved in the critical collection of his _Opuscula_ and translated in _Francis of Assisi: Early Documents_, vol. 1. Section 4 is his _Cantico delle Creature_. Section 6 is the Collect of the Roman Missal. Sections 7 and 8 are later devotional compositions and are labelled as such.
- **Wording varies.** English renderings of Francis's prayers and of the Italian Collect differ between translations; none of the renderings above is normative or official except the Italian of the Collect.
- **One common misattribution.** "Preach the Gospel at all times. When necessary, use words" is a modern aphorism found neither in Francis's writings nor in the early biographies. It summarises his practice rather than reporting his words.
- **A caution on the "Prayer for Animals"** and similar texts in devotional booklets: these are generally anonymous compositions of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries written in a Franciscan spirit. A prayer may express Francis's spirit, and the Church may commend it to the faithful, without being a text he wrote.
