## Executive Summary

The interview is a wide-ranging Catholic spiritual-warfare primer built around Dave VanVickle's decades of experience assisting exorcists across the United States. VanVickle opens by describing how one enters the exorcism assistant role — typically through a pre-existing trust relationship with a bishop-appointed exorcist, often one's own spiritual director — and recounts his very first case, which remains the worst he ever witnessed: a 110-pound woman with superhuman strength, knowledge of hidden things, and physical aversion to holy water, who was finally liberated after three weeks of prayer.

The interview's central pastoral argument is **incarnational Catholicism wins the spiritual battle**. Every Catholic thing — the priesthood, the sacraments, holy water, incense, the Rosary, the Brown Scapular, sacred images — offends demons because it is the flesh of Jesus Christ extended through His Body the Church. The most counter-intuitive and pastorally useful takeaways concern unexpected vectors for possession. VanVickle names **yoga** as the most common surprising gateway, with a detailed account of a devout Catholic woman who began with fitness classes, was progressively drawn into Hindu ashram life, and ended totally possessed — losing her family, her health, and her faith before recovering through sacramental confession and a retreat. He also treats **tattoos** (when acquired in mortal sin or given by a malicious practitioner) and **pornography** (which he calls "the perfect weapon of Satan" and a more pressing concern for the modern Church than the traditional occult), with a sustained discussion of the philosophical reasons demons hate humans — the irrevocable nature of their own choice versus our linear, repentable time.

Several vivid anecdotal claims are reported as VanVickle's first-hand witness (a 110-pound woman throwing grown men across a room; a levitating baptismal candidate requiring fourteen firefighters to pull her down; a mafia hitman whose cross tattoos gave demons legal rights to his body). These are not Catholic doctrine and should not be cited as such. The Marian and Michael claims — that Our Lady's presence during exorcism is "palpable," that demons are particularly terrified of her because they "don't know" her and cannot tempt her — also converge with widely circulated exorcist testimony and with the Church's Marian doctrine but are not defined dogma. They are best received as encouraging devotional tradition.

The closing pastoral exhortation — "place your hope in the Immaculata," "let Our Lady take your biggest battles" — paired with the scriptural example of St. Dominic forcing demons to confess that Our Lady is all-powerful (drawn from [St. Louis de Montfort](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/2677)) and the practical recommendation of Marian Consecration, offers ordinary Catholics a coherent and deeply Catholic response to a topic that is often sensationalized.

## Main Teachings of the Interview

### 1. The four signs of possession and how exorcists distinguish possession from mental illness

The interview's most pastorally useful first hour is its careful explanation of the four traditional signs by which a Catholic exorcist distinguishes genuine demonic possession from a psychiatric condition. VanVickle draws these directly from the _Prenotanda_ of the Roman Ritual of 1614 (preserved and updated in the 1998 _De Exorcizandis Obsessis a Daemonio_).

The four signs are:

1. **Preternatural physical abilities.** Superhuman strength (the 110-pound woman bending a brass candlestick and throwing five or six grown men across the room); levitation (the baptismal candidate floating above the font). VanVickle is quick to note that even levitation has natural explanations that some modern audiences will propose ("science just hasn't caught up with that yet") — but the combination of levitation with the other signs excludes natural explanations.
2. **Knowledge of unlearned languages.** The possessed person is observed to be proficient in languages (often ancient languages) they have never learned. No psychiatric condition produces fluency in classical languages.
3. **Clairvoyance.** The possessed person knows things they could not possibly know — the hidden sins of those present, the unspoken prayers of the exorcist, events from the lives of those in the room. VanVickle observes that demons use clairvoyance "to try to get you freaked out" — it is the demon's "favorite" of the four because it most effectively destabilizes the spiritual life of those involved.
4. **Aversion to the sacred.** A physical, involuntary reaction to holy water, blessed objects, sacred images, prayers invoking the saints. VanVickle gives the classic test: when a priest surreptitiously sprinkles holy water on a person's back, the person turns around and demands to know what is happening. A sufferer of schizophrenia or dissociative identity disorder will not react to a sacramental with revulsion.

The pastoral point is decisive: **none of these signs can be mimicked by any mental illness**. Exorcism is therefore undertaken only after medical and psychiatric evaluation has ruled out a natural explanation, in keeping with the Church's long-standing prudential discipline. The 1998 _Rituale Romanum_ (revised under Pope John Paul II in the wake of the document cited in the interview) and the diocesan protocol generally require that the possessed person be seen by a doctor and a psychiatrist before the bishop will authorize a formal exorcism.

> **Doctrinal note.** The four signs are not listed in the Creed and are not conditions of salvation. They are the prudential criteria by which the Church distinguishes the genuine supernatural phenomenon of possession from its natural mimics. The Church's teaching on the existence of possession ([CCC 1673](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/1673)) is the doctrinal claim; the four signs are the pastoral application.

### 2. Saint Michael and the Blessed Virgin Mary in exorcism

VanVickle is asked directly whether he has ever seen St. Michael the Archangel appear during an exorcism. His answer is: "A few times… I think most exorcists would say most exorcisms end with Our Lady."

The identification is twofold. Sometimes it is the possessed person's own testimony, given either during the session ("I see St. Michael standing behind the priest") or afterwards ("I was above my body, watching"). Sometimes it is the demon's own reaction — a shift from bravado to dread that signals a new supernatural presence. VanVickle notes the peculiar familiarity between the demons and St. Michael: "It's kinda like watching two fighters that have fought for their whole career… their battle spills into time."

For Our Lady, the sign is "palpable" — a felt fear from the demons and a felt peace from Mary, both of which the assistant experiences directly. The demons, in his testimony, shift from bravado to pleading when Mary is invoked, and they sometimes attempt to bargain. The theological reason he gives is that the demons "know us through temptation" but cannot tempt Mary; she "gives them nothing." Because they cannot "read" her as they read us, they do not understand why she has such power over them, and they are particularly terrified by her approach.

This converges with two long-standing strands of Catholic teaching:

- The scriptural pattern of the Woman who crushes the serpent's head ([Gen 3:15](https://www.drbo.org/x/d?b=drb&c=gen&g=3&p=15); [Rev 12:1-6](https://www.drbo.org/x/d?b=drb&c=rev&g=12&p=1)).
- The Church's defined Marian doctrine — Mother of God ([CCC 495](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/495)), perpetual virginity ([CCC 499](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/499)), Immaculate Conception ([CCC 491](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/491)), Assumption ([CCC 966](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/966)), and her powerful intercession ([Lk 1:28](https://www.drbo.org/x/d?b=drb&c=luk&g=1&p=28); [CCC 2677](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/2677)).

The interview makes a pastoral argument from the moment of the Cross: "When Christ died on the cross and that blood and water flowed into all the church, it was like he took that victory, put it in a little bow, and handed it to Our Lady and said, here, your job now is to exact that victory over the evil one." This is a pastoral image rather than a defined dogma. The Church's defined Marian doctrine ([_Lumen Gentium_ 8](https://www.vatican.va/archive/hist_councils/ii_vatican_council/documents/vat-ii_const_19641121_lumen-gentium_en.html); [_Redemptoris Mater_](https://www.vatican.va/content/john-paul-ii/en/encyclicals/documents/hf_jp-ii_enc_25031987_redemptoris-mater.html) 45-47; [CCC 970-975](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/970)) establishes Mary as the Mother of the Redeemer and the eschatological image of the Church; the specific formulation in the interview is a particular (and moving) reading of how that doctrine plays out in spiritual combat.

### 3. The four Catholic distinctives that offend demons

Asked what specific Catholic practices demons find most offensive, VanVickle gives an answer that converges with the historical testimony of exorcists from [St. John Vianney](https://stams.nl/) to Fr. Gabriele Amorth and Fr. José Antonio Fortea: **everything incarnational**.

- **The _im persona Christi_ of the priest.** When the priest puts on the stole and prays in the person of Christ, the demons see Christ — and "they are terrified of Jesus." VanVickle notes that he could imitate the Latin and the gestures if he wanted, but the demons "would laugh me out of that place" because he lacks the Christological character of the ordained priest. Riecker observes that this is why virtually every Protestant who witnesses an exorcism becomes Catholic; the visible action is so manifestly Catholic that the Catholic reading of the priesthood is forced upon the observer.
- **The seven sacraments and especially the Eucharist.** Demons "despise the incarnation," and anything that is incarnational — flesh touching the divine — offends them. The Eucharist is the supreme case: the priest's confession "this is my Body" is treated by demons with the same dread that the demons treated Christ's own Incarnation.
- **The sacramentals and devotional life.** Holy water, incense, blessed candles, palms, ashes, the Brown Scapular, the Rosary — all of these are extensions of the Incarnation into material reality, and the demons find them "mind-boggling" and "unbelievably angering." VanVickle gives the example of incense: that "we could see and smell incense and think of the prayers of the saints and join our prayers with this" is incomprehensible to a purely spiritual being.
- **The Marian and angelic intercession.** As noted above, the demons are particularly terrified of Our Lady and St. Michael.

This converges with the Church's sacramental and liturgical theology ([CCC 774-776](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/774); [CCC 1113-1134](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/1113); [CCC 1666-1679](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/1666)). The Church teaches that the seven sacraments are the principal means of grace ([CCC 1210](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/1210); [CCC 1534](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/1534)) and that sacramentals (blessings, exorcisms, blessed objects) prepare for, accompany, or extend the grace of the sacraments. The interview's "incarnational" framing is a pastoral gloss on this theology, not a substitute for it.

### 4. The Surprising Vectors for Possession

#### 4.1 Yoga

VanVickle names **yoga as the most common surprising gateway to demonic activity** among the people who seek his diocese's help. The headline case is a devout Catholic woman who "just wanted to get in shape" and began with hot yoga, became progressively drawn into more advanced classes, eventually left her husband, traveled to a bhagaram (Hindu ashram) in India for training, returned to start her own practice, and "ended up totally possessed." She lost her marriage, her hair, her health; her family eventually persuaded her to seek sacramental help. She was freed through sacramental reconciliation, sacramentals, and a retreat in which she gave her life to the Lord "for the first time really."

VanVickle does not claim that every yoga practitioner will be possessed, but he does make a theological argument about the intrinsic incompatibility of yoga with Christianity. The Sanskrit word _prana_ (often translated neutrally as "energy") actually means the spiritual life force, the breath of life that flows through all things; the goal of yogic practice is the shedding of the natural will until "we become divine." This is _apotheosis_, a strict contradiction of the Christian doctrine of creaturely dependence on God. The most dangerous form is Kundalini Yoga, which seeks to "shed any attachment to the body" and culminates in the claim that the practitioner "can be worshipped as a god."

The case is treated by VanVickle as a paradigmatic example of the **principle that even sincere, devout Catholics can open themselves to demonic influence without intending to do so**. The mechanism is twofold: implicit admission (when the practitioner is in a state of mortal sin or grave spiritual vulnerability) and the spiritual content of the practice itself. VanVickle reports that he has personally stopped doing P90-X because of his concerns about the yoga elements.

This teaching converges with the Church's general caution against religious syncretism and against practices that have an explicitly religious content incompatible with Christian doctrine ([CCC 2117](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/2117); the condemnation of "conci­liating hidden powers"). It also converges with the catechetical treatment of "New Age" practices and Eastern meditation methods in the various Church documents on the subject (cf. [_Jesus Christ the Bearer of the Water of Life_](https://www.vatican.va/roman_curia/pontifical_councils/interelg/documents/rc_pc_interelg_doc_20030203_new-age_en.html)). The specific demonization claim is, however, first-hand testimony and should be received as pastoral witness.

#### 4.2 Tattoos

The most striking tattoo case is a mafia hitman who got a small cross tattooed on his back after each successful killing. He came to faith toward the end of his life (his cancer diagnosis precipitated a "real conversion"), but exorcists could not liberate him despite years of prayer. The breakthrough came when his shirt rode up during a session and the pattern of crosses was visible — a "counter" of his victims. The exorcist exercised the tattoos; this was the legal right the demons had been holding to his body. He died still possessed, but with the assurance of salvation through his conversion.

A second, lesser risk is a tattoo given by a malicious practitioner — for example, a witch who takes the blood and the ink and uses them for curses. VanVickle explicitly distinguishes his position from those exorcists who teach that the act of tattooing itself is demonic; the act is morally neutral, but the context and content can give demons a "right" to a body.

#### 4.3 Pornography

VanVickle is asked whether pornography is cursed. He answers that he does not know whether it is literally cursed, but he calls it "the perfect weapon of Satan" because of how it operates:

- It damages the imagination, making it very difficult to forget what has been seen.
- It immediately puts users in mortal sin.
- It is taken in through the eyes — which, in Catholic incarnational theology, are spiritual as well as biological.
- It establishes a long relationship with "heinous sin," which lowers the "hedge of grace" around a person until "demons can kinda have their way with you."

He names pornography as the most pressing concern for the modern Church — "there's gonna be a time in the next ten years when they're gonna have to appoint 10 or 12 exorcists in every diocese because of pornography." The four traditional sources of demonic admission, in his pastoral scheme, are (1) violation of the first commandment (occult activity), (2) grave woundedness from childhood (such as sexual abuse), (3) long relationships with heinous sin, and (4) the combination of these.

This converges with the Church's consistent teaching on the protection of chastity ([CCC 2337-2359](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/2337)) and on the spiritual dangers of habitual mortal sin ([CCC 1855-1863](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/1855); [CCC 1862-1863](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/1862) on the wound of sin). It also converges with the catechetical teaching that the grace of God is the only effective remedy against sin ([CCC 1422-1429](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/1422); [CCC 2010-2011](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/2010)). The specific claim about the future number of exorcists needed is pastoral conjecture.

### 5. Why demons hate humans: epiternity and the irreversibility of choice

The interview's philosophical center is VanVickle's explanation of why demons, with infinite other things they could do, are obsessed with destroying human souls. He gives four reasons:

1. **The Incarnation.** Demons despise the flesh of Jesus Christ because the Incarnation is the supreme mystery of God's condescension — the eternal Word taking on created human nature. Anything incarnational offends them; we are, in a sense, walking around in the very thing they despise.
2. **The irreversibility of the demonic choice.** Demons have total foreknowledge of the consequences of their rebellion, and they do not live in linear time the way we do. The interview uses the term **"epiternity"** for their mode of existence — a kind of time that is "time-less," in which their choice is a constant, living, present rebellion. They are not "rebelling" (a present action) but "in rebellion" (a permanent state). Because of this they cannot repent — repentance requires temporal succession (one moment's choice can be reversed in the next moment's), and they do not have temporal succession in the way we do.
3. **Jealousy of our ability to repent.** Because we live in linear time, we can at one moment choose ourselves and at the next moment choose God again and receive His mercy. This drives the demons to fury. They see humans — beings they consider far beneath them — receiving the mercy they themselves can never receive.
4. **Our merit grows every time we beat them.** Every successful spiritual battle increases our merit in God's eyes, and the demons know this. They also know that at the Last Judgment they will be required to serve the saints ([1 Cor 6:3](https://www.drbo.org/x/d?b=drb&c=1co&g=6&p=3)). VanVickle sums up the underlying dynamic: "they can't hurt God, but they hurt God by hurting those who God loves."

The philosophical category "epiternity" — sometimes called "aevum" or "aeviternity" in scholastic terminology — has a long history in Catholic thought. St. Thomas Aquinas treats it in the _Summa Theologiae_ (I, q. 10, a. 5; q. 58, a. 3) as the mode of existence proper to angels and to the separated soul, between the eternity proper to God (which has no succession at all) and the time proper to material creatures (which has succession). VanVickle's pastoral use of the category — to explain why demons cannot repent — is consistent with this tradition.

The demons' ultimate motive, in VanVickle's reading, is that "we're the apple of God's eye, and they just can't stand that fact." This converges with the catechetical teaching on the irrevocable nature of the angelic choice ([CCC 391-395](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/391); [CCC 1033-1037](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/1033) on the impossibility of repentance after death for those who have rejected God). It also converges with the patristic reading of [John 12:31](https://www.drbo.org/x/d?b=drb&c=joh&g=12&p=31): the "prince of this world" is cast out.

### 6. Hauntings and infestations

VanVickle draws a sharp pastoral distinction between two kinds of unusual supernatural phenomena in houses:

- **A haunting** is the manifestation of a purgatorial soul seeking prayers, Masses, or other acts of suffrage. It is "annoying" rather than dangerous. The characteristic marks are that the soul only signals a need for prayer, does not engage in dialogue, and seeks no relationship with the living. The pastoral response is to pray for the soul, have a Mass offered, and ask for the house's blessing.
- **An infestation** is the manifestation of a demon in a place. It begins annoyingly and escalates to dark and terrifying. The "rights" the demon uses to enter can be implicit or explicit. Explicit infestation is caused by an inhabitant who is a witch or occultist with a dedicated space for summoning. Implicit infestation is caused by serious mortal sin — pornography addiction, murder, the practice of abortion in a location. The worst case VanVickle has ever seen (and "probably the worst in American history") is a pre-legal abortion clinic that had buried the babies in the backyard. The site, in his judgment, would require full excavation of the contaminated soil, re-burial of the remains in consecrated ground, and re-importation of fresh earth before any blessing would be effective.

This pastoral taxonomy converges with the Church's teaching on the communion of saints and the efficacy of prayers for the dead ([CCC 958-962](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/958); [CCC 1030](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/1030)). It also converges with the catechetical teaching that demons can act upon material creation when given some "right" through the sins of human beings, a teaching that the [Roman Ritual of Exorcism](https://www.vatican.va/news_services/liturgy/saints/ns_lit_doc_20000131_index_exorcism_en.html) itself presupposes. The particular story about the abortion clinic is reported as VanVickle's first-hand witness; it should be received as pastoral testimony, not as an independent journalistic record.

### 7. The authority of the baptized over lesser demonic activity

Asked whether a husband has the authority to command a demon to leave his wife alone, VanVickle answers affirmatively but with pastoral nuance. He is not in the strict school that says only priests can use command formula; he believes that baptized laypeople have real authority in lesser forms of spiritual warfare, subject to the discipline of their local ordinary. He has no problem with a layperson commanding a demon to leave "in the name of Jesus Christ" — for example, on a retreat or in a frightening encounter — though he would never do so during a formal exorcism and would never treat it as full possession-deliverance, which is reserved to the bishop's appointed priest ([CIC can. 1172](https://www.vatican.va/archive/cod-iuris-canonici/eng/documents/cic_lib-iv-cann-1166-1192_sacramentals_en.html)).

Command language is not strictly required: the Rosary, a novena, the intercession of the saints, and other non-command forms of prayer are "just as powerful." Authority comes through holiness and baptism and (for priests) through office.

This converges with the Church's teaching on the common priesthood of the faithful ([CCC 1268-1271](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/1268); [CCC 1641](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/1641)) and with the catechetical distinction between the seven sacraments (which produce their effect _ex opere operato_) and the sacramentals (which produce their effect through the dispositions of those involved; [CCC 1670-1672](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/1670)). It is also consistent with the catechetical teaching on the baptismal priesthood ([CCC 1546-1547](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/1546)).

### 8. Misconceptions about exorcism

VanVickle closes his doctrinal section by correcting several common misconceptions about the spiritual battle:

- **It is not like the movies.** Demons do not stand a chance against an ordained priest. VanVickle reports hearing a demon say that "an average parish priest will steal hundreds of thousands of souls from him in the box" — referring to the confessional. A priest loses an exorcism only if he is doing something wrong.
- **The battle is already over.** VanVickle quotes Fr. Chad Ripperger's formulation that exorcism is "just a cleanup operation. We're just getting rid of already won." The cross of Christ has already won the decisive victory ([Col 2:15](https://www.drbo.org/x/d?b=drb&c=col&g=2&p=15); [CCC 677](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/para-677)). The demons can still harm us, but the war has already been decided.
- **Scary movies begin with the wrong problem.** Real demonic infestation rarely begins with the "most heinous kinds of evil" — satanism, trespass on burial grounds, etc. It begins with **sin**, and especially with habitual mortal sin. The pastoral focus should be on the sins in our own lives, not on the macabre.
- **Physical violence during exorcism is rarer than popular culture suggests.** VanVickle himself has been bitten, scratched, and hit by flying furniture, but most of the most violent reported cases involve assistants who were not in a state of grace. The interview cites Fr. Carlos Martins as having had brain surgery after a possession case.

### 9. The closing pastoral exhortation: "Our Lady is there. Tag me in."

VanVickle's closing message is pastoral rather than doctrinal. He tells viewers that, after more than two decades of assisting exorcists, the most consistent lesson he has learned is that Our Lady is the decisive ally in spiritual combat. He uses the image of a 1980s WWF tag-team wrestling match: "Our Lady is just, 'tag me in.' Don't keep me out of these battles." She does not want our small worries but "the biggest battles" — marriage problems, prodigal children, addictions. He advises the viewer to place hope in the Immaculata and her relationship to her Son, and to let her continually present the viewer to Jesus.

He cites the famous story of [St. Dominic](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/2677) casting out demons from [The Secret of the Rosary](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/2677) by St. Louis de Montfort. St. Dominic forces the demons to proclaim which saint they hate most; they refuse, are beaten, and finally confess that Our Lady is all-powerful and that many people who would otherwise be damned are saved by her intercession. The interview reads the story as a paradigm of how demons relate to Mary.

VanVickle then addresses the common objection to Marian intercession: how can Our Lady hear all our prayers at once if she is in heaven? His answer is that the saints in glory are not bound by earthly space-time in the way the objector assumes. He gives the analogy of the Risen Christ, who "doesn't need to open a door" but can still eat fish (cf. [Lk 24:36-43](https://www.drbo.org/x/d?b=drb&c=luk&g=24&p=36); [Jn 21:9-14](https://www.drbo.org/x/d?b=drb&c=joh&g=21&p=9)). Our Lady in her perfection has been raised to this eternal mode of existence and so has the capacity to hear all of us at all times. (For further treatment of this objection, see the [_Total Consecration to Jesus Incarnate Wisdom through Mary_](../spiritual-life/total-consecration-jesus-incarnate-wisdom-mary.md) document.)

He concludes by recommending [Marian Consecration](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/2677) — particularly the form composed by St. Louis de Montfort and popularized by [St. John Paul II](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/2677) (his papal motto was _Totus Tuus_). "Marrying consecration cures a myriad of ills," he says, and he has "seen it a million times." The interview closes with Riecker's final exhortation: "Jesus Christ has already conquered. We must remain faithful. Be not afraid. God is with you."

### 10.4 Pastoral bottom line for ordinary Catholics

The interview converges with ordinary magisterial teaching at almost every point where VanVickle is making a doctrinal or pastoral claim. The points of contact are:

- **Stay in the state of grace.** Habitual mortal sin (especially pornography) is the most common and most preventable gateway to demonic influence. Receive the [Eucharist](../sacraments/eucharist.md) worthily and frequently.
- **Go to [Confession](../sacraments/penance-reconciliation.md) regularly.** Confessed sins are off-limits to demons as ammunition. Unconfessed mortal sin gives demons "rights" to your body in spiritual combat.
- **Be cautious about yoga**, especially forms that have explicit Hindu religious content (Kundalini Yoga, _prana_ as spiritual life force, _apotheosis_). VanVickle's case study shows that even a devout Catholic can be progressively drawn into possession through what begins as fitness.
- **Avoid pornography** as a matter of spiritual urgency. It is "the perfect weapon of Satan."
- **Pray the [Rosary](../liturgy/rosary/how-to-pray.md) daily.** It is, in the exorcists' consistent testimony, the single most effective non-sacramental prayer in spiritual warfare.
- **Place hope in Our Lady** and her intercession. Consider [Marian Consecration](../spiritual-life/total-consecration-jesus-incarnate-wisdom-mary.md) as a life-commitment.
- **Trust the bishop-appointed priest** for any actual exorcism. Do not engage demons in conversation, theological dispute, or curiosity.
- **Remember the victory is already won.** Christ has conquered ([CCC 677](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/para-677)). Exorcism is "cleanup," not combat.
- **Avoid the temptation to focus on the macabre.** Real demonic infestation usually begins with **sin**, not with satanism or trespass on burial grounds. The pastoral focus should be on the sins in our own lives.
