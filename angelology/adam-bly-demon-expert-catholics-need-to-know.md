## Executive Summary

The interview is a wide-ranging Catholic spiritual-warfare primer built around Adam Bly's experience assisting exorcists in the Diocese of Pittsburgh and around the country. Bly opens with the path that brought him from graduate research in clinical psychology and signal processing of brainwaves, through a Pennsylvania Department of Corrections clinical career, into the Church's ministry of liberation after a chance encounter with a haunted house in Aliquippa outside Pittsburgh. He then turns to the questions Catholics most often ask: what to do about a haunted house, how to recognize the difference between a poor soul in purgatory and a demon pretending to be one, what people unknowingly do that opens the door to demons, and how Mary, the angels, and the sacraments fit into genuine deliverance.

The interview's central pastoral argument is Bly's "pyramid" of spiritual warfare: the dramatic prayers of exorcism are the small peak of a structure whose broad foundation is the sacramental life, whose middle layer is a balanced prayer life, and whose growth in the soul must keep pace with the liberation God wills. "God only frees us as quickly as we're growing towards him" is the pithy form of the teaching, paired with St. Paul's "thorn in the flesh" ([2 Cor 12:7-10](https://www.drbo.org/x/d?b=drb&c=2co&g=12&p=7)) as the biblical anchor: grace, not spectacle, is what is needed.

The most useful takeaways are doctrinal and practical: avoid the occult and ghost hunting entirely; get a home blessed and stop all spirit-related play in it if disturbances occur; go to Confession regularly (confessed sins cannot be weaponized against you by demons); receive the Eucharist worthily; pray the rosary daily; trust the Church's prudential discernment of sacramentals (exorcism is a sacramental, not a sacrament); and never treat exorcism as a "magic wand" detached from conversion of life.

Several vivid anecdotal claims are reported as Bly's first-hand witness (a possessed person spitting nails during sessions, doors flying open, demons broadcasting unconfessed sins in detail, possessed persons sending text messages and speaking unknown languages). These are not Catholic doctrine and should not be cited as such. The Marian claims — that Mary appears to the possessed person in roughly four out of five liberation sessions on the final day and says "it's over," that she "defeats" demons utterly, and that demons cower at the rosary and the Hail Mary — converge with widely circulated exorcist testimony (cf. Fr. Chad Ripperger, Fr. Gabriele Amorth) and with private revelation such as those of [St. Louis de Montfort](../saints/louis-grignion-de-montfort.md) and [St. Bernadette](../saints/bernadette-soubirous.md), but are not defined dogma. They are best received as encouraging devotional tradition.

## Main Teachings of the Interview

### 1. The discernment of spirits in haunted houses

Bly's first extended teaching concerns the discernment of spirits. Only two kinds of spirit manifest, in his account: poor souls in purgatory and demons. There is no "Casper the Friendly Ghost." The decisive marks of a poor soul, when God permits one to appear, are that the soul **only signals a need for prayer** — knocking, a brief appearance, a request for Masses — and **does not engage in dialogue, answer questions, or seek a relationship** with the living. Demons, by contrast, "play nice" only at the outset, then become destructive and terrifying; they manipulate by luring the living into a relationship and a long conversation.

This converges with the Church's general teaching on the intercessory role of the faithful departed and with exorcists' longstanding pastoral observation that demonic manifestations mimic the human in order to bind a person to the spirit. Bly is explicit that "practically 100%" of what ghost hunters record are demons, because ghost hunting is a form of necromancy — calling up the dead — which the Old Testament calls an "abomination" ([Deut 18:10-12](https://www.drbo.org/x/d?b=drb&c=deu&g=18&p=10); cf. [1 Sam 28](https://www.drbo.org/x/d?b=drb&c=1sa&g=28)) and which, in Bly's pastoral reading, God has no reason to reward with a genuine apparition of a soul in purgatory.

The practical advice for a haunted house is correspondingly simple and entirely Catholic: get the house blessed (preferably before or at move-in), stop every form of occult play in it (Ouija boards, spells, divination, ghost hunting), use the [Epiphany Blessing of the Threshold](../sacramentals/epiphany-blessing.md) (chalk writing over the door), and, if manifestations are violent or destructive, contact the parish priest because more than a standard blessing may be required. Most hauntings, in Bly's experience, are inherited from previous occupants' occult activity rather than knowingly invited by the current residents.

### 2. How people unknowingly invite demons

Bly identifies four main doors through which Catholics in particular unwittingly open themselves to demonic influence. Each of these is treated in the interview as something lay people do not recognize as spiritually dangerous, not as deliberate occultism.

- **Ghost hunting.** Most popular ghost-hunting TV shows and streaming content depict what the Old Testament calls necromancy. Catholics who play along, Bly reports from his public talks, frequently do not realize that they are sinning or that the voices and apparitions are demons.
- **Ouija boards and pendulums.** These are uniquely dangerous because the user is not merely inviting communication but literally handing over physical control of the body — arms and hands — to a spirit. The "worst case" Bly has ever seen came from years-long heavy use of a Ouija board as a daily decision-making tool.
- **Wicca, witchcraft, and "white witch" practices.** Even practitioners who do not intend demonic contact find that the practice inevitably darkens into it. "Sooner or later, your life becomes a nightmare."
- **Divination of any kind.** All forms of divination are listed together; no practice is treated as harmless.

This list converges with the [Catechism of the Catholic Church §2117](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/2117): "All forms of divination are to be rejected: recourse to Satan or demons, conjuring up the dead or other practices wrongly supposed to 'unveil' the future. Consulting horoscopes, astrology, palm reading, interpretation of omens and lots, the phenomena of clairvoyance, and recourse to mediums all conceal a desire for power over time, history, and, finally, other human beings, as well as a wish to conciliate hidden powers. They contradict the honor, respect, and loving fear that unite us to God alone."

### 3. Mary and the Rosary in deliverance

Bly's most distinctive experiential claim is that Mary appears to the possessed person **in roughly four out of five liberation sessions**, typically on the day of final liberation, and that she almost always says the same three words: **"It's over."** He reports this for Christians and non-Christians alike; one non-Christian woman who had almost no exposure to Christianity asked afterwards "who was that woman?" because Mary, in Bly's account, does not introduce herself.

A second vision, given not on a liberation day, is reported from a possessed man who was not yet freed: during a session in which the priest was speaking of the defeat of the demons at the Crucifixion, the man broke into convulsive sobbing and reported that he had been taken back to the Cross and "couldn't look up" at Jesus, but that Mary stood there in black mourning clothes, her face covered except for her eyes, and that the devastating sadness in her eyes was something he could not bear to keep looking at. He describes her eyes as "a gray-blue sky with clouds moving through it."

Bly explains this Marian mediation as part of God's ordinary pedagogy: Jesus wants people to develop a relationship with his mother as intercessor. The months and years of gradual liberation before the final day are punctuated by Jesus freeing the person session after session — "like peeling an onion" — and the possessed person spontaneously develops a deep devotion to the Eucharist. Non-Catholic Christians in his caseload, Bly reports, almost always end up wanting to enter the Church, not because anyone tells them to but because they say, "I'm drawn to the tabernacle. I want to be closer to him." At the very least, he argues, even apart from Marian doctrine proper, Mary's free "yes" at the Annunciation was a real human cooperation with the Incarnation, so the idea of her as mediatrix has at least that level of scriptural warrant ([Lk 1:38](https://www.drbo.org/x/d?b=drb&c=luk&g=1&p=38)).

He also reports the recurrent demon reaction to Mary during sessions. When the priest brings up Jesus, the demon's attitude is in effect "yeah, of course we know he's God, we have to leave when he says so, we know that better than you do." When Mary is invoked, the demon shifts to abject fear and pleadings — "Don't call that woman, please don't call that woman" — and will sometimes add that Mary "defeats me so utterly," that they have never been able to touch her, and that they have never been able to make her sin. Bly treats this as one of the more reliable demons' self-testimonies because it does not serve the demon's tactical interest and converges with the longstanding [Marian tradition](../saints/louis-grignion-de-montfort.md) of Mary as the Woman who crushes the serpent ([Gen 3:15](https://www.drbo.org/x/d?b=drb&c=gen&g=3&p=15); [Lk 1:28](https://www.drbo.org/x/d?b=drb&c=luk&g=1&p=28); [Rev 12:1](https://www.drbo.org/x/d?b=drb&c=rev&g=12&p=1)).

The pastoral conclusion Bly draws is that the rosary is the single best deliverance prayer. Riecker agrees with the formulation; both treat daily rosary as a baseline, not a heroic extra.

> **Doctrinal note.** The Marian claims are first-hand exorcist testimony, not defined dogma. They should be received as encouraging devotional tradition consistent with Catholic teaching on Mary as Mother of God, Theotokos, ever-virgin, assumed into heaven, and intercessor, but they are not the kind of claim that can be cited as "the Church teaches that Mary appears in four out of five exorcisms."

### 4. Exorcism vs. deliverance vs. sacramentals

Bly distinguishes three things that are routinely confused in Catholic conversation.

- **Deliverance prayer.** "Jesus, please help this person." It is a request. Laypeople and clerics alike may pray it; it asks God to act.
- **Exorcism.** "In the name of Jesus, get out." It is a direct command and therefore a direct conflict between the priest and the demon. It is reserved by canon law to a priest with the express permission of his ordinary (the diocesan bishop). Laypeople may assist but may not perform it ([CIC can. 1172](https://www.vatican.va/archive/cod-iuris-canonici/eng/documents/cic_lib-iv-cann-1166-1192_sacramentals_en.html)).
- **Sacramental.** Exorcism is a sacramental, not a sacrament. Sacraments are seven in number and were instituted by Christ; sacramentals are sacred signs instituted by the Church that prepare for, accompany, or extend the grace of the sacraments ([CCC 1667](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/1667)). Sacramentals have no guaranteed effect; sacraments do.

The practical upshot is that Confession is "more powerful" than exorcism only in this technical sense: Confession is a sacrament with a guaranteed action (the forgiveness of sins), while exorcism is a sacramental whose fruit depends on the dispositions of all involved, including whether the possessed person is secretly holding on to the demon or refusing to give up an occult practice. Bly is careful to note that exorcism days are nonetheless days of tremendous, sometimes mystical, grace, and that the long middle periods of deliverance ministry are more like a slog than a spectacle.

A practical consequence: demons can broadcast a person's unconfessed shameful sins to the room during a session — in detail, as though they had been standing there when it happened — but they **cannot** weaponize sins that have been confessed. Confession therefore functions as a kind of "off-switch" for that particular demonic ammunition. This is Bly's pastoral recommendation of Confession as a baseline, not as a piece of deliverance theater.

### 5. The role of the Eucharist, angels, and saints in exorcism

#### 5.1 The Eucharist

It is **generally discouraged** to bring the Eucharist out of the tabernacle during an exorcism because there is a real risk of profanation: the possessed person may spit on, throw, or otherwise defile it. Bly reports that demons commonly spit on crucifixes and icons, and that some are bold enough to attempt the same with the Blessed Sacrament. In exceptional situations — for example, in a remote location far from a church — he has heard of the Blessed Sacrament being exposed in a monstrance, but this is described as rare and pastorally unusual.

The pastoral implication for ordinary Catholics is unchanged: receive the Eucharist worthily and frequently, and trust that sacramental worthiness — not proximity to a possession case — is what matters.

#### 5.2 Guardian angels

Guardian angels are not, in Bly's experience, typically invoked explicitly during exorcisms. He describes one exception in which the mother of a possessed young man left mid-session on a sudden inspiration, drove home, retrieved a vintage print of a guardian angel, returned within twenty minutes, and held it up; the demon went into fits and screamed "no," and the young man later reported hearing his guardian angel say, "I am his guardian angel and I am here to free him." Bly treats this as an exceptional, once-in-a-career episode, not as a routine.

The baseline practice is that **St. Michael** is the archangel invoked during exorcism. **St. Raphael** is invoked especially for the demon he defeated in the Book of Tobit ([Tob 12:15](https://www.drbo.org/x/d?b=drb&c=tob&g=12&p=15)). **St. Gabriel** is invoked in cases where the person has a devotion to him. **Mary's intercession** is treated as central and constant.

Bly recommends that, even outside the exorcism context, Catholics ask their guardian angel to pray for them and beg grace from God, particularly in spiritual trouble, but this is general devotional advice rather than exorcism-specific protocol.

#### 5.3 Why more women than men seek exorcism

Over Bly's roughly twenty-year sample, the women-to-men ratio among those presenting for help is approximately **three to one**. He is careful to say that the cases he does not see — men who do not ask for help — cannot be quantified, so the "why" remains partly speculative. The pastoral theory he entertains is connected to the Fall and the Incarnation: because God became man through Mary, the demons' offense at the Incarnation has been directed, in his pastoral reading, against women, parallel to Herod's slaughter of the innocents in pursuit of the newborn King ([Mt 2:16](https://www.drbo.org/x/d?b=drb&c=mat&g=2&p=16)). The theory is offered as pastoral speculation, not as doctrine.

### 6. The extraordinary phenomena of possession

In addition to the spiritual and psychological phenomena above, Bly reports the following extraordinary physical phenomena as first-hand and second-hand observation across his years of casework. None of these is Catholic doctrine, and none should be cited as more than the careful testimony of a single practitioner who has spent twenty years in this work.

- **Spitting nails.** In a particularly heavy case involving Satan himself, the possessed person would spit literal nails from her mouth at the priest and at an icon of St. Michael for the entire session; at the end of the day there would be 30 to 60 nails on the floor. The same person entered and spoke normally; she was not cheeking the nails. European exorcists with 40–50 years of practice report wooden boxes of razor blades, hinges, nails, and similar objects retrieved from the mouths of the possessed.
- **Doors flying open.** During sessions in which the demon calls for help from other demons, doors have flown open as if kicked in from the inside, with no human agent.
- **Knowledge of unconfessed sins.** Demons can name a person's most shameful moments "in some detail, as if they were standing there when it happened," even events that occurred when the person was alone decades earlier. Confessed sins are off-limits.
- **Ancient or unknown languages.** The possessed person has been observed to speak languages they have never learned, including ancient languages.
- **Text messages from the possessed person's body.** Bly reports (with visible surprise from the host) that demons can send text messages using the possessed person's phone and body.
- **Out-of-body experience during possession.** The possessed person frequently reports being "outside" the body, looking at the room from above, hearing what is being said from her mouth, and having no control over the body.

These phenomena are presented as reportable but not as the criteria for distinguishing genuine possession. The Catholic criteria are medical (the symptoms must not be explainable by mental illness) and spiritual, and they are applied by the bishop's appointed priest, not by the possessed person or the popular press.

### 7. The pyramid of spiritual warfare

Bly's most quoted teaching, and the one the interview is essentially structured around, is the **"pyramid" of spiritual warfare**. The dramatic prayers of exorcism and deliverance are the small peak of a structure whose broad foundation is the sacramental life and whose middle layer is a balanced prayer life.

```
                   /\
                  /  \
                 / EX \   <-- Special dramatic prayers / deliverance ministry
                /      \      (peak; only the bishop-appointed priest performs
               /--------\      the actual exorcism; laity assist)
              /          \
             /  BALANCED  \  <-- Daily prayer: Our Father, rosary,
            /   PRAYER     \     novenas, devotions (both petition and
           /    LIFE        \    worship; not just "free me of this problem")
          /                  \
         /--------------------\
        /                      \
       /   SACRAMENTAL FOUND.   \  <-- Baptism, Confirmation, Eucharist,
      /   (broadest layer)        \     Confession, Marriage, Holy Orders,
     /                             \    Anointing of the Sick. Sacramentals
    /______________________________\   (holy water, blessings, medals)
```

The foundational claim is that **God only frees us as quickly as we are growing toward him**. The liberation is not a magic wand waved by a specialist; it is the fruit of a conversion of life. People who seek a dramatic intervention without changing their lives will not receive the full liberation. Bly anchors the teaching with St. Paul's "thorn in the flesh" ([2 Cor 12:7-10](https://www.drbo.org/x/d?b=drb&c=2co&g=12&p=7)): God told Paul "my grace is sufficient for you," and the affliction was not removed. The implication is that some suffering is allowed to produce merit, and that the response to affliction is grace, not spectacle.

The pastoral analog Riecker draws on is Naaman the Syrian ([2 Kgs 5:1-14](https://www.drbo.org/x/d?b=drb&c=4ki&g=5&p=1)): he wanted dramatic healing and was told to bathe seven times in the Jordan, refused, was challenged by his servant, obeyed, and was cured. The spiritual growth of the average Catholic is more like that — quiet, sacramental, persistent — than like the dramatic prayers of an exorcist.

This is consistent with the Catechism's "hierarchy of truths" ([CCC 90](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/90)) and with the ordinary magisterial teaching that the sacraments are the principal means of grace ([CCC 1422](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/1422)).

### 8. Demons and aliens

Asked at the close of the interview about UFOs and aliens, Bly gives a layered answer.

- On a strict scientific reading he does not find interstellar travel plausible: the distances are too great, the time scales too long, and we have not picked up the relevant electromagnetic signals outside the Wow! signal of 1977.
- On the experiential side, he is certain that **demons will manifest as whatever will push your buttons**: dead relatives, totem animals, aliens. Whatever will hook the viewer is what the demon will choose.
- On contemporary military UFO testimony, he does not have insider access. His guess is that what pilots are seeing is advanced human (or human-adjacent) technology that the public has not been told about, not actual extraterrestrials. The interview does not develop this further.

The pastoral conclusion Bly draws is one of ordinary epistemic humility: do not rush to conclude that what looks like an alien is an alien. ### 9.3 Pastoral bottom line for ordinary Catholics

The interview converges with ordinary magisterial teaching at almost every point where Bly is making a doctrinal or pastoral claim. The points of contact are:

- Get the house blessed; use the [Epiphany Blessing of the Threshold](../sacramentals/epiphany-blessing.md); stop all occult play.
- Go to [Confession](../sacraments/penance-reconciliation.md) regularly. Confessed sins are off-limits to demons as ammunition.
- Receive the [Eucharist](../sacraments/eucharist.md) worthily and frequently.
- Pray the [rosary](../liturgy/rosary/how-to-pray.md) daily.
- Avoid all occult practices, including the seemingly harmless ones (pendulums, fortune-telling of any kind, ghost-hunting entertainment, astrology, tarot).
- Read [CCC 2117](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/2117) and the surrounding sections on the eighth commandment.
- Trust the bishop-appointed priest for any actual exorcism; do not engage demons in conversation, theological dispute, or curiosity.
- Avoid the temptation to seek deliverance as a "magic wand" detached from conversion of life. The growth is in the sacramental foundation and the balanced prayer life; the dramatic prayer is the peak, not the workhorse.
