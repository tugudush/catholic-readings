# Fr. Gabriele Amorth, _An Exorcist Tells His Story_: Summary and Study Guide

## Source and Scope

This document summarizes **Fr. Gabriele Amorth's _An Exorcist Tells His Story_** (Ignatius Press, 1999; translated by Nicoletta V. MacKenzie from _Un esorcista racconta_, Edizioni Dehoniane, Rome, 1990). The book is the practitioner's memoir and working manual of the priest who served as exorcist of the Diocese of Rome — the first such book by a Roman exorcist to reach a wide English-reading public, and the source from which most later popular Catholic writing on demonology and deliverance draws.

It is a summary, not a translation, a critical edition, or a verbatim transcript. Quotations are kept short; the prayers, formulas, and case narratives belong to the book and should be read there. Page numbers are not cited because the local copy is a scanned conversion that does not preserve reliable page markers; chapters are cited instead.

Three things must be said at the outset about how to read it:

- **It is a memoir and a manual, not a theological treatise.** Amorth writes as a pastor reporting what he has seen and done. His doctrinal statements are mostly conventional and well sourced; his diagnoses, anecdotes, polemics, and proposals are his own.
- **It reports experience that cannot be independently verified.** Physical objects said to be expelled from the body, demons' answers, and second-hand case histories are exorcist testimony. This summary reports them as such and never promotes them to doctrine.
- **It is a period document.** The Italian original appeared in 1990 and carries the polemical heat of the late 1980s: named criticisms of theologians and clergy, complaints about the unreformed rite, and cultural observations that read differently a generation later.

## Executive Summary

The book makes one argument in two movements. The first is doctrinal: **Satan is a real, personal creature who fell by his own fault, was defeated by Christ, and continues to act only by divine permission** — and therefore a Christology, a theology of redemption, or a pastoral program that ignores him is not merely incomplete but incoherent. Amorth presses this against the "demythologized" Satan of mid-century academic theology, arguing from Scripture, the Fathers, the Fourth Lateran Council, the Second Vatican Council, and the Catechism that the Church has never regarded the devil as a personification of abstract evil.

The second movement is practical: **exorcism is a sacramental of the Church, not a charism of the enthusiastic**, and it has been allowed to decay. Amorth describes how a case is received, diagnosed, and treated; the four-phase behavior of a demon across a course of exorcisms; the use of the exorcised water, oil, and salt of the Ritual; the exorcism of houses; the four kinds of curse; and the discernment of true charisms from seers, sensitives, healers, prana therapists, sorcerers, and frauds. The through-line of Part Two is that **diagnosis is the exorcist's first task and the Ritual's first purpose** — a claim he returns to repeatedly against exorcists who treat only "possession", and against bishops and theologians who treat nothing at all.

The book's most quoted assertions are its most contested: that the three signs of possession listed in the 1614 Ritual (unknown tongues, superhuman strength, knowledge of hidden things) appear **during** exorcisms and practically never before them; that "an unnecessary exorcism never harmed anyone" while a wrongly refused one has; that the ineffectiveness of medicine combined with the effectiveness of blessings is a determinative diagnostic factor; and that a diocese without an exorcist is guilty of a grave pastoral omission.

## Part One: The Doctrine

### 1. The centrality of Christ, and therefore of Satan

Amorth opens by correcting a catechetical habit: the story of creation as "angels first, then a test, then a fall, then the world, then the Fall of man, then the remedy of Christ". Against it he sets the christological hymns of [Colossians 1:15-20](https://www.drbo.org/x/d?b=drb&c=col&g=1&p=15) and Ephesians 1, and the prologue of John: Christ is "the firstborn of all creatures", and everything — angels and men alike — was created for him and in expectation of him. The angels are not strangers to the mystery of Christ; St. Athanasius, he notes, even holds that they owe their salvation to his blood.

This is the hinge of the whole book. Satan's rebellion is not a private quarrel in heaven but the refusal of a creature to accept that creation is centered on Christ: he "wanted to continue to be the absolute first, the center of creation". Hence Amorth's rule that a Christology which ignores Satan can never measure the redemption, and a demonology which ignores Christ is mere superstition.

He is careful on two points. First, evil, suffering, death, and hell are **not** acts of God; the demons themselves, questioned, insist that hell was their doing, not God's — an anecdote Amorth reports and does not build doctrine upon. Second, the four scriptural truths that answer fatalism: God wills all to be saved; no one is predestined to hell; Christ died for all; sufficient grace is given to all.

### 2. The power of Satan

Amorth's target here is the theological fashion — he names Bultmann's demythologizing and the statistics of theologians who affirm demons "in theory" but not in pastoral practice — which reduces Satan to a symbol of evil. His reply is that Satan is a spiritual person with intellect, will, freedom, and initiative; that the only dogmatic definition is the Fourth Lateran Council's ("the devil and the other demons were created good by God; but they became evil through their own fault"); and that whoever denies Satan ends by denying sin, and so cannot understand what Christ did.

The chapter's structure is then a taxonomy that governs the rest of the book:

- **Ordinary activity** — temptation, aimed at everyone, resistible with grace. Amorth deliberately sets it aside.
- **Extraordinary activity**, permitted by God, in six forms:

| Form                      | What it is                                                                                                   |
| ------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| External physical pain    | Demons beating or striking a person from outside (the Curé d'Ars, Padre Pio); no exorcism needed.            |
| Possession                | The demon acts through the body without the victim's consent; the victim is morally blameless.               |
| Oppression                | Illness, misfortune, or affliction of body or circumstances without loss of consciousness (Job; 2 Cor 12:7). |
| Obsession                 | Persistent intrusive and absurd thoughts, often with suicidal despair, that the victim cannot expel.         |
| Infestation               | Disturbances in houses, objects, or animals — a term he refuses to apply to persons.                         |
| Subjugation or dependence | Voluntary submission: the blood pact, consecration to Satan, satanic priesthood.                             |

He closes by insisting that where there is no possession the ordinary means of grace suffice — prayer, sacraments, almsgiving, forgiveness, the intercession of Our Lady, the saints, and the angels — and by a warm passage on guardian angels, each person's "most faithful friend twenty-four hours a day, from conception to death".

### 3. Exorcism as a sacramental

The distinction on which Amorth insists throughout:

- **Deliverance prayer** flows from [Mark 16:17](https://www.drbo.org/x/d?b=drb&c=mar&g=16&p=17) ("in my name they will cast out demons"); it is open to all the faithful, individually or in groups, and requires no authorization.
- **Exorcism**, properly so called, is the **sacramental** instituted by the Church, administered only by a bishop or by a priest with express and individual permission (then canon 1172; now the same canon in the 1983 Code; see also CCC 1673). "Simple exorcism" is the renunciation in Baptism (CCC 1237); "solemn exorcism" is the sacramental of the Ritual.

Two consequences follow. The title "exorcist" is abused when claimed by priests without faculties or by laity who at best pray deliverance prayers and at worst practice witchcraft. And the purpose of exorcism is **twofold, in this order: diagnosis, then liberation**. Amorth argues that the diagnostic function has been forgotten by everyone who writes on the subject, and that this is a pastoral disaster — "an unnecessary exorcism never harmed anyone", whereas he has had reason to regret refusing one. He also concedes the other danger: mistaking mental illness for possession has been warned against by the Church since the Synod of Reims in 1583, and cooperation with psychiatrists is indispensable in complex cases.

### 4. Why people come under extraordinary activity

Amorth groups the routes into four, and the grouping does most of his pastoral work:

1. **By God's permission alone** — the innocent victim, entirely without personal fault (Job, St. Paul's thorn, the saints who were physically attacked).
2. **Through a curse or spell** — no fault in the victim, but human agency in the one who curses or hires a curse.
3. **Through a grave and hardened state of sin** — the one category that is the victim's own doing; he cites Judas, "and after the morsel, Satan entered into him" ([John 13:27](https://www.drbo.org/x/d?b=drb&c=joh&g=13&p=27)).
4. **Through association with evil people or places** — séances, occult consultation, satanic sects, warlocks and card readers, and beyond them the blood pact.

The chapter is also where Amorth's cultural diagnosis appears: the collapse of practice, "where religion regresses, superstition progresses", the spread of occult practice among the young, and the consequent duty of bishops to appoint exorcists. He judges the removal of most exorcisms from the baptismal rite and the dropping of the Leonine prayers to have been grave mistakes — a pastoral judgment, not a doctrinal one, and one he states as his own opinion.

## Part Two: The Practice

### 5. The point of departure: diagnosis

An exorcism cannot be improvised, and the 1614 Ritual's twenty-one norms — which he rates as still wise and still binding — begin by warning the exorcist against credulity. In practice the exorcist begins with an interview: what the symptoms are, when they began, what preceded them, what medicine has done.

The recurring diagnostic profile he describes:

- Head and stomach as the two most affected areas: intractable headaches, and acute pain below the sternum that wanders and resists treatment.
- Sudden inability to study or concentrate in a young person who had no prior difficulty.
- Aversion to the sacred — prayer stops, church is avoided, blasphemy and violence against images appear.
- Noises, footsteps, doors opening and closing, objects moving or vanishing.
- Objects found inside pillows or mattresses: coloured threads, knotted cords, hair, slivers of wood or iron, puppets, blood clots — which he treats as evidence of malefice.
- And the rule he states as determinative: **the ineffectiveness of medicine together with the effectiveness of blessings**.

He warns that the three Ritual signs appear during exorcisms, practically never before them, and that demanding them as a precondition for exorcism is inexperience. The chapter closes with case histories — Martha, Pierluigi, the mute demon, the family with the blaspheming grandfather — and with the observation that the same demon can be present in more than one person at once.

### 6. The first "blessings"

Method, as he actually practises it:

- **Euphemism**: exorcisms are always called "blessings", the presence of evil "negativity", so that a suggestible person is not confirmed in a fixation on being possessed.
- **Latin**, for the same reason and because the Ritual's prayers are in Latin.
- The **Alcuin prayers** (ninth–tenth century): introductory prayers and three exorcisms in a logical progression, to which Amorth and Fr. Candido added an invocation of Our Lady after the pattern of Leo XIII.
- The **stole** at the neck, the hand on the head, anointing with the **oil of catechumens** at the first exorcism (an inference from baptismal anointing, not prescribed by the Ritual), and attention to the eyes and pupils.
- **Brevity** at the first session; demons hide, and repeated sessions force them into the open.
- **Restraint in questioning**: the Ritual forbids curiosity. Ask the name, the number, and what serves liberation — nothing more.

### 7. The demon's behavior in four phases

| Phase            | Behavior                                                                                                                            |
| ---------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Before discovery | Symptoms are treated as medical or psychiatric; the victim is disbelieved; many turn first to healers or sorcerers and worsen.      |
| During exorcisms | Concealment, indifference, then reaction; reluctance to give the name; threats and discouragement aimed at the exorcist.            |
| Nearing the exit | Desperation — "I am dying", "you are killing me" — and the communication of that despair to the victim, who often wants to stop.    |
| After liberation | Relapse is possible and is worse where prayer and the sacraments have been abandoned (Matthew 12:43-45); occasional exorcisms help. |

He notes the diagnostic contrast with mental illness: the disturbed patient seeks attention, the demon conceals himself.

### 8. A victim's witness

A long first-person account by a man identified only as G. G. M. — a happy adolescence, a move to Rome, a drift into the occult-adjacent company, a conversion, a pilgrimage to Medjugorje, and then the return of a darkness he describes as being "killed by an unseen knife". Months of fever, inability to eat, the sense of being outside his own body, hospitalization with every test normal, a collapse in the street, and a vision of hell "from a distance". The chapter exists to make the reader feel what the diagnostic chapters describe; it is testimony, not evidence.

### 9. Water, oil, and salt

The three exorcised sacramentals, available to any priest without special faculty:

- **Exorcised water** — forgiveness, defense against the evil one, divine protection; it also protects dwellings.
- **Exorcised oil** — dispels demonic power and, he says, separates impurities from the body where a curse was taken in food or drink.
- **Exorcised salt** — placed across thresholds and in the corners of affected rooms.

He also values **blessed incense** for houses, and the Ritual's **blessing of clothing** — which he uses both therapeutically and as a test, since a person under influence may tear off blessed clothing or find food cooked with blessed water bitter. He is careful about the logic of tests: a reaction is a positive indication, but **no reaction proves nothing**, because demons work to avoid detection. The chapter also warns that visions, locutions, and claims to be a visionary are among the most frequent signs of diabolic deception.

### 10. Exorcising houses

There is no biblical precedent and the Ritual does not provide for it, yet Amorth judges it necessary in some cases and grounds it in CCC 1673, which speaks of exorcism for **objects** as well as persons. Phenomena are unglamorous: squeaks, thumps, the sensation of being watched, doors and windows opening at fixed hours, footsteps in the hall, objects moved, animals heard but not seen. Pets are useful witnesses — a cat staring at one spot, an animal fleeing in terror.

The method: several of the Ritual's prayers for the protection of places, the first part of the first exorcism of persons adapted to the building, a room-by-room blessing, a second round with blessed incense, and a **Mass celebrated in the house**. Where the cause is a curse that is renewed, the exorcism must be repeated until the house is "evil proof"; where the place has hosted séances or satanic ritual, he has sometimes advised the family to leave. A milder remedy he reports from Fr. Pellegrino Ernetti: a mixture of exorcised water, oil, and salt, a teaspoon of it poured nightly on every windowsill and threshold while praying the Our Father.

### 11. The curse

Amorth's four-part taxonomy, with the caution that the terms overlap:

| Form                 | Description                                                                                                          |
| -------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Black magic          | Satanic rites, culminating in black masses, invoking the demon against a specific person.                            |
| Curses               | Spoken imprecations, most damaging when uttered by a parent or grandparent, and worst at a wedding or against life.  |
| The evil eye         | A spell worked through sight, presupposing the will to harm a particular person by demonic means.                    |
| The spell (malefice) | A manufactured object offered to Satan and used directly (in food or drink) or indirectly (on a proxy or belonging). |

The most affecting passages are the cases of parental curses — the father who cursed his son at birth, the father who cursed his daughter on her wedding day, the grandmother's curse on a grandson's legs that was fulfilled decades later. The most practical are the instructions for disposal: objects sprinkled with blessed water, burned in the open, and the ashes thrown into running water, never into a drain.

Two cautions carry the chapter's doctrinal weight. First, the Ritual (norm 8) forbids sending the afflicted to sorcerers, and Amorth calls advice to the contrary an unpardonable mistake. Second, and against his own material: **do not live in fear of a curse**. Most complaints dissolve into suggestibility; curses often fail because God does not permit them, because the target prays deeply, because the practitioner is incompetent or a fraud, or because the devil deceives his own servants. "If we live in communion with God, it is Satan and all of hell who tremble at our presence — unless we ourselves open the door to him" ([James 4:7](https://www.drbo.org/x/d?b=drb&c=jas&g=4&p=7); 1 Peter 5:9).

### 12. More on witchcraft

This chapter widens the lens: the biblical prohibitions of [Deuteronomy 18:10-12](https://www.drbo.org/x/d?b=drb&c=deu&g=18&p=10) and Leviticus 19–20; the two mechanisms of magic (imitative, working by resemblance on a proxy, and contagious, working through something belonging to the target); the sacralizing apparatus of "high magic" — pentacles, magnetized objects, talismans sold to the desperate; divination and tarot, which he treats chiefly as a mechanism of dependency and suspicion; and spiritualism, where the medium lends voice and gesture to spirits who "are always and only demons". He allows exactly one biblical exception, Saul and the witch of Endor (1 Samuel 28), and reads it as a permitted anomaly, not a technique.

He then argues that whole groups and even nations can be struck or led — naming Marx, Hitler, and Stalin as examples of diabolic proportions — and that collective possession of classrooms, associations, and communities, while easier to contract, is harder to cure. That passage is his opinion, not the Church's teaching, and should be reported as such.

### 13. Who can expel demons

A survey of the field, with the sharpest pastoral reasoning in the book:

- **All the faithful** may pray deliverance prayers; groups praying concurrently with an exorcism are a great help, as are relics (above all of the Holy Cross), the rosary, and images of St. Michael.
- **Priests with faculties** administer the sacramental; every priest's ordination gives him a ministerial power ordered to the service of the faithful.
- **Charisms** of healing and liberation are given freely by the Spirit, and Amorth offers four tests of authenticity: a life that lives the Gospel deeply; a service given absolutely free; only Church-approved means, with nothing magical or superstitious; and good fruits — "the tree is known by its fruit" (Matthew 12:33). He cites _Lumen Gentium_ 12 on the bishops' competence to judge charisms, and laments that the Council's guidance is largely ignored.
- **Seers and sensitives** he uses as collaborators, describing cases where they identified the age at which a spell was contracted or the precise place to apply the stole; he judges their gift more likely paranormal than charismatic, and insists their impressions be checked.
- **Healers, prana therapists, sorcerers, gypsies**: healers have no effect on illnesses of evil origin; prana therapy requires "extreme prudence" because of the danger of evil infiltration; sorcerers divide into the genuinely diabolical and the merely fraudulent, and the fraudulent are far more numerous — he records burning a "talisman" that had cost a victim $8,000.

The chapter ends with the warning that applies most to himself and his colleagues: the temptation to find a quicker way to heal outside the Church's means, which is the first step into magic.

### 14. The "Cinderella" of the Ritual

The polemical centre of the book. Every section of the Roman Ritual was revised after the Council except the one on exorcisms, still marked "work in progress" — so that when a victim looks for an exorcist, "they come up against the usual sign: 'Work in progress'". He quotes Paul VI's address of 15 November 1972 on the devil as a chapter of Catholic doctrine that is "practically ignored", and then turns on named theologians and clergy of the late 1980s whose published positions he regards as denying in practice what they concede in theory. He also criticizes exorcists themselves — "we lost the school", the apprenticeship by which an experienced exorcist trained his successor — and records his own figures: in nine years he had exorcised more than thirty thousand people and noted ninety-three cases of true possession, most of long standing.

The chapter closes with the case of Annelise Michel (Klingenberg, 1986) and the trial of the two priests, which he says a later study substantially exonerated, but which he concedes contributed to clergy refusing the office. He addresses bishops directly: a bishop who neither acts nor delegates when faced with a valid request is, he argues, guilty of a grave sin of omission (Matthew 25). His positive proposals come in the appendix: Cardinal Suenens' suggestion that episcopal conferences ask Rome to restore the office of exorcist, and Fr. La Grua's proposal that every diocese have a discernment group of three or four, including a doctor and a psychologist, referring cases to medicine, to a prayer group, or to the exorcist according to severity.

## The Documentary Appendices

The appendices are the book's evidence file, and they are the part most worth reading in full:

1. **The Diabolical Vision of Pope Leo XIII** — the 1886 origin of the Leonine prayers and the St. Michael prayer, with the testimony of Cardinal Nasalli Rocca and the report in _Ephemerides Liturgicae_; Amorth adds Pius XI's 1930 intention for Russia.
2. **St. Teresa of Ávila on fear of the devil** — the passage from chapter 25 of her _Life_ in which she takes a cross and dares the demons: "I pay no more attention to them than to flies."
3. **The thought of St. Irenaeus** — the fall through pride and envy of man, the "recapitulation" in Christ the second Adam, and the ransom framing of redemption.
4. **A Vatican document on demonology** — the CDF's _Christian Faith and Demonology_ (1975), set against Bultmann and against theologians who affirm demons in theory only.
5. **Pastoral directives to be rebuilt** — his case that the Ritual now mentions only possession while exorcists in fact treat oppression, obsession, and infestation, plus the Suenens and La Grua proposals.
6. **The CDF letter to ordinaries of 29 September 1985** — three points: canon 1172 requires express and individual episcopal permission; the faithful may not use the Leonine exorcism formula; and bishops must ensure that no one without the faculty leads meetings at which demons are questioned directly. Amorth adds Acts 19 (the sons of Sceva) as the scriptural warning about amateurs.
7. **Prayers of deliverance** — a prayer against malefice from the Greek Ritual, the _Anima Christi_, a prayer against every evil, and a prayer for inner healing.

The 1985 letter deserves emphasis because it is the one document in the book that **restrains** rather than encourages: it forbids the faithful from using the Leonine formula, forbids the untrained from leading sessions that interrogate demons, and reminds bishops that exorcism is reserved to mandated priests. Amorth prints it in full and accepts it.

## Catholic Doctrinal Assessment

The book mixes grades of authority and does not always separate them. This table sorts the main claims.

| Claim                                                                                         | Grade                                                                                                                                                                                                                  |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Satan and the demons are personal creatures, created good, evil by their own fault            | **Dogma.** Fourth Lateran Council (1215); CCC 391–395.                                                                                                                                                                 |
| Satan acts in the world and on persons only by divine permission, and cannot take the will    | **Doctrine.** CCC 395; the Book of Job; 2 Corinthians 12:7.                                                                                                                                                            |
| Christ's ministry included exorcism, and he gave the Church power over demons                 | **Dogma.** Scripture; CCC 550; the Church's constant practice.                                                                                                                                                         |
| Exorcism is a sacramental, not a sacrament, and is reserved to a bishop or a mandated priest  | **Discipline / law.** Canon 1172; CCC 1673. Not immutable, but binding.                                                                                                                                                |
| Baptism contains the only "simple exorcism"; solemn exorcism is the sacramental of the Ritual | **Doctrine with disciplinary form.** CCC 1237, 1673.                                                                                                                                                                   |
| Possession, oppression, obsession, infestation, and subjugation are distinct categories       | **Common theological terminology.** Traditional and useful; not defined. The 1999 rite's _Praenotanda_ use a narrower set.                                                                                             |
| The three Ritual signs appear during exorcisms and rarely before                              | **Exorcist experience.** Contested even among exorcists; not doctrine.                                                                                                                                                 |
| "An unnecessary exorcism never harmed anyone"                                                 | **Pastoral opinion.** Contested; the 1985 CDF letter moves the other way on untrained ministry.                                                                                                                        |
| Ineffective medicine plus effective blessings is a determinative sign                         | **Prudential criterion.** Useful heuristic; not a rule of faith, and no substitute for medical care.                                                                                                                   |
| Objects (nails, glass, hair, dolls) are expelled from the body                                | **Exorcist testimony.** Unverifiable; report, do not teach.                                                                                                                                                            |
| Curses and malefice really cause harm, and objects can carry them                             | **Pastoral practice and experience.** The Church condemns recourse to magic (CCC 2115–2117) and the Ritual assumes malefice; the causal claims rest on exorcist testimony, and Amorth himself warns against credulity. |
| There is no "white" magic                                                                     | **Sound doctrine.** All magic involves recourse to Satan; CCC 2117.                                                                                                                                                    |
| Groups and nations can be led by demonic influence (Marx, Hitler, Stalin; "satanic rock")     | **Opinion.** Not Church teaching; historically and theologically unverifiable as stated.                                                                                                                               |
| Bishops who fail to provide exorcists commit a grave sin of omission                          | **His moral-theological argument**, from canon 529 and Matthew 25. A serious claim, argued, not defined.                                                                                                               |
| The devil does not retaliate against faithful exorcists                                       | **Pastoral judgment**, against a popular belief he considers damaging; he allows rare exceptions.                                                                                                                      |

Two further distinctions the book itself keeps, and the reader should keep with it:

- **Diabolism is not superstition.** Amorth's constant refrain is that superstition grows as faith declines, and that "the devil and holy water" do not mix. His remedies are the Church's ordinary means.
- **Private revelation is not doctrine.** The Leo XIII vision, the Medjugorje references, the demons' reported answers about hell, and the case narratives are all in the register of private revelation and testimony. None of it is proposed for belief as faith.

## Points Where the Reader Should Be Careful

- **The named polemics are period pieces.** Amorth names theologians, a Turin exorcist, a Florentine canon penitentiary, and a Palermo pastor whose published positions he regarded as undermining the ministry. This summary records that he made the argument; it does not adopt the characterizations, and the underlying theological questions are still legitimately debated.
- **Numbers in the book are his own tally**, offered without audit: more than thirty thousand people exorcised and ninety-three cases of possession in nine years. Note also that nine years of ministry from a 1990 publication implies a start around 1981, earlier than the 1986 appointment commonly reported; the discrepancy is best explained by counting his apprenticeship under Fr. Candido Amantini.
- **The book predates the reform it demanded.** The revised rite, _De exorcismis et supplicationibus quibusdam_, was promulgated in 1999 — the same year as this translation — and its _Praenotanda_ restate the ministry, the required permission, and the signs with more precision than the 1614 Ritual. Reading Amorth's complaints without the 1999 rite gives a false picture of the present discipline.
- **Medical and psychiatric referral is not optional.** Amorth says so repeatedly, but his confidence in exorcist diagnosis reads, to a modern reader, more strongly than his cautions. The Church's own 1583 warning against confusing illness with possession, which he cites approvingly, should govern.
- **Nothing here replaces the sacraments.** The book's own hierarchy is evangelization, then sacraments, then sacramentals, with exorcism last among them.
