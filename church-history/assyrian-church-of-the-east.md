# The Assyrian Church of the East: History, Theology, Liturgy, and Relations with Rome

The **Assyrian Church of the East** (Classical Syriac: ܥܕܬܐ ܕܡܕܢܚܐ ܕܐܬܘܪ̈ܝܐ, _ʿĒḏtā ḏ-Maḏnḥā ḏ-ʾĀṯūrāyē_), officially the **Holy Apostolic Catholic Assyrian Church of the East**, is an East Syriac Christian Church descending from the ancient **Church of the East** — the apostolic community of Mesopotamia and Persia that, while geographically outside the Roman Empire and administratively independent of the Byzantine patriarchates, preserved an unbroken line of bishops, the sacrifice of the Holy Qurbana (Eucharist), and the East Syriac liturgical tradition to the present day.

From the Catholic perspective, this community holds a singular and hopeful position among the Churches separated from the Holy See: it is recognized as a **true particular Church**, built upon orthodox faith and apostolic succession, possessing "true sacraments, and above all, by apostolic succession, the priesthood and the Eucharist" (_Unitatis Redintegratio_, 15), and joined to the Chaldean Catholic Church — its sister heir of the same ancient tradition — by a pastoral provision for sacramental sharing in situations of necessity (Pontifical Council for Promoting Christian Unity, _Guidelines for Admission to the Eucharist Between the Chaldean Church and the Assyrian Church of the East_, 2001).

## 1. Identity, Names, and the Question of the "Nestorian" Label

### 1.1 The Names the Church Uses

Historically the community called itself simply **_ʿĒḏtā ḏ-Maḏnḥā_** — "the Church of the East" (or "of the East/Syria of the East") — a geographic designation reflecting its location beyond the eastern frontier of the Roman Empire, centered on the Persian capital of **Seleucia-Ctesiphon**. The fuller title "Holy Apostolic Catholic Assyrian Church of the East" was adopted in the twentieth century, and the shortened form **Assyrian Church of the East** became official in 1976, during the patriarchate of Mar Dinkha IV, in conscious continuity with the ancient Assyrian and Mesopotamian heritage of its faithful.

### 1.2 Why "Nestorian" Is Both Inaccurate and Misleading

For centuries Western and Byzantine writers called this Church "Nestorian," after **Nestorius**, Patriarch of Constantinople (428–431), condemned at the **Council of Ephesus** (431) for his opposition to the Marian title _Theotokos_ ("Mother of God"). The label, however, is seriously misleading on several counts:

- **Historically**, the Church of the East's Christology was not shaped by Nestorius (whose own precise teaching is shrouded in obscurity) but by the **Antiochene tradition** of **Diodorus of Tarsus** and **Theodore of Mopsuestia** (c. 350–428), and definitively formulated by **Babai the Great** (d. 628) at the synod of 612 — decades after Nestorius. As the Syriac scholar Sebastian P. Brock wrote, "the association between the Church of the East and Nestorius is of a very tenuous nature, and to continue to call that Church 'Nestorian' is, from a historical point of view, totally misleading and incorrect."
- **Doctrinally**, the Church of the East never taught that Christ is "two persons" — the heresy properly called Nestorianism (dyoprosopism). Its traditional formula confesses two _qnome_ (Syriac ܩܢܘܡܐ, corresponding to the Greek _hypostases_ in the sense of concrete individual natures), "unmingled, but everlastingly united in the one _prosopon_ (person)" of Christ.
- **Pastorally**, Patriarch Mar Dinkha IV explicitly repudiated the elements of Nestorian doctrine associated with the label on the occasion of his accession in 1976.

Nevertheless, the name "Nestorius" is still venerated in the Church's calendar of saints and Fathers, and the **Anaphora of Nestorius** remains in its liturgical books — facts which the Catholic Church acknowledges with historical realism while recognizing, as the 1994 Common Christological Declaration demonstrates, that the underlying faith is not the heresy condemned at Ephesus. (The name "Nestorian" has also been used in an ethnic sense — e.g., "Catholic Nestorians" — which adds further confusion and is best avoided.)

## 2. Apostolic Origins and the First Centuries

### 2.1 The Apostolic Foundation

The Church of the East venerates as its founders **Saint Thomas the Apostle** (who, according to ancient tradition, evangelized the East on his way to India), **Saint Addai of Edessa** (identified with Thaddeus of Edessa, one of the Seventy[-two] Disciples), and **Saint Mari** (also numbered among the Seventy[-two]), Addai's companion in the evangelization of Mesopotamia and Persia. The **Anaphora of Addai and Mari**, the Church's principal Eucharistic Prayer, is treasured as the liturgical memorial of this apostolic mission — a claim the Catholic Church treats with great respect, since the anaphora "was composed and used with the clear intention of celebrating the Eucharist in full continuity with the Last Supper and according to the intention of the Church" (PCPCU, _Guidelines_, 2001).

The community organized itself around the metropolitan see of **Seleucia-Ctesiphon**, which came to hold primacy among the Persian bishops; its holder received the title **Catholicos** (from the Greek καθολικός, "universal"), and later **Catholicos-Patriarch of the East**. A continuous patriarchal list is claimed from the first century — the current Catholicos-Patriarch Mar Awa III is numbered the 122nd successor.

### 2.2 The Edessene School and the Syriac Heritage

The theological culture of the Church of the East formed at **Edessa** (modern Şanlıurfa) and later at the **School of Nisibis**, founded after the Byzantine emperor's closure of the Persian school of Edessa in 489. Syriac Christianity produced a vast patristic literature — the hymns of Saint Ephrem (d. 373, honored across the Syriac traditions), the commentaries of Theodore of Mopsuestia, the ascetical writings of Isaac of Nineveh (7th century, venerated East and West), and the theological synthesis of Babai the Great.

### 2.3 Separation from the Byzantine Ecclesiastical Sphere

The Christological controversy of the fifth century unfolded differently in Persia than in the Roman Empire. Nestorius was **celebrated** in the Persian Church while Theodore of Mopsuestia remained its supreme theological authority; conversely, the Council of Ephesus (431) and the Council of Chalcedon (451) were never received. Two factors deepened the separation:

- **Political**: The Roman-Persian wars made Persian ecclesiastical independence from "Roman" (Byzantine) authority a matter of royal suspicion; the Persian Church's association with Byzantine dogmatic decrees endangered its faithful. The anathemas of Ephesus against Nestorius were viewed in Persia as a Roman imperial act against Persian theological tradition.
- **Theological**: The Antiochene Christology of Theodore — emphasizing the fullness and reality of Christ's humanity and the distinction of the natures — was incompatible with the Cyrilline formulas of Ephesus as these were received at the time.

## 3. The Christology of Babai the Great

The normative Christological teaching of the Church of the East was composed by **Babai the Great** (d. 628), abbot of the monastery of Nardos near Seleucia-Ctesiphon, during the controversy following Ephesus and at the synod held in 612. Babai taught that in Christ there are:

- **Two _qnome_** (individual natures/hypostases) — divine and human — "unmingled," each retaining "all their properties, faculties and operations"; and
- **One _prosopon_** — one "person" or "face," the single object of adoration: the same God the Word, begotten of the Father before all worlds according to his divinity, born of the Virgin Mary according to his humanity.

This is a form of **dyophysitism** (two-nature doctrine) distinct from Chalcedonian terminology — the difference lies less in the underlying faith than in the Syriac and Antiochene vocabulary (_qnomâ_, _kyānâ_ ["nature"], _parsōpâ_) which does not map neatly onto the Greek _hypostasis_/_physis_ distinctions. Modern scholarship (Brock, Wilmshurst) emphasizes that the Church of the East's own formula is **not** the radical dyoprosopism ("one and another," two persons) condemned at Ephesus — a point which made possible the reconciliation expressed in 1994.

## 4. The Golden Age: Missions to India, Central Asia, and China

From the fifth to the fourteenth century the Church of the East was the most expansive missionary Church in the world, carrying the Gospel far beyond the frontiers of the Roman Empire:

- **India**: The **Chaldean Syrian Church** of Kerala (today the largest diocese of the Assyrian Church of the East, centered in Thrissur) traces its origin to the apostolic mission of Saint Thomas Christians; consistent metropolitan relations with the East Syriac patriarchate were maintained for centuries and revived with the arrival of the Portuguese around 1500.
- **Central Asia and China**: East Syriac missionaries evangelized the Sogdians, Turks, and Mongols. The famous **Nestorian Stele of Xi'an** (erected 781 A.D.) records the arrival of the mission at the Tang court; the Church of the East established metropolitan sees across the Silk Road and in the Mongol imperial capital of Karakorum.
- **The Mongol period**: In the late thirteenth century two East Syriac monks of Mongol-Uyghur origin, **Rabban Bar Sauma** (who traveled to Europe as an ambassador) and **Mar Yaballaha III** (Catholicos-Patriarch, 1281–1317), embodied the extraordinary reach of the Church of the East across Asia.

The decline came with the fall of the Mongol Ilkhanate, the campaigns of **Timur** (Tamerlane) in the late fourteenth century, and the progressive isolation of the community in the mountains of Hakkari, the Urmia plain, and the Mosul region.

## 5. The Schism of 1552, the Chaldean Catholic Church, and Modern Consolidation

### 5.1 The 1552 Crisis and the Union of Mar Sulaqa

Under Patriarch **Shemon VII Ishoyahb** (1539–1558), residing at the Rabban Hormizd Monastery near Alqosh, disputes over hereditary patriarchal succession and union with Rome produced a rupture. A pro-Catholic party at Mosul sent the priest **Yohannan Sulaqa** to Rome; he entered into full communion with the Catholic Church and was confirmed by **Pope Julius III** in April 1553 as Patriarch of the Chaldeans. Taking the name **Shimun**, he founded the line of patriarchs that eventually became, after centuries of further divisions and reunions, the modern **Chaldean Catholic Church** — the East Syriac particular Church in full communion with the Pope (see [eastern-catholic-churches.md](eastern-catholic-churches.md)).

### 5.2 The Two Traditionalist Lines and Their Consolidation

The non-united Church of the East continued under two rival patriarchal lines:

- The **senior Eliya line of Alqosh**, which preserved the traditional faith and independence until the death of Eliya XII (XIII) in 1804;
- The **junior Shimun line of Qochanis**, founded after Sulaqa, which definitively broke with Rome under **Shimun XIII Dinkha** (1662–1700) by 1672.

In 1804 the Eliya line lapsed, and the reunited traditionalist Church — consolidated under the Shimun line and thereafter known as the **Assyrian Church of the East** — became the sole representative of the non-Catholic East Syriac tradition. A further body, the **Ancient Church of the East**, separated in 1968 (after the reform decrees of 1964), based in Baghdad; it is generally regarded as sharing the same sacramental reality.

### 5.3 Persecution and the Sayfo

The nineteenth and twentieth centuries brought catastrophic suffering: the Kurdish attacks and massacres in Hakkari (1843–1846), the destruction of the patriarchal see at Qodchanis during the **Sayfo** (the Assyrian genocide of 1915–1918, alongside the Armenian and Greek genocides), the murder of Patriarch **Shimun XIX Benyamin** in 1918, and the **Simele massacre** of 1933 in Iraq. Patriarch **Shimun XXI Eshai** (1920–1975) led the Church from exile — Cyprus, Chicago, San Francisco — and was assassinated in November 1975 in San Jose, California. The synod of 1973 had already abolished the hereditary patriarchal succession practiced since the fifteenth century.

### 5.4 The Modern Patriarchates

- **Mar Dinkha IV** (1976–2015): elected at a synod of seven bishops at St. Paul's Abbey, Alton, England (12–17 October 1976), the 120th Catholicos-Patriarch; transferred the patriarchal see to Chicago during the Iran–Iraq War. His patriarchate was marked above all by ecumenical work with the Holy See, culminating in the **Common Christological Declaration of 1994**. He died on 26 March 2015.
- **Mar Gewargis III** (2015–2021): elected 18 September 2015 and enthroned at the Cathedral of St. John the Baptist, Erbil; he promoted the return of the patriarchal see to Iraq.
- **Mar Awa III** (2021– ): elected 8 September 2021 as the 122nd Catholicos-Patriarch, formerly Bishop of California and Secretary of the Holy Synod; the see is now fixed at Ankawa, Erbil.

## 6. Doctrine and Worship

### 6.1 The Councils Received and the Creed

The Assyrian Church of the East receives the first **two ecumenical councils** — **Nicaea I (325)** and **Constantinople I (381)** — and professes the **Nicene-Constantinopolitan Creed**, including the eternal procession of the Holy Spirit **from the Father** (as in the original form of the Creed, without the _Filioque_; compare the discussion in [greek-orthodox-church.md](greek-orthodox-church.md)). The Christological definitions of Ephesus (431) and Chalcedon (451) are not received as dogmatic, though — as the 1994 Declaration makes clear — the faith expressed by them is substantially shared.

### 6.2 Marian Theology

The Church honors the Blessed Virgin Mary with the liturgical title **"the Mother of Christ our God and Saviour."** The 1994 Common Christological Declaration established the equivalence of this expression with the Catholic titles "Mother of God" (_Theotokos_) and "Mother of Christ":

> The humanity to which the Blessed Virgin Mary gave birth always was that of the Son of God himself. That is the reason why the Assyrian Church of the East is praying to the Virgin Mary as "the Mother of Christ our God and Saviour". In the light of this same faith the Catholic tradition addresses the Virgin Mary as "the Mother of God" and also as "the Mother of Christ". We both recognize the legitimacy and rightness of these expressions of the same faith and we both respect the preference of each Church in her liturgical life and piety.

The Marian dogmas of the Immaculate Conception (1854) and the Assumption (1950) are not professed by the Assyrian Church, whose Marian piety is ancient, intense, and expressed above all in the great East Syriac hymns and commemorations.

### 6.3 The Biblical Canon: The Syriac Peshitta

The Church uses the **Peshitta** Bible (ܦܫܝܛܬܐ, "the simple/clear") in Syriac. The Old Testament corresponds largely to the Hebrew canon together with certain additional books (e.g., Tobit and Judith), while the traditional **New Testament contains 22 books** — omitting 2 Peter, 2 John, 3 John, Jude, and Revelation from the canon (though these are sometimes printed for convenience in modern Peshitta editions). For Catholic teaching on the full canon of 73 books, see [scripture/bible-history-canon.md](../scripture/bible-history-canon.md).

### 6.4 Asceticism, Fasting, and Clerical Life

The East Syriac tradition preserves an austere rule of fasting (including the ancient **Fast of Nineveh** before Lent and strict Wednesdays and Fridays), and its own liturgical calendar (the East Syriac liturgical year, with the seasons of Annunciation, Epiphany, Great Fast, Resurrection, Apostles, Summer, and Eliyah–Cross). Clerical discipline: **priests and deacons may be married** (ordination after marriage), while **bishops are chosen from the celibate**; the patriarchal succession was hereditary from uncle to nephew from the fifteenth century until its abolition in 1973–1975.

## 7. The Liturgy: The Holy Qurbana and the Anaphora of Addai and Mari

### 7.1 The East Syriac Rite

The liturgy is celebrated in Classical Syriac, the language of Jesus' own Aramaic heritage, and (as in the Chaldean Catholic Church, which shares the same rite) in three Eucharistic anaphoras, distributed by liturgical calendar:

| Anaphora                               | Attributed to         | Period of Use                                         | Contains an Institution Narrative? |
| -------------------------------------- | --------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| **Anaphora of Addai and Mari**         | Addai and Mari (72)   | Palm Sunday to year-end — c. 200 days (principal use) | No coherent narrative (see §7.2)   |
| **Anaphora of Theodore of Mopsuestia** | Theodore (d. 428)     | From the start of the liturgical year — c. 16 weeks   | Yes                                |
| **Anaphora of Nestorius**              | Nestorius (d. c. 451) | Five prescribed liturgical occasions                  | Yes                                |

### 7.2 The Anaphora of Addai and Mari: The Question of the Words of Institution

The **Anaphora of Addai and Mari** is one of the oldest Eucharistic Prayers in Christendom — scholars date its final redaction variously between the late second and third centuries, probably in the region of Edessa. From time immemorial it has been used **without a coherent recitation of the Institution Narrative** ("This is my Body… This is my Blood…"). Since Catholic tradition holds the words of the Institution to be a constitutive part of the Eucharistic Prayer (Council of Florence, DH 1321), the Holy See undertook a long historical, liturgical, and theological study. On **17 January 2001** the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith concluded — with the approval of Pope John Paul II — that **the Anaphora can be considered valid**, on three grounds:

1. **Its antiquity and intention**: it is one of the most ancient anaphoras, "composed and used with the clear intention of celebrating the Eucharist in full continuity with the Last Supper," and its validity "was never officially contested, neither in the Christian East nor in the Christian West."
2. **The ecclesial reality of the Assyrian Church of the East**: the Catholic Church "recognises the Assyrian Church of the East as a true particular Church, built upon orthodox faith and apostolic succession," which "has also preserved full Eucharistic faith in the presence of our Lord under the species of bread and wine and in the sacrificial character of the Eucharist" (cf. _Unitatis Redintegratio_, 15).
3. **The dispersed presence of the Institution**: the words of Institution are indeed present in the anaphora "not in a coherent narrative way and _ad litteram_, but rather in a **dispersed euchological way**" — integrated in prayers of thanksgiving, praise, and intercession that form a "quasi-narrative" of the institution. Thus the central prayer:

   O my Lord, in thy manifold and ineffable mercies,
   make a good and gracious remembrance
   for all the upright and just fathers who were pleasing before thee,
   in the commemoration of the body and blood of thy Christ,
   which we offer to thee upon the pure and holy altar,
   as thou hast taught us.

   …and let thy Holy Spirit come, O my Lord,
   and rest upon this offering of thy servants,
   and bless it and sanctify it,
   that it may be to us, O my Lord, for the pardon of sins,
   and for the forgiveness of shortcomings,
   and for the great hope of the resurrection from the dead,
   and for new life in the kingdom of heaven
   with all who have been pleasing before thee.

### 7.3 The Mystery of the Holy Leaven (_Malka_)

A distinctive East Syriac practice is the sacrament of **Holy Leaven** (_Malkā_): a portion of consecrated bread, renewed annually by the bishop on Holy Thursday (a remnant of the old being mixed into the new), and distributed to every parish, where it is mixed into every Eucharistic loaf. Tradition holds that the Leaven descends from the bread Christ blessed and broke at the Last Supper, which Saint John preserved and dipped into the Blood that flowed from Christ's side. The Holy See recognizes this rite as "a visible sign of historic and symbolic continuity between the present Eucharistic celebration and the institution of the Eucharist by Jesus" (PCPCU, _Guidelines_, 2001).

### 7.4 Sacraments and Prayer

The Church celebrates the seven sacraments (the "Holy Mysteries"): Baptism (by triple immersion, followed immediately by Chrismation and first Communion — as in the Eastern Catholic Churches), Confirmation, the Holy Qurbana, Penance, Anointing of the Sick, Holy Orders, and Matrimony. Its daily prayer is the **Liturgy of the Hours** (the _Shhima_ and other cycles of psalms, hymns, and prayers) in the Syriac tradition. Its houses of worship are characteristically simple: an aniconic tradition, developed under centuries of Islamic rule, favors a plain **cross** (without corpus) on the eastern wall, though historical manuscripts show that figurative art existed in earlier centuries.

## 8. Ecclesiastical Organization

The Church is governed episcopally: the **Catholicos-Patriarch**, assisted by the **Holy Synod** of bishops and metropolitans, over dioceses (eparchies) and parishes. The principal jurisdictions include:

| Jurisdiction                                                                                        | Region                                                             |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Patriarchal Province of Seleucia-Ctesiphon                                                          | Ankawa, Erbil, Iraq                                                |
| Archdiocese of Australia, New Zealand and Lebanon                                                   | Oceania and Lebanon (see also the Sydney cathedral of St. Hurmizd) |
| Archdiocese of Syria                                                                                | al-Hasakah, Qamishli, the Khabur villages; Damascus; Aleppo        |
| Archdiocese of India (Chaldean Syrian Church)                                                       | Thrissur, Kerala, and the Gulf                                     |
| Diocese of Iran                                                                                     | Tehran, Urmia, Salmas                                              |
| Diocese of Nohadra and Nineveh                                                                      | Dohuk and the Nineveh Plain                                        |
| Diocese of Baghdad, Russia and Georgia                                                              | Iraq, Caucasus                                                     |
| Dioceses of the Americas, Canada, Scandinavia and Germany, Western Europe, Victoria and New Zealand | Global diaspora                                                    |

The great majority of the faithful now live in the diaspora (the United States, Sweden, Australia, and elsewhere) as a consequence of the persecutions and upheavals of the twentieth and twenty-first centuries; the **Chaldean Syrian Church of India** (in communion since 1999 as a metropolitan diocese) is the largest single diocese.

## 9. Relations with the Catholic Church

### 9.1 The Road to 1994

After the Second Vatican Council — whose decree _Unitatis Redintegratio_ (1964) recognized the ecclesial reality and valid sacraments of the Eastern communities not in full communion (§§ 14–18, esp. §15) — contacts between Rome and the patriarchate of the Church of the East deepened. In 1969 the Holy See invited an observer relationship; the decisive moment came on **11 November 1994**, when **Pope John Paul II** and **Catholicos-Patriarch Mar Dinkha IV** signed the **Common Christological Declaration** in Saint Peter's Basilica. Its central confession:

> As heirs and guardians of the faith received from the Apostles as formulated by our common Fathers in the Nicene Creed, we confess one Lord Jesus Christ, the only Son of God, begotten of the Father from all eternity who, in the fullness of time, came down from heaven and became man for our salvation. […] Therefore our Lord Jesus Christ is true God and true man, perfect in his divinity and perfect in his humanity, consubstantial with the Father and consubstantial with us in all things but sin. His divinity and his humanity are united in one person, without confusion or change, without division or separation.

And on the ancient controversy:

> The controversies of the past led to anathemas, bearing on persons and on formulas. The Lord's Spirit permits us to understand better today that the divisions brought about in this way were due in large part to misunderstandings. […] Living by this faith and these sacraments, it follows as a consequence that the particular Catholic churches and the particular Assyrian churches can recognize each other as **sister Churches**.

The Declaration also established a **Mixed Committee for theological dialogue**, which has met annually since 1995.

### 9.2 The Chaldean-Assyrian Rapprochement

The Declaration opened the way for reconciliation between the two heirs of the ancient Church of the East: on 29 November 1996 Patriarch **Mar Raphaël I Bidawid** (Chaldean Catholic Church) and Patriarch **Mar Dinkha IV** signed common proposals for the re-establishment of ecclesial unity, approved by both Synods on 15 August 1997 in a **Joint Synodal Decree**.

### 9.3 The 2001 Guidelines for Admission to the Eucharist

On 20 July 2001 (promulgated 25 October 2001), the **Pontifical Council for Promoting Christian Unity** — in agreement with the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith and the Congregation for the Eastern Churches — issued the _Guidelines for Admission to the Eucharist Between the Chaldean Church and the Assyrian Church of the East_, providing:

1. **When necessity requires**, Assyrian faithful may participate and receive Holy Communion in a Chaldean celebration, and Chaldean faithful "for whom it is physically or morally impossible to approach a Catholic minister" may receive in an Assyrian celebration (cf. CCEO can. 671 §2–3; _Directory for the Application of Principles and Norms on Ecumenism_ 123, 125).
2. Each Church celebrates according to its own liturgical tradition.
3. When Chaldean faithful participate in an Assyrian celebration, the Assyrian minister "is warmly invited to insert the words of the Institution in the Anaphora of Addai and Mari, as allowed by the Holy Synod of the Assyrian Church of the East" (which granted this option in 1978).
4. The provision concerns **only** the Chaldean and Assyrian Churches and is **not** equivalent to full Eucharistic communion: "both particular Churches are not yet in full communion. They are still travelling, with hope and courage, towards that blessed day when full and visible communion will be attained" (cf. _Ut Unum Sint_, 78).

### 9.4 The Present State and Remaining Obstacles

The two Churches recognize each other as sister Churches sharing one faith in the mystery of the Incarnation, one sacramental life, and one apostolic tradition. What remains for **full visible communion** is chiefly:

- **Ecclesiology**: the full acceptance of the Petrine ministry and primacy of the Bishop of Rome as defined by Vatican I and exercised in the Catholic Church (compare [church-hierarchy.md](church-hierarchy.md));
- **The ecumenical councils**: the dogmatic reception of Ephesus (431) and Chalcedon (451) — and of Constantinople II (553), which condemned Theodore of Mopsuestia and certain writings of Nestorius;
- **The liturgical heritage**: the veneration of Nestorius and Theodore in the calendar and the use of their anaphoras remain delicate questions on both sides.

On the thirtieth anniversary of the Common Christological Declaration, the Pontifical Council for Promoting Christian Unity published _The Catholic Church and the Assyrian Church of the East: 30th Anniversary of the Common Christological Declaration_ (Collana _Ut Unum Sint_ 8, Libreria Editrice Vaticana, 2024), reviewing three decades of dialogue and the shared path toward unity.

**Note on sacramental discipline**: the 2001 provision is mutual **only** between the Chaldean Catholic Church and the Assyrian Church of the East. For the general Catholic norms governing sharing in the Eucharist with Eastern Christians, see the _Code of Canons of the Eastern Churches_ (cann. 670–671) and the _Directory for the Application of Principles and Norms on Ecumenism_ (1993), nn. 122–125. The Assyrian Church itself practices open admission to the baptized of other Churches at its own liturgies, though this courtesy is not reciprocal under Catholic discipline.
