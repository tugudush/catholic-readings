# Receiving Holy Communion: In the Hand vs. on the Tongue

The question of _how_ to receive Holy Communion — whether in the hand or on the tongue — is one of the most frequently discussed topics in contemporary Catholic liturgical practice. This document presents the Church's authoritative teaching: the historical development of both practices, the current canonical and liturgical norms governing the Latin Rite, the distinct traditions of the Eastern Catholic Churches, and the theological principles that must guide every Catholic's approach to the reception of the Most Holy Eucharist.

The central teaching is clear: **Communion in the hand is not intrinsically a desecration**; it is a lawful manner of receiving where permitted by the competent ecclesiastical authority. At the same time, **Communion on the tongue is the universal right of every Catholic**, and no one may be denied the Sacrament for choosing this manner of reception. Both forms call for the same interior faith in the Real Presence and the same exterior reverence befitting the Body, Blood, Soul, and Divinity of Jesus Christ.

---

## 1. The Historical Development

### 1.1 The Earliest Practice: Communion in the Hand

The earliest documented manner of receiving Holy Communion in the Church was **in the hand**. The most famous patristic witness is **St. Cyril of Jerusalem** (c. 313–386), who instructed the newly baptized in his _Catechetical Lectures_ (c. 348):

> "When you approach, do not go stretching out your open hands or having your fingers spread out, but make your left hand a throne for your right hand, since it is about to receive the King, and hollow your palm and receive the Body of Christ, saying 'Amen.' Then, after you have carefully sanctified your eyes by the touch of the Holy Body, consume it, taking care not to lose any particle of it. For if you lose any of it, you suffer a loss, as it were, of one of your own members. Tell me, if anyone gave you grains of gold, would you not hold onto them with all care, taking care not to lose any of them and thus to suffer the loss? Should you not be much more careful, then, to ensure that not a crumb falls away from that which is more precious than gold or precious stones?" (Catechetical Lecture 23, §21–22)

St. Cyril's instruction reveals several important features of the early practice:

- The communicant made a **throne** of the left hand to receive the King of Kings.
- The host was consumed **immediately** and with great care for fragments.
- The gesture expressed profound **reverence** — it was not casual or indifferent.
- The faithful responded **"Amen"** before receiving, as they do today.

Other patristic witnesses confirm Communion in the hand as the ordinary practice of the early centuries: **St. John Chrysostom** (c. 347–407), **St. Ambrose of Milan** (c. 339–397), and **St. Augustine of Hippo** (354–430), who spoke of the Eucharist as the "bread of angels" placed into the hands of the faithful, and of the sin of those who "receive the Body of Christ with polluted hands."

The practice was widespread and unremarkable for the first six or seven centuries of the Church's life. It must be understood, however, in the context of a Church that was still heavily persecuted or recently emerged from persecution, where the faithful carried the Eucharist to the sick and to those in prison, and where a profound sense of the sacred permeated every aspect of Christian life.

### 1.2 The Shift to Communion on the Tongue

By the early Middle Ages, the practice of the Latin Church shifted decisively toward **Communion on the tongue**. Several factors contributed to this development:

1. **Deepening Eucharistic Theology**: The Church's understanding of the Real Presence — that the consecrated bread and wine become, in their very substance, the Body and Blood of Christ (transubstantiation, defined at the Fourth Lateran Council in 1215 and at Trent in 1551) — led to an increasing emphasis on the need for the utmost care in handling the Sacred Species.

2. **The Rise of the Laity Receiving under One Kind**: As the laity increasingly received Communion under the species of bread alone (a practice that became universal in the West by the thirteenth century), the practical risks associated with the chalice disappeared, but the concern for fragments of the host remained — and Communion on the tongue, administered by the consecrated hands of the priest, was seen as the surest safeguard.

3. **The Clericalization of the Liturgy**: The growing distinction between the sanctuary (the _presbyterium_) and the nave, and the reserve of handling the Eucharist to ordained ministers, reflected a heightened sense of the sacred. The laity no longer approached the altar to receive standing in the hand; they knelt at the altar rail and received on the tongue.

4. **The Council of Rouen (c. 878)** and later local councils explicitly forbade Communion in the hand for the laity. By the time of **Pope St. Gregory the Great** (590–604), Communion on the tongue was already the norm in Rome, and it became universal in the West for over a millennium.

This discipline was not considered a rejection of the ancient practice but a prudential development in response to pastoral circumstances — the Church, in her authority to regulate the liturgy, judged that Communion on the tongue better safeguarded reverence for the Eucharist and prevented profanation.

### 1.3 The Restoration of Communion in the Hand (Post–Vatican II)

After the Second Vatican Council, the practice of Communion in the hand was gradually reintroduced in the Latin Church through a series of indults (permissions) granted by the Holy See to individual episcopal conferences. The first such indult was granted to **Germany** in 1969, followed by many other countries.

The key document governing this reintroduction is the 1969 instruction **_Memoriale Domini_** of the Congregation for Divine Worship, which:

- Affirmed that Communion on the tongue "must certainly be retained" as the universal norm of the Latin Church.
- Acknowledged that in certain regions the practice of Communion in the hand had already been introduced without authorization.
- Established a procedure by which an episcopal conference could, by a two-thirds majority vote and with the _recognitio_ (approval) of the Apostolic See, permit Communion in the hand within its territory.
- Emphasized that the new manner must not diminish reverence, and that the faithful must be properly catechized to avoid any loss of faith in the Real Presence or any irreverence toward the Sacrament.

The instruction concluded:

> "In view of the state of the Church as a whole today, this manner of distributing Holy Communion must not be imposed but must be introduced gradually, with proper catechesis, and in such a way that it is not regarded as a necessary consequence of the liturgical reform." (_Memoriale Domini_, 1969)

## 2. Current Norms and Discipline

### 2.1 The Universal Right to Communion on the Tongue

The authoritative 2004 instruction **_Redemptionis Sacramentum_** (Congregation for Divine Worship and the Discipline of the Sacraments, no. 92) states:

> "Although each of the faithful always has the right to receive Holy Communion on the tongue, at his choice, if any communicant should wish to receive the Sacrament in the hand, in areas where the Bishops' Conference with the _recognitio_ of the Apostolic See has given permission, the sacred host is to be administered to him or her. However, special care should be taken to ensure that the host is consumed by the communicant in the presence of the minister, so that no one goes away carrying the Eucharistic species in his hand. If there is a risk of profanation, then Holy Communion should not be given in the hand to the faithful."

This paragraph establishes several critical principles:

| Principle                                     | Explanation                                                                                                                                                            |
| --------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Right to the tongue**                       | Every Catholic, without exception, has the right to receive Communion on the tongue. No priest, deacon, or extraordinary minister may refuse this manner of reception. |
| **In the hand is permitted where authorized** | Communion in the hand is lawful only where the episcopal conference has obtained Rome's _recognitio_. Where permission has not been granted, it may not be practiced.  |
| **Immediate consumption**                     | The host must be consumed in the presence of the minister. Walking away with the Host unconsumed is forbidden.                                                         |
| **Risk of profanation**                       | Where there is a genuine danger of the Eucharist being carried away, lost, or used for profane purposes, the minister must not distribute Communion in the hand.       |

### 2.2 The Manner of Receiving in the Hand

The **General Instruction of the Roman Missal** (GIRM 160) specifies the reverent manner of receiving in the hand:

> "The communicant replies _Amen_, and then receives the Sacrament either on the tongue or, where this is allowed and if the communicant so chooses, in the hand. When receiving Holy Communion in the hand, the communicant should place the left hand upon the right hand (or, for those who are left-handed, the right hand upon the left), approach the minister, receive the Body of the Lord, and then consume it immediately before returning to the place."

The communicant should:

- Make a **bow of the head** (or genuflection, according to local custom) as a gesture of reverence before receiving.
- Make the left hand a **throne** for the right, echoing St. Cyril of Jerusalem's ancient instruction.
- Respond **"Amen"** — a personal affirmation of faith in the Real Presence.
- Receive the Host in the palm and consume it **immediately and carefully**, ensuring no particle remains.
- Never carry the Host away from the place of distribution.

### 2.3 The Manner of Receiving on the Tongue

When receiving on the tongue, the communicant:

- Approaches the minister, makes the prescribed gesture of reverence (bow or genuflection).
- Responds **"Amen."**
- Extends the tongue sufficiently for the minister to place the Host upon it, without biting, snapping, or reaching for the Host with the lips.
- Allows the minister to place the Host and withdraws after receiving.

The GIRM and _Redemptionis Sacramentum_ make clear that this remains the **ordinary and preferred manner** of the universal Church, even where Communion in the hand is also permitted.

### 2.4 The Code of Canon Law

The _Code of Canon Law_ (CIC) addresses the manner of receiving Communion in broad terms:

> "Holy Communion is to be given under the form of bread alone, or under both species according to the norm of the liturgical laws, or even under the form of wine alone in a case of necessity." (CIC 925)

The specific postures and gestures are left to liturgical law (the GIRM and instructions of the Holy See), which the Code itself defers to. The canonical principle is that liturgical law is to be observed faithfully, and no one may add, remove, or change anything in the liturgy on their own authority (CIC 846 §1; _Redemptionis Sacramentum_ 10–11).

## 3. Is Communion in the Hand a Desecration?

### 3.1 The Distinction Between Intrinsic Evil and Circumstantial Abuse

The Church does **not** teach that receiving Communion in the hand is intrinsically a desecration or sacrilege. If it were intrinsically sinful, the Church would have no authority to permit it, since the Church cannot dispense from divine law or authorize what is inherently evil.

What the Church does teach is that Communion in the hand can **become** an occasion of desecration when:

- The host is not consumed immediately but is **carried away** (for later consumption, for display, for use in occult or profane rituals).
- **Particles** of the consecrated host are carelessly dropped, scattered, and lost, without the communicant or minister ensuring that every fragment is reverently consumed.
- The communicant receives with a **lack of faith** in the Real Presence — treating the Host as ordinary bread.
- The manner of receiving is **casual, hurried, or irreverent**, as though receiving a snack rather than the Body of the Lord.

These are **abuses of the permission**, not arguments that the permission itself is invalid. The fault lies not in the gesture but in the disposition and care (or lack thereof) of the communicant.

### 3.2 The Danger of Profanation

_Redemptionis Sacramentum_ (no. 92) is explicit: **"If there is a risk of profanation, then Holy Communion should not be given in the hand to the faithful."**

This is a pastoral judgment to be made by the minister in concrete circumstances. In a parish where the faithful are well catechized and reverent, Communion in the hand ordinarily poses no special risk of profanation. In contexts where the Eucharist has been stolen or abused — for example, at large public Masses where strangers may be present, or where hostile groups are known to seek consecrated hosts for desecration — the minister may prudently decide to distribute only on the tongue.

### 3.3 The Question of Sacrilege

Sacrilege is the violation or unworthy treatment of a sacred person, place, or thing (CCC 2120). To **knowingly and deliberately** handle the Eucharist with contempt, to discard it, or to use it for profane purposes is a **grave sacrilege** and, if the act involves throwing away the consecrated species or retaining them for a sacrilegious purpose, incurs a **_latae sententiae_** (automatic) **excommunication** reserved to the Apostolic See (CIC 1367; CIC 1382 §1).

Receiving Communion in the hand with faith and reverence — immediately consuming the Host, inspecting the palm for particles, and approaching with due preparation — is **not** a sacrilege. It is the lawful reception of the Sacrament in the manner permitted by the Church.

## 4. Eastern Catholic Practice

The **23 Eastern Catholic _sui iuris_ Churches**, while sharing the identical Eucharistic faith with the Latin Church, have their own ancient and distinct traditions for the distribution of Holy Communion:

| Rite Family                                              | Manner of Distribution                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                             |
| -------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Byzantine Rite** (Ukrainian, Ruthenian, Melkite, etc.) | The consecrated Lamb (leavened bread) is cut into small pieces placed in the chalice with the consecrated Wine. The priest administers Holy Communion to the faithful by **intinction** — using a liturgical **spoon** (_lavida_) — placing the commingled Body and Blood directly into the mouth. |
| **Maronite Rite** (Antiochene tradition)                 | Communion is distributed by **intinction**: the host is dipped in the Precious Blood and placed on the tongue. In some places, the faithful receive the Body in the hand and then drink from the chalice, but intinction is the traditional and preferred manner.                                  |
| **Armenian Rite**                                        | The faithful receive the Body of Christ in the mouth, followed by the Precious Blood from the chalice (or by intinction in some places).                                                                                                                                                           |
| **Chaldean and Syro-Malabar Rites**                      | Distribution is by intinction on the tongue.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                       |
| **Coptic and Ethiopic Rites**                            | Communion is distributed on the tongue, the Body and Blood administered separately or by intinction.                                                                                                                                                                                               |

The **Code of Canons of the Eastern Churches** (CCEO) establishes the general norm:

> "The Sacraments are to be administered according to the liturgical prescriptions of the liturgical books." (CCEO 671 §1)

> "In the administration of the Divine Eucharist, the liturgical prescriptions of the proper liturgical books are to be observed." (CCEO 712)

The Eastern tradition does not know Communion in the hand as a historical practice for the laity during the Divine Liturgy. The use of the spoon in the Byzantine tradition developed precisely to safeguard the reverent distribution of the Eucharist and to prevent the loss of particles — the same pastoral concern that led the West to Communion on the tongue.

This difference of discipline between East and West is a legitimate expression of the Church's catholicity: the one faith, celebrated in distinct liturgical traditions, each with its own safeguards for the reverence due to the Holy Mysteries.

## 5. Theological Principles

### 5.1 Distinguishing Levels of Authority

It is essential to distinguish what is of divine law from what is of ecclesiastical discipline:

| Level                                    | Content                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                        | Authority                                             |
| ---------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| **Divine Revelation / Dogma**            | Christ is truly, really, and substantially present — Body, Blood, Soul, and Divinity — under the appearances of bread and wine (Council of Trent, Session XIII, Decree on the Eucharist, can. 1; CCC 1374–1377).                                                                                                                                               | De fide — unchangeable, binding on all the faithful.  |
| **Apostolic Tradition**                  | The Eucharist is the sacrificial memorial of Christ's Passover, instituted at the Last Supper and handed down through apostolic succession (CCC 1356–1361; Council of Trent, Session XXII).                                                                                                                                                                    | De fide — part of the deposit of faith.               |
| **Ecclesiastical Discipline**            | The manner of receiving Communion (in the hand or on the tongue; kneeling or standing; under one kind or both kinds) is regulated by the Church's liturgical law and is subject to change by competent authority (GIRM 160; _Redemptionis Sacramentum_ 92; CIC 925).                                                                                           | Disciplinary — binding while in force but changeable. |
| **Theological Opinion / Personal Piety** | Arguments that one manner is "more reverent" or "safer" than the other, or prudential preferences for the tongue based on concern about particles, are matters of theological opinion and personal devotion, not binding doctrine. Catholics are free to hold these opinions, but they may not treat them as dogma or judge the orthodoxy of those who differ. | Theological opinion — not binding.                    |

### 5.2 The Primacy of Interior Disposition

External gestures — whether receiving in the hand or on the tongue, kneeling or standing — are **signs** of interior reverence. They are important, but they are not the substance of reverence. The Church teaches that what matters most is:

- **Faith** in the Real Presence: the conviction that this is no ordinary bread but the Body of Christ (CCC 1374).
- **Freedom from grave sin**: the communicant must be in a state of grace, having received sacramental absolution for any mortal sins committed since Baptism (CCC 1385; CIC 916).
- **The Eucharistic fast**: abstaining from food and drink (except water and medicine) for at least one hour before receiving Holy Communion (CIC 919).
- **Proper preparation and thanksgiving**: approaching the altar with devotion, making an act of faith and humility before receiving, and spending time in grateful prayer after Communion (CCC 1386–1387).

The external gesture, important as it is, is subordinate to this interior preparation. St. Augustine's well-known admonition applies to both manners of reception:

> "No one eats that flesh unless first he adores it. ... We sin if we do not adore." (Augustine, _Enarrationes in Psalmos_ 98, §9; cited in CCC 1378)

## 6. Pastoral Considerations

### 6.1 The Right to Choose

No Catholic should feel pressured to receive in a manner that troubles their conscience. The law of the Church is clear: the faithful may **choose** either manner (where both are permitted), and no one may be refused Communion for choosing one over the other. A priest or extraordinary minister who denies Communion on the tongue to a communicant who requests it violates a universal liturgical right and acts against the explicit norm of _Redemptionis Sacramentum_.

### 6.2 Hygiene and Health Concerns

In times of public health crisis (as during the COVID-19 pandemic), bishops may temporarily restrict Communion to the hand for prudential reasons, invoking the principle of the common good. Such restrictions are disciplinary, temporary, and must be lifted when the danger passes. Even in such circumstances, the right to the tongue cannot be permanently abolished, and individual accommodations should be made for those whose conscience requires it.

### 6.3 Fragments and Particles

One of the most serious concerns raised about Communion in the hand is the danger of **particles** — tiny fragments of the consecrated host — being lost. The Church's faith is that Christ is entirely present in every particle of the consecrated species, however small. The _General Instruction of the Roman Missal_ instructs the priest to purify the sacred vessels and his own fingers carefully (GIRM 278–280), and the same care must extend to the communicant who receives in the hand: after consuming the Host, the communicant should inspect the palm for any remaining particle and reverently consume it.

This is a matter not of scrupulosity but of **realistic piety**: if one chooses to receive in the hand, one accepts the responsibility to ensure that no fragment of the Lord's Body is lost.

### 6.4 The Witness of the Saints

Many saints and popes of recent centuries have personally preferred and recommended Communion on the tongue as a sign of deeper reverence. **Pope St. John Paul II** wrote:

> "To touch the sacred species and to distribute them with their own hands is a privilege of the ordained." (_Dominicae Cenae_, 1980, §11)

**Pope Benedict XVI**, from the time of his priestly ministry through his pontificate, consistently distributed Communion only on the tongue to kneeling communicants, as a visible witness to the reverence due to the Eucharist. This practice was not a canonical requirement imposed on the universal Church but a personal and liturgical choice — a "sign," as his master of ceremonies explained, of emphasizing the sacredness of the moment and the primacy of the gratuitous gift.

**St. Teresa of Calcutta (Mother Teresa)** was famously distressed by what she perceived as a loss of reverence toward the Eucharist, attributing it in part to the manner of reception. Her sisters, the Missionaries of Charity, continue to receive on the tongue as a sign of their devotion.

These witnesses are powerful and worthy of respect, but they do not constitute a binding norm. They represent the prudential judgment of holy men and women that, in the conditions of the modern world, Communion on the tongue is a safer and more expressive way to manifest faith in the Real Presence. Catholics are free to consider and adopt this judgment — but they are not bound to it under pain of sin.

## 7. Summary

| Question                                              | Answer                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                   |
| ----------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Is Communion in the hand a desecration?**           | No, not intrinsically. It is a lawful manner of receiving where the Church permits it and when done with faith and reverence. It becomes an abuse or occasion of desecration only when the host is not consumed immediately, particles are lost, or it is received without faith in the Real Presence.                   |
| **Is Communion on the tongue always permitted?**      | Yes. Every Catholic has the **universal right** to receive on the tongue, at his or her choice (_Redemptionis Sacramentum_ 92). No one may be refused.                                                                                                                                                                   |
| **Which manner is more reverent?**                    | The Church's discipline permits both, and both can be done reverently. The Church's universal norm and preference remains Communion on the tongue (_Memoriale Domini_). Prudential arguments that one manner is safer or more expressive of faith are legitimate theological opinions but are not matters of divine law. |
| **What if a priest refuses Communion on the tongue?** | The priest violates a universal liturgical norm and the communicant's right. The matter should be raised charitably with the pastor, and if unresolved, with the diocesan bishop.                                                                                                                                        |
| **Do Eastern Catholics receive in the hand?**         | No. The historical and universal practice of the Eastern Catholic Churches is Communion on the tongue by intinction (the spoon in the Byzantine Rite, the dipped host in the Maronite and other Eastern rites). Communion in the hand for the laity is not part of the Eastern liturgical tradition.                     |
| **What matters most?**                                | Faith in the Real Presence, the state of grace, proper preparation, and the care taken to ensure no particle of the Sacred Species is lost or profaned. The external gesture is the servant of interior adoration, not its substitute.                                                                                   |
