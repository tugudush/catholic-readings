# The Angelus (_Angelus Domini_)

The **Angelus** is a traditional Marian devotional exercise that commemorates the Annunciation of the Angel Gabriel to the Blessed Virgin Mary and the Incarnation of the Word. Its three daily pauses sanctify the morning, midday, and evening by recalling that the eternal Son of God became man in the womb of the Virgin Mary through the power of the Holy Spirit.

The prayer is named from its opening Latin words, _Angelus Domini_ ("The Angel of the Lord"). It is a prayer about Christ's Incarnation, prayed with Mary and through her intercession. The Church recommends it as a venerable form of popular piety, while distinguishing it from the sacraments, the Liturgy of the Hours, and prayers that are universally obligatory.

## 1. Character and Status

The Congregation for Divine Worship and the Discipline of the Sacraments describes the Angelus as a traditional prayer used three times daily: at dawn, midday, and dusk. It calls the prayer's rhythm "quasi-liturgical" because it sanctifies the hours of the day, not because the Angelus is itself a liturgical hour. The same Directory encourages its preservation and promotion while recognizing that modern circumstances can make the customary times difficult ([Directory on Popular Piety and the Liturgy, no. 195](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/4fba701f-fda0-47a5-a2de-2e210d81f770/ref/195)).

| Aspect               | Catholic understanding                                                                                  |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Subject**          | The Annunciation and the Incarnation of the Word                                                        |
| **Structure**        | Three versicle-and-response pairs, each followed by a Hail Mary, then a concluding versicle and Collect |
| **Customary times**  | Morning, noon, and evening; the times are approximate rather than fixed universal clock hours           |
| **Kind of prayer**   | Marian devotional exercise and approved popular piety                                                   |
| **Doctrinal center** | The revealed mystery that the Son of God became man for our salvation                                   |
| **Obligation**       | Not a universal obligation for the lay faithful and not a sacrament or liturgical hour                  |
| **Easter practice**  | The _Regina Caeli_ replaces the Angelus during the Paschal Season                                       |

The Angelus does not present a private revelation or a new doctrine. Its prayerful content comes from Sacred Scripture, the Church's confession of the Incarnation, and the Church's liturgical prayer. Its daily schedule, bell customs, and indulgence belong to ecclesiastical discipline and devotional tradition and can develop without altering the dogma they express.

## 2. The Complete Prayer

The English wording of the Angelus varies among prayer books. The following is the traditional devotional form; the Latin text preserves the standard form used in the Church's indulgence concession.

### English

Begin and end with the Sign of the Cross according to local custom.

```text
V. The Angel of the Lord declared unto Mary.
R. And she conceived of the Holy Spirit.

Hail Mary, full of grace, the Lord is with thee;
blessed art thou among women,
and blessed is the fruit of thy womb, Jesus.
Holy Mary, Mother of God,
pray for us sinners,
now and at the hour of our death. Amen.

V. Behold the handmaid of the Lord.
R. Be it done unto me according to thy word.

Hail Mary, full of grace, the Lord is with thee;
blessed art thou among women,
and blessed is the fruit of thy womb, Jesus.
Holy Mary, Mother of God,
pray for us sinners,
now and at the hour of our death. Amen.

V. And the Word was made flesh.
R. And dwelt among us.

Hail Mary, full of grace, the Lord is with thee;
blessed art thou among women,
and blessed is the fruit of thy womb, Jesus.
Holy Mary, Mother of God,
pray for us sinners,
now and at the hour of our death. Amen.

V. Pray for us, O holy Mother of God.
R. That we may be made worthy of the promises of Christ.

Let us pray.
Pour forth, we beseech thee, O Lord, thy grace into our hearts;
that we, to whom the Incarnation of Christ thy Son
was made known by the message of an angel,
may by his Passion and Cross
be brought to the glory of his Resurrection.
Through the same Christ our Lord. Amen.
```

### Latin

```text
benedicta tu in mulieribus,
et benedictus fructus ventris tui, Iesus.
Sancta Maria, Mater Dei,
ora pro nobis peccatoribus,
nunc et in hora mortis nostrae. Amen.

V. Et Verbum caro factum est.
R. Et habitavit in nobis.

Ave, Maria, gratia plena, Dominus tecum;
benedicta tu in mulieribus,
et benedictus fructus ventris tui, Iesus.
Sancta Maria, Mater Dei,
ora pro nobis peccatoribus,
nunc et in hora mortis nostrae. Amen.

V. Ora pro nobis, sancta Dei Genitrix.
R. Ut digni efficiamur promissionibus Christi.

Oremus.
Gratiam tuam, quaesumus, Domine, mentibus nostris infunde;
ut qui, Angelo nuntiante, Christi Filii tui incarnationem cognovimus,
per passionem eius et crucem
ad resurrectionis gloriam perducamur.
Per eundem Christum Dominum nostrum. Amen.
```

The prayer therefore contains three Hail Marys, not three decades of the Rosary. The versicles give the devotion its biblical movement: announcement, consent, and the Word made flesh.

## 3. Biblical and Theological Foundations

### 3.1 The Annunciation

The first versicle recalls the angelic announcement to Mary in **Luke 1:26-31**. The response, "And she conceived of the Holy Spirit," is grounded in the angel's explanation that the Holy Spirit would come upon Mary and that the child would be called holy, the Son of God (**Luke 1:35**). The Angelus compresses the Annunciation narrative into a brief profession of the Incarnation.

### 3.2 Mary's Fiat

"Behold the handmaid of the Lord" and "Be it done unto me according to thy word" are drawn from Mary's response in **Luke 1:38**. The Angelus does not treat Mary's consent as an independent source of redemption. It reverently remembers her free cooperation with God's grace within the one saving plan accomplished by the Father through the Son and in the Holy Spirit.

### 3.3 The Word Made Flesh

"The Word was made flesh" comes from **John 1:14**. The line is a direct confession of the Christian faith: the eternal Word truly became man while remaining what He eternally is, the Son of God. The prayer is therefore Marian in form but Christological in its center.

### 3.4 The Hail Mary

The opening of the Hail Mary combines the Archangel Gabriel's greeting, "Hail, full of grace" (**Luke 1:28**), with Elizabeth's blessing of Mary and the fruit of her womb (**Luke 1:42**). The title **Mother of God** safeguards the truth that the child Mary bore is one divine Person, Jesus Christ, true God and true man. The Catechism explains the scriptural structure and theological meaning of the Hail Mary in [CCC 2676-2679](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/_P9B.HTM), and the title Mother of God in [CCC 495](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/_P1F.HTM).

### 3.5 The Paschal Mystery

The Collect asks that those who have come to know the Incarnation through the angel's message may be brought "by his Passion and Cross" to the glory of the Resurrection. The Angelus therefore does not isolate Christmas from Easter. It joins the beginning of the Incarnation to the whole Paschal Mystery: the Son who takes flesh is the same Lord who suffers, dies, rises, and brings humanity to glory.

This connection is one reason Pope Paul VI said that the Angelus retains its freshness through its biblical character, its quasi-liturgical rhythm, and its openness to the Paschal Mystery ([Marialis Cultus, no. 41](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/c1716af6-053b-489e-a6b0-3e034f6c893c/ref/41)).

## 4. Historical Development

The history of the Angelus should be described with appropriate caution. The custom did not appear all at once in its present form, and no single founder can be established with certainty for every element of the devotion.

| Period or witness               | Historical significance                                                                                                                                         |
| ------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Medieval evening custom**     | A practice of reciting three Hail Marys at the sound of an evening bell became established in parts of Europe.                                                  |
| **Early fourteenth century**    | The Catholic Encyclopedia identifies a 1307 decree of the Provincial Synod of Gran as an early documented witness to an evening bell and three Hail Marys.      |
| **Pope John XXII**              | Documents traditionally dated to 1318 and 1327 are associated with papal recommendation and indulgencing of the evening practice.                               |
| **Later medieval development**  | Morning and midday recitations were added over time; the three-times-daily pattern gradually became customary.                                                  |
| **Modern magisterial guidance** | Paul VI recommended the traditional recitation in _Marialis Cultus_ (1974), and the Directory on Popular Piety and the Liturgy reaffirmed it in no. 195 (2002). |

The Catholic Encyclopedia itself warns that the history is difficult to trace with confidence. Its account is useful as an early historical reference, but it is not a magisterial source and should not be used to assign dogmatic authority to medieval origin stories ([Catholic Encyclopedia, "Angelus"](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/f2c979a8-871d-4f46-a059-320d3b837a76/ref/Angelus)).

### 4.1 The Angelus Bell

The bell traditionally announced the prayer and invited the faithful to stop briefly, remember the Incarnation, and pray for peace and safety. In a parish, monastery, school, or household, the bell can still provide a visible and audible rhythm of prayer. A bell is not, however, the essence of the devotion. The current indulgence concession specifies the pious recitation of the Angelus with its versicles and prayer, not the presence of a bell ([Enchiridion of Indulgences, Concession 17, no. 2](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/b4e30e90-7ed9-4cc5-be4b-305ed07d425b/ref/Concessiones.%2017)).

## 5. Daily Practice

### 5.1 The Three Times

The traditional times are:

1. **Morning or dawn:** The day begins under the sign of the Word's Incarnation and Mary's fiat.
2. **Midday:** Work is interrupted briefly to return attention to God and to the mystery of salvation.
3. **Evening or dusk:** The faithful remember the Incarnation and entrust the night to God through Mary's intercession.

These are customary periods, not universally fixed hours. A person may adapt the recitation to work, school, travel, family duties, or community schedules. Pope Paul VI observed that morning, noon, and evening remain characteristic periods of the day that invite a pause in prayer even when older bell customs are difficult to maintain ([Marialis Cultus, no. 41](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/c1716af6-053b-489e-a6b0-3e034f6c893c/ref/41)).

### 5.2 A Practical Order

1. Make the Sign of the Cross.
2. Pray the first versicle and response.
3. Pray one Hail Mary.
4. Pray the second versicle and response, followed by a Hail Mary.
5. Pray the third versicle and response, followed by a Hail Mary.
6. Pray the concluding versicle and response.
7. Pray the Collect.
8. Make the Sign of the Cross if this is part of the local custom.

When circumstances make the full form difficult, the Directory on Popular Piety encourages the preservation of the devotion and mentions at least the recitation of three Hail Marys. This abbreviated practice should not be confused with the full form named in the current indulgence concession, which specifies the Angelus with its versicles and concluding prayer.

### 5.3 Personal, Family, and Communal Prayer

The Angelus can be prayed privately, by a family, or in a community. A parish or religious community may sing the Hail Marys, proclaim the Gospel of the Annunciation, and ring bells on suitable occasions. The Directory specifically commends such solemn recitation in religious communities, Marian shrines, and meetings or conventions (no. 195).

The prayer should be a genuine pause rather than a mechanical interruption. Its purpose is to turn the mind toward the Incarnation, receive the example of Mary's faith, and allow the mystery of Christ to order the ordinary work of the day.

## 6. The Indulgence

The current _Enchiridion Indulgentiarum_ grants a **partial indulgence** to the faithful who piously recite the Angelus with its versicles and prayer at dawn, midday, or evening. During the Paschal Season, the same concession applies to the pious recitation of the _Regina Caeli_ with its customary prayer ([Concession 17, no. 2](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/b4e30e90-7ed9-4cc5-be4b-305ed07d425b/ref/Concessiones.%2017)).

Several distinctions are important:

- The Angelus itself is not a sacrament and does not forgive sins by the mere repetition of words.
- The current concession is **partial**, not plenary.
- The concession calls for the versicles and concluding prayer; a bell is not listed as a requirement.
- Older devotional books may mention historical indulgence amounts or conditions. Those older notices should not be treated as the current law of indulgences without reference to the present _Enchiridion_.
- The general norms governing indulgences remain applicable; the grant does not turn a devotional exercise into a liturgical obligation.

## 7. The _Regina Caeli_ in Eastertide

During the Paschal Season, the _Regina Caeli_ replaces the Angelus. This is not an additional fourth Marian prayer or a version of the Angelus with Hail Marys; it is the Easter antiphon that proclaims the Resurrection. The Roman Liturgy of the Hours also appoints the _Regina Caeli_ as the Marian antiphon at Compline throughout Eastertide ([General Instruction of the Liturgy of the Hours, no. 92](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/6dabf215-26a6-416e-9356-e85952fa84a4/ref/92)).

The substitution ordinarily extends from Easter Sunday through Pentecost Sunday. After the Paschal Season, the Angelus resumes.

### English

```text
Queen of heaven, rejoice, alleluia.
For he whom you did merit to bear, alleluia.
Has risen, as he said, alleluia.
Pray for us to God, alleluia.

V. Rejoice and be glad, O Virgin Mary, alleluia.
R. For the Lord has risen indeed, alleluia.

Let us pray.
O God, who gave joy to the whole world
through the Resurrection of your Son, our Lord Jesus Christ,
grant, we beseech you,
that through the intercession of the Virgin Mary, his Mother,
we may obtain the joys of everlasting life.
Through Christ our Lord. Amen.
```

### Latin

```text
Regina caeli, laetare, alleluia.
Quia quem meruisti portare, alleluia.
Resurrexit, sicut dixit, alleluia.
Ora pro nobis Deum, alleluia.

V. Gaude et laetare, Virgo Maria, alleluia.
R. Quia surrexit Dominus vere, alleluia.

Oremus.
Deus, qui per resurrectionem Filii tui Domini nostri Iesu Christi
mundum laetificare dignatus es,
praesta, quaesumus,
ut per eius Genitricem Virginem Mariam
perpetuae capiamus gaudia vitae.
Per Christum Dominum nostrum. Amen.
```

The _Regina Caeli_ has its own biblical and theological emphasis. The Angelus contemplates the Word becoming flesh; the _Regina Caeli_ celebrates that the same Son has risen. Both prayers lead the faithful through Mary to the mystery of Christ.

## 8. Relationship to Other Catholic Prayer

### 8.1 The Mass and the Liturgy of the Hours

The Angelus complements the Church's liturgy by sanctifying ordinary moments outside the celebration of the Mass or the canonical hours. It cannot replace Sunday Mass, a required liturgical hour, or any sacramental preparation. Its "quasi-liturgical" rhythm means that it serves the sanctification of time in harmony with the liturgy.

### 8.2 The Rosary

Both the Angelus and the Rosary include the Hail Mary, but they have different structures and purposes:

| Angelus                                               | Rosary                                                               |
| ----------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Three Hail Marys                                      | Five decades in each set of mysteries                                |
| Focuses on the Annunciation and Incarnation           | Meditates on the mysteries of Christ and the Blessed Virgin Mary     |
| Traditionally prayed at three points in the day       | Usually prayed as a longer devotional exercise at a chosen time      |
| Concludes with an Incarnation-to-Resurrection Collect | Includes the Our Father, ten Hail Marys, and Glory Be in each decade |

The Angelus can be a small daily companion to the Rosary; one does not replace the other.

## 9. Latin and Eastern Catholic Perspectives

The Angelus developed especially within the Latin Catholic tradition. All Catholic Churches share the faith expressed by it: the Annunciation, the virginal conception of Jesus by the power of the Holy Spirit, the true Incarnation of the Son, and the honored intercession of the Mother of God.

The Angelus is not a universal required devotion in the twenty-four Catholic Churches. Eastern Catholic faithful may adopt it as a private or communal devotion, but their own liturgical and devotional traditions also contain rich Marian forms, including the **Theotokion**, the **Akathist Hymn**, and the **Paraklesis**, according to the particular Church and rite. These differences concern liturgical heritage and devotional discipline, not the Catholic doctrine of the Incarnation or the dignity of the Theotokos.

The Easter _Regina Caeli_ is likewise especially familiar in the Latin tradition. An Eastern Catholic parish may preserve its own Paschal hymnody and Marian offices while remaining fully united with the same Paschal faith.

## 10. Spiritual Meaning

The Angelus gives the day a threefold return to the center of Christian faith:

- **God enters human history:** The eternal Word takes flesh rather than remaining distant from creation.
- **Grace invites a free response:** Mary's fiat models obedient faith without making her the source of divine grace.
- **Christ's whole mystery is one:** The Incarnation leads to the Passion, Cross, Resurrection, and the hope of everlasting life.
- **Ordinary time becomes prayer:** Work, meals, travel, study, and evening rest can be gathered into remembrance of salvation.
- **Mary leads to Christ:** The Hail Mary praises what God has done and asks the Mother of God to pray for us.

The devotion is therefore brief but not slight. Its words are few because its mystery is inexhaustible: God became man, and the faithful are invited to let that truth shape the day.
