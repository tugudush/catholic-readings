# Prayer After Communion

## 1. Overview

The **Prayer After Communion** is a short, contemporary devotional prayer of **thanksgiving after Holy Communion** — a personal colloquy with our Lord Jesus Christ immediately after receiving His Body and Blood in the Most Holy Sacrament. It circulates widely as a prayer-card graphic produced by **Ignatius Catholicum**, a Catholic devotional publisher, and reads:

    Lord Jesus, I thank You
    for coming to me in the
    Most Holy Sacrament.
    May Your Body and Blood
    fortify my soul to live
    according to Your will.
    Fill my heart with
    Your profound peace,
    renew my spirit with
    Your divine love, and
    guide me always in
    Your sacred truth.
    Amen.

This prayer belongs to the venerable Catholic practice of **private thanksgiving after Communion**. It is a **private devotional composition** — not a liturgical text from the Roman Missal and not an indulgenced formula from the _Enchiridion Indulgentiarum_. Nevertheless, it expresses precisely the dispositions the Church recommends after the reception of the Eucharist: gratitude, self-offering to God's will, peace, love, and fidelity to the truth.

The Catholic tradition also possesses a rich treasury of **older, more widely established prayers after Communion** — above all the _Anima Christi_, the _Suscipe_ of St. Ignatius of Loyola, and the Prayer of St. Thomas Aquinas — which are presented in full in § 5 below.

## 2. The Prayer

    Lord Jesus, I thank You
    for coming to me in the
    Most Holy Sacrament.
    May Your Body and Blood
    fortify my soul to live
    according to Your will.
    Fill my heart with
    Your profound peace,
    renew my spirit with
    Your divine love, and
    guide me always in
    Your sacred truth.
    Amen.

_(Prayer circulated by: Ignatius Catholicum)_

### Structure of the Prayer

The prayer unfolds in four movements, mirroring the classical pattern of **thanksgiving, petition, and self-surrender** recommended in traditional methods of thanksgiving after Communion:

| Movement                | Words                                                                            | Grace Implored                                                                                               |
| ----------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **1. Thanksgiving**     | "Lord Jesus, I thank You for coming to me in the Most Holy Sacrament."           | Gratitude for the Real Presence received                                                                     |
| **2. Fortitude**        | "May Your Body and Blood fortify my soul to live according to Your will."        | Strength to live in conformity with God's will — recalling the Eucharistic promise of "food for the journey" |
| **3. Interior Renewal** | "Fill my heart with Your profound peace, renew my spirit with Your divine love…" | Peace of heart and renewal in charity                                                                        |
| **4. Guidance**         | "…and guide me always in Your sacred truth."                                     | Fidelity to the truth of Christ and His Church                                                               |

The closing "Amen." seals the prayer as an act of faith in what has been asked.

## 3. The Source: Ignatius Catholicum

**Ignatius Catholicum** is a Catholic devotional publisher and social-media account that produces and circulates prayer cards, devotional images, and catechetical graphics — including the prayer reproduced above. The name recalls **St. Ignatius of Loyola** (1491–1556), founder of the Society of Jesus, whose own spirituality placed a special emphasis on prayer after Holy Communion (see the _Anima Christi_ and the _Suscipe_, § 5 below).

As a modern devotional composition of **private authorship**, the prayer:

- Is **not** part of the liturgical books of the Church (neither the Roman Missal nor the Divine Office);
- Is **not** a formula indulgenced in the _Enchiridion Indulgentiarum_;
- Carries the authority of a **pious private devotion**, which the faithful are free to use but are never obliged to pray;
- Is theologically sound in every particular, and may fittingly be prayed during the period of silent thanksgiving after Communion prescribed by the rubrics.

## 4. The Practice of Thanksgiving after Communion: Theological Foundation

Thanksgiving after Holy Communion is an ancient and strongly recommended practice rooted in the very meaning of the Eucharist.

### 4.1 The Eucharist Is Itself Thanksgiving

The very name **Eucharist** comes from the Greek εὐχαριστία (_eucharistia_), "thanksgiving." The celebration of the Mass is the Church's supreme act of gratitude to the Father through Christ. The _Compendium of the Catechism of the Catholic Church_ teaches that the Church gives thanks continually, "above all in celebrating the Eucharist in which Christ allows her to participate in his own thanksgiving to the Father" (_Compendium_, 555). The communicant's personal prayer after Communion is a **personal continuation and interior appropriation** of the thanksgiving already offered by the whole Church in the sacred Liturgy.

### 4.2 Christ Remains Present within the Communicant

The Baltimore Catechism taught:

> "We should spend sufficient time in Thanksgiving after Holy Communion to show due reverence to the Blessed Sacrament; for Our Lord is personally with us as long as the appearance of bread and wine remains." (_Baltimore Catechism No. 3_, q. 914)

Because the sacramental presence of Christ endures as long as the Eucharistic species remain, the moments immediately following Communion are an exceptional opportunity for intimate converse with the Lord dwelling within the soul.

### 4.3 The Liturgical Rubrics Provide for This Prayer

- The **General Instruction of the Roman Missal** directs that, after the distribution of Communion, "a sacred silence may be observed for a while, or a psalm or other canticle of praise or a hymn may be sung" (GIRM 88), and instructs that "the sacred silence" is to be observed "as a part of the celebration" (GIRM 45).
- When Communion is distributed outside Mass, "a period of silence may be observed" for private prayer (GIRM 271).
- Pope Pius XII, in **_Mediator Dei_** (1947), urged that after receiving Holy Communion the faithful "give thanks to God" and warned against those who would "belittle" private thanksgiving after Communion (_Mediator Dei_, 123–124).

### 4.4 The Indulgence for Thanksgiving after Communion

The current **_Enchiridion Indulgentiarum_** (4th ed., 1999, _Concessio_ 8) grants a **partial indulgence** to the faithful who, using any legitimately approved formula, make **an act of thanksgiving after Communion** — citing the _Anima Christi_ and the _En ego, o bone et dulcissime Iesu_ as examples, both of which appear in the Roman Missal's appendix of prayers of thanksgiving after Mass (_Gratiarum actio post Missam_). A **plenary indulgence** is granted, on the usual conditions, to those who on the Fridays of Lent recite the _En ego_ before an image of Christ crucified **after Communion** (_Enchiridion Indulgentiarum_, _Concessio_ 8 § 1, 2°).

## 5. Other Popular and Common Prayers after Communion

Beyond the modern devotional prayer above, the Catholic tradition offers a treasury of classic prayers after Communion. The following are the most widely used.

### 5.1 The Anima Christi ("Soul of Christ")

The **Anima Christi** is the most famous prayer after Communion in the Western tradition. Although traditionally attributed to **St. Ignatius of Loyola**, who placed it at the head of his _Spiritual Exercises_, the prayer is in fact **older than the Saint**: it dates from the first half of the fourteenth century, is found in manuscripts of c. 1370, and was enriched with indulgences by **Pope John XXII in the year 1330** (_Catholic Encyclopedia_, "Anima Christi"). Under the older _Raccolta_ it carried an indulgence of 300 days each recitation, 7 years when said after Holy Communion, and a plenary indulgence (on the usual conditions) for those who recited it daily for a month (S. Poenit., 9 January 1854). In the current _Enchiridion Indulgentiarum_ it remains the **first-named formula for the partial indulgence attached to thanksgiving after Communion**.

    Soul of Christ, sanctify me.
    Body of Christ, save me.
    Blood of Christ, inebriate me.
    Water from the side of Christ, wash me.
    Passion of Christ, strengthen me.
    O good Jesus, hear me.
    Within Thy wounds hide me.
    Suffer me not to be separated from Thee.
    From the malignant enemy defend me.
    At the hour of my death call me,
    and bid me come to Thee,
    that with Thy saints I may praise Thee
    for ever and ever. Amen.

**Latin original:**

    Anima Christi, sanctifica me.
    Corpus Christi, salva me.
    Sanguis Christi, inebria me.
    Aqua lateris Christi, lava me.
    Passio Christi, conforta me.
    O bone Iesu, exaudi me.
    Intra tua vulnera absconde me.
    Ne permittas me separari a te.
    Ab hoste maligno defende me.
    In hora mortis meae voca me,
    et iube me venire ad te,
    ut cum Sanctis tuis laudem te
    in saecula saeculorum. Amen.

### 5.2 The Suscipe of St. Ignatius of Loyola

The **_Suscipe_** ("Take, Lord, and receive") is the act of total self-surrender from the _Spiritual Exercises_ of St. Ignatius of Loyola (nn. 234–237). Composed for the "Contemplation for Obtaining Love," it is frequently used as a prayer of **offering after Holy Communion**, in which the soul returns to God every gift received, above all the Gift of Himself in the Eucharist:

    Take, Lord, and receive all my liberty,
    my memory, my understanding, and my entire will —
    all I have and call my own.
    You have given all to me.
    To You, Lord, I return it.
    Everything is Yours; do with it what You will.
    Give me only Your love and Your grace,
    for that is enough for me. Amen.

### 5.3 The Prayer of St. Thomas Aquinas after Communion

The great **thanksgiving of St. Thomas Aquinas** (1225–1274), the Angelic Doctor, is a classical prayer of gratitude and petition traditionally said after Holy Communion. It asks that the sacrament received may be "not a condemnation to punishment, but a saving intercession unto pardon," and that it may work in the soul the increase of every virtue and union with God:

    I thank You, Lord, holy Father,
    almighty everlasting God,
    who have deigned, not because of my merits
    but solely out of the condescension of Your mercy,
    to satisfy me, a sinner, Your unworthy servant,
    with the precious Body and Blood
    of Your Son, our Lord Jesus Christ.
    I pray that this holy Communion
    may not be for me a condemnation to punishment,
    but a saving intercession for pardon.
    May it be for me the armor of faith
    and the shield of good will.
    May it be the emptying out of my vices,
    the extinction of my concupiscence and lusts,
    the increase within me of charity and patience,
    of humility and obedience, and of all virtues;
    a firm defense against the snares of all my enemies,
    visible and invisible;
    the perfect quieting of all my evil impulses,
    both of the flesh and of the spirit;
    a firm union with You, the one true God;
    and a happy consummation of my end.
    And I beseech You that You would vouchsafe
    to bring me, a sinner,
    to that ineffable banquet
    where You, with Your Son and the Holy Spirit,
    are to Your saints true light,
    full satisfaction, everlasting joy,
    consummate gladness, and perfect happiness.
    Through the same Christ our Lord. Amen.

### 5.4 En ego, o bone et dulcissime Iesu (The Prayer before a Crucifix)

The **"Behold, O good and most sweet Jesus"** — long attributed to St. Francis de Sales and traditionally prayed before a crucifix — is also a classic thanksgiving after Communion. The current _Enchiridion Indulgentiarum_ cites it (with the _Anima Christi_) as an approved formula for the **partial indulgence** attached to thanksgiving after Communion, and grants a **plenary indulgence** for reciting it on the **Fridays of Lent** before an image of Christ crucified **after receiving Communion** (_Concessio_ 8):

    Behold, O good and most sweet Jesus,
    I cast myself upon my knees in Your sight,
    and with the most fervent desire of my soul
    I pray and beseech You
    that You would impress upon my heart
    lively sentiments of faith, hope, and charity,
    with true repentance for my sins
    and a firm purpose of amendment;
    while with deep affection and grief of soul
    I ponder within myself
    and mentally contemplate Your five most precious wounds,
    having before my eyes the words
    which David the Prophet put on Your lips concerning You, O good Jesus:
    "They have dug My hands and My feet;
    they have numbered all My bones." (Psalm 21:17–18)

### 5.5 The Transfige of St. Bonaventure

The **_Transfige_** ("Pierce, O most sweet Lord Jesus") is a brief, ardent prayer of love traditionally attributed to **St. Bonaventure** (c. 1217–1274), the Seraphic Doctor, and often used after Communion as an act of loving union with the Lord received:

    Pierce, O most sweet Lord Jesus,
    my inmost soul with the most joyous and healthful wound of Your love,
    with true, serene, and most holy apostolic charity,
    that my soul may ever languish and melt
    with entire love and longing for You alone,
    and yearn for You in all things.
    May You be my food and my drink,
    my love and my delight,
    my sweetness and my joy. Amen.

### 5.6 The Adoro te devote of St. Thomas Aquinas

The **_Adoro te devote_** ("Devoutly I adore You") is one of the great Eucharistic hymns of St. Thomas Aquinas, composed for the Feast of Corpus Christi. Its closing stanzas, which express the soul's longing for the Beatific Vision, are a fitting close to a period of thanksgiving after Communion:

    Iesu, quem velatum nunc aspicio,
    oro fiat illud quod tam sitio:
    ut te revelata cernens facie,
    visu sim beatus tuae gloriae. Amen.

    Jesus, whom now I see hidden,
    I pray that what I so thirst for may come about:
    that seeing Your face revealed,
    I may be blessed with the vision of Your glory. Amen.

## 6. How and When to Pray after Communion

The Church does not prescribe any single formula; the important obligation is to **give God fitting thanksgiving** in recollection, avoiding needless haste. The following order, drawn from the traditional methods of thanksgiving, is widely recommended:

1. **Immediate adoration and thanksgiving** — a brief act of faith in the Real Presence of Christ within the soul (e.g., "My Lord and my God" — cf. John 20:28).
2. **The _Anima Christi_** — the classic prayer of thanksgiving, said kneeling after returning to one's place.
3. **Personal colloquy** — a period of silent conversation with Jesus, presenting to Him one's needs, one's family, one's resolutions, and the needs of the Church and the world.
4. **A formal prayer of offering** — the _Suscipe_ of St. Ignatius or the Prayer of St. Thomas Aquinas, surrendering one's whole life to God.
5. **A Marian aspiration** — commending the reception of the Lord to the Blessed Virgin Mary (who first received Him), e.g., the _Memorare_ or a Hail Mary.

The prayer in § 2 above may fittingly be used in steps 1 or 4, and may be followed or preceded by the traditional formulas in § 5 according to one's devotion.

## 7. Eastern Catholic Dimension

The practice of **thanksgiving after Holy Communion** is universal in the Catholic Church, East and West, though it takes different liturgical forms.

- In the **Byzantine Divine Liturgy**, the communicants remain in prayer after receiving the Sacred Gifts while the priest consumes the remainder of the Precious Blood; the choir sings "We have seen the true Light, we have received the Heavenly Spirit." The faithful are then invited to give thanks, and the deacon prays: "Upright, having partaken of the Immortal, Holy, Pure, and Immortal, Heavenly, Life-giving, and Awful Mysteries of Christ, let us worthily give thanks to the Lord."
- In the Byzantine tradition, a set of **Prayers of Thanksgiving after Holy Communion** (from the _Orologion_ / Book of Hours) is appointed to be read after the Liturgy, paralleling the Latin practice of the _Gratiarum actio post Missam_.
- The **Catechism of the Ukrainian Catholic Church, _Christ – Our Pascha_** teaches that after receiving the Eucharist the faithful should "remain in thanksgiving and prayer," because "Christ the Savior has united Himself with the faithful in the Divine Eucharist" (_Christ – Our Pascha_, 375, 814).

The prayers in § 5 are Latin-rite formulas; Eastern Catholics may equally make use of their own thanksgiving prayers from the _Orologion_, or any approved formula, in keeping with the same theological reality of Christ's abiding presence.

## 8. Levels of Authority

| Element                                                                                                                                               | Level of Authority                                                                                              |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| The **Real Presence** of Christ in the Eucharist, Body, Blood, Soul, and Divinity, and the obligation of reverence toward it                          | **Dogma** (Council of Trent, Session XIII; cf. CCC 1373–1381)                                                   |
| The **duty and desirability of thanksgiving after Communion**; the rubrical provision of sacred silence                                               | **Magisterial teaching / liturgical discipline** (GIRM 45, 88, 271; _Mediator Dei_, 123–124; _Compendium_, 555) |
| The **partial indulgence** for an act of thanksgiving after Communion; the **plenary indulgence** on Fridays of Lent for the _En ego_ after Communion | **Ecclesiastical discipline** (_Enchiridion Indulgentiarum_, _Concessio_ 8)                                     |
| The **traditional prayers** after Communion (_Anima Christi_, _Suscipe_, Prayer of St. Thomas Aquinas, _En ego_, _Transfige_, _Adoro te devote_)      | **Pious tradition** (indulgenced at various periods; freely used, never obligatory)                             |
| The **modern devotional prayer of Ignatius Catholicum** (§ 2)                                                                                         | **Private devotion** (no official approval status; theologically sound and freely usable)                       |
