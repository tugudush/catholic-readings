# Fasting & Abstinence

The Catholic discipline of **fasting** (_ieiunium_) and **abstinence** (_abstinentia_) is one of the oldest and most misunderstood elements of the Christian life. This document presents the full Catholic doctrine and practice of fasting: its biblical foundations, its development from the Apostolic age through the Fathers, the present law of both the Latin and the Eastern Catholic Churches, its spiritual meaning, and pastoral guidance for its observance. It distinguishes carefully between what is **divine law**, what is **ecclesiastical discipline**, and what is **voluntary devotion**.

For the fasting and abstinence norms of each liturgical season, see [liturgy/liturgical-calendar.md](liturgical-calendar.md). For the Eucharistic fast observed before receiving Holy Communion, see [liturgy/order-of-the-mass.md](order-of-the-mass.md).

---

## 1. Two Distinct Obligations: Fasting and Abstinence

In Catholic terminology the two words are technical and must never be conflated:

| Term           | Latin         | What it limits         | The rule                                                                                                    |
| -------------- | ------------- | ---------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Fasting**    | _Ieiunium_    | The **amount** of food | One full meal a day, plus two smaller meals that together do not equal a full meal; no eating between meals |
| **Abstinence** | _Abstinentia_ | The **kind** of food   | The flesh of warm-blooded animals (meat); fish, shellfish, eggs, and dairy are **not** meat                 |

Both are forms of **penance**. The Church binds the faithful to a common observance of penance "especially through fast and abstinence" ([Code of Canon Law, canon 1249](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/927224e3-8c2d-44ed-a9fb-dc736030081d/ref/1249)), and both are joined by tradition to the other two pillars of Christian conversion: **prayer** and **almsgiving** ([CCC 1434](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/1434)).

A day of **abstinence** is not necessarily a day of **fast** — on an ordinary Friday one may eat as much as one likes, provided one eats no meat. A day of **fast** limits quantity but does not of itself exclude any class of food. Ash Wednesday and Good Friday are days of **both** fasting and abstinence.

## 2. Levels of Doctrinal Authority

Fasting must be understood at two distinct levels of authority, and confusing them produces both scrupulosity and laxity:

1. **The duty of penance — divine law.** The obligation of conversion and penance for sin is rooted in divine revelation itself. Our Lord's first proclamation was "Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand" (Matthew 4:17), and He presupposed fasting as a normal practice of His disciples (Matthew 6:16–18). The Council of Trent taught that penance after sin is necessary for salvation (Session XIV, on the Sacrament of Penance), and the Catechism presents the fourth precept of the Church as serving this divinely grounded duty: it "ensures the times of ascesis and penance which prepare us for the liturgical feasts and help us acquire mastery over our instincts and freedom of heart" ([CCC 2043](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/2043)). No Catholic may conclude that penance is merely optional.

2. **The particular days and manner — ecclesiastical discipline.** _Which_ days are days of fasting and abstinence, _what exactly_ they require, and _who_ is bound are matters of positive ecclesiastical law, determined by the Church's authority and therefore mutable. The law has changed across the centuries — from the stricter discipline of the [1917 Code](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0c4de460-291d-4922-807a-94a54b5715d8/ref/1252), through the simplification of Pope Paul VI's _Paenitemini_ (1966), to the present [1983 Code of Canon Law, canons 1249–1253](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/927224e3-8c2d-44ed-a9fb-dc736030081d/ref/1251). The **binding obligation** of the fast is not in doubt; the **precise content** of the law is what the Church legislates and may revise.

Fasting on particular days is therefore **not dogma**. It is a sacred discipline — serious, binding under the Church's authority, and never to be treated lightly — but it is not immutable divine law. This is why the Church can and does dispense from it, and why the obligation can be commuted for pastoral reasons ([canon 1245](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/927224e3-8c2d-44ed-a9fb-dc736030081d/ref/1245)).

## 3. Biblical Foundations

### 3.1 The Old Testament

Fasting in the Old Testament appears in several closely related contexts: **mourning and repentance**, **supplication in crisis**, **preparation for divine revelation**, **intercession for the community**, and **renewal of the covenant**. It is almost always accompanied by prayer, sackcloth, ashes, and an appeal to God's mercy.

- **The Day of Atonement (Yom Kippur)** — the one fast commanded by the Law of Moses: "It shall be to you a sabbath of solemn rest, and you shall afflict yourselves" (Leviticus 16:31; cf. 23:27–32). This is the model of the annual solemn fast.
- **The fasts of Moses and Elijah** — each fasted forty days and forty nights before receiving divine revelation (Exodus 34:28; 1 Kings 19:8). St. Athanasius draws the Christian lesson: "Moses received the Law when he was fasting; and so Peter when fasting was taught the grace of the New Testament" (_Letter 63_, 16).
- **Fasts of supplication and mourning** — David fasted in mourning and intercession (2 Samuel 1:12; 12:16); Esther called a three-day fast of her people before approaching the king (Esther 4:16); Daniel fasted and prayed for the restoration of Israel (Daniel 9:3; 10:3); the people of Nineveh fasted in repentance at the preaching of Jonah (Jonah 3:5–10).
- **The four annual fasts** — commemorating the fall of Jerusalem (Zechariah 8:19; cf. Jeremiah 52).
- **The prophets' critique** — the prophets insist that external fasting is worthless without interior conversion and justice. Isaiah 58:1–12 is the classical passage: the fast the Lord chooses is "to loose the bonds of wickedness, to undo the thongs of the yoke, to let the oppressed go free… to share your bread with the hungry, and bring the homeless poor into your house" (Isaiah 58:6–7). Fasting is not magic and never a substitute for obedience.
- **Tobit** — "Prayer is good when accompanied by fasting, almsgiving, and righteousness" (Tobit 12:8), a text that shaped the Church's pairing of the three.

By the time of Christ, the devout Jew fasted **twice weekly**, on Mondays and Thursdays (Luke 18:12, the Pharisee's boast).

### 3.2 The New Testament

- **Our Lord's forty-day fast** — Jesus Himself fasted forty days and forty nights in the wilderness before His public ministry (Matthew 4:1–11; Mark 1:12–13; Luke 4:1–13). This is the pattern of the Church's forty-day Lent, and it shows fasting as **spiritual combat** against the Tempter (cf. Matthew 17:21: "this kind never comes out except by prayer and fasting").
- **The Sermon on the Mount** — Jesus assumes His disciples will fast: "When you fast, do not look dismal, like the hypocrites… But when you fast, anoint your head and wash your face, that your fasting may not be seen by men but by your Father who is in secret" (Matthew 6:16–18). Fasting is to be discreet, humble, and directed to God alone.
- **The Bridegroom saying** — when the disciples of John ask why His disciples do not fast, Jesus answers: "The wedding guests cannot mourn as long as the bridegroom is with them, can they? The days will come, when the bridegroom is taken away from them, and then they will fast" (Matthew 9:14–15; Mark 2:18–20; Luke 5:33–35). This is the Church's charter for fasting: after the Ascension, the Bride fasts in longing for the Bridegroom, especially in expectation of His return.
- **The practice of the Apostolic Church** — the Church at Antioch "were worshiping the Lord and fasting" when the Holy Spirit sent Barnabas and Saul on mission, and again "with prayer and fasting" when they committed elders to the Lord (Acts 13:2–3; 14:23). St. Paul speaks of his own "labors… watchings, hunger" and of how he "pommels [his] body and subdues it, lest after preaching to others [he himself] should be disqualified" (2 Corinthians 11:27; 1 Corinthians 9:27; cf. 2 Corinthians 6:5; Romans 8:13).

## 4. The Early Church and the Fathers

### 4.1 The Weekly Fast: Wednesday and Friday

The earliest extra-biblical witness to Christian fasting is the _Didache_ (The Teaching of the Twelve Apostles, late first / early second century):

> "Let not your fasts be with the hypocrites, for they fast on the second and fifth days of the week; but do you fast on the fourth and sixth days." ([_Didache_ 8](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/1e34c4c5-4f60-4771-8788-614cd9c184e5/ref/8))

Christians deliberately **moved the twice-weekly fast from Monday/Thursday (the Jewish days) to Wednesday and Friday** — Wednesday, the day of the betrayal, and Friday, the day of the Crucifixion. In the West these became the **station days** (_dies stationum_), observed with fasting and public prayer; Tertullian describes the Wednesday and Friday "stations" as fasts lasting until the ninth hour (about 3:00 p.m.) (_On Fasting_ 14). This is the origin of the "Wednesday and Friday" fasting that survives today in the Eastern Catholic tradition and in stricter traditional Latin observance.

### 4.2 The Paschal Fast and the Development of Lent

- **The Easter fast** — from the earliest centuries the Church fasted before Easter. St. Irenaeus (c. 180), quoted by Eusebius, attests that the length varied — "some think one day ought to be observed, others two, others yet more; some measure the day as consisting of forty continuous hours" (_Church History_ V.24) — while all observed a pre-Paschal fast.
- **The forty days** — the Lenten fast of forty days, modeled on Christ's forty days in the desert, is attested throughout the fourth century. The Council of Nicaea (325) already presumes "the days of the fast before Easter" (canon 5); St. Athanasius' _Festal Letters_ (from 330) announce the beginning of the forty days; St. Cyril of Jerusalem and the pilgrim Egeria describe its observance. By the end of the fourth century the forty-day Lent was universal.
- **Councils defending the fast** — the Synod of Gangra (c. 340) anathematized the Eustathians who, under pretext of asceticism, despised the Church's appointed fasts and fasted on the Lord's Day, "whereas, because Christ rose from the grave… we should spend it in offering joyous thanks to God" ([canon 18](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/54b4cf7e-71c4-4f82-8fb0-3771010ab40c/ref/Canon%2018)). The Council of Laodicea (4th c.) decreed that "the fast of Lent must be observed throughout the whole season" (canon 50). The Council in Trullo (692) prohibited fasting on Saturdays and Sundays throughout the year (except Holy Saturday), to distinguish Christian observance from Jewish practice (canon 55), and regulated the canonical fasts.

### 4.3 The Teaching of the Fathers

The Fathers present fasting as an act of the whole person — the body's restraint serving the soul's freedom:

- **St. Basil the Great** preached that fasting "is the mother of health, the teacher of temperance… the guardian of the soul," and that the true fast is not merely the abstinence of the mouth but the restraint of the passions (_Homily on Fasting_).
- **St. John Chrysostom** insists fasting is worthless without charity: "Do you fast? Give me proof of it by your works. If you see a poor man, take pity on him. If you see a friend being honored, do not envy him… It is a far better thing to eat and drink and give thanks to God than to fast and murmur against Him" (_Homilies on the Statues_, 3).
- **St. Leo the Great** taught that fasting "tames the flesh, checks concupiscence, and disposes the soul to receive divine grace" (cf. _Sermon_ 12, on the fast of the tenth month).
- **St. Thomas Aquinas** synthesizes the tradition: fasting is an act of **temperance**, and it serves three ends — "first, to check the concupiscences of the flesh; secondly, to make the mind more ready for the contemplation of heavenly things; thirdly, to make satisfaction for sins" (_Summa Theologiae_, II–II, q. 147, a. 1). He holds that the specific days of fasting are determined by the Church, not by natural law alone (q. 147, a. 3).

## 5. Historical Development of the Latin Discipline

The Latin discipline has always combined two streams: the **weekly** Friday penance (and the older Wednesday/Friday stations) and the **annual** fasts of Lent, the Ember Days, and the vigils of major feasts. The details have varied greatly:

| Era / Document                                                                                                                 | Discipline                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                 |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Early Church**                                                                                                               | Wednesday and Friday station fasts; the pre-Paschal fast (varying length)                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  |
| **Fourth–sixth centuries**                                                                                                     | Forty-day Lent universal; Ember Days (Rome) and vigils develop; no fasting on Sundays                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                      |
| **Middle Ages**                                                                                                                | The Lenten fast of one meal a day, taken in the afternoon (_none_, later noon); "black fast" (no food till evening, no meat, often no fish, eggs, or dairy); the name "collation" for the evening snack derives from the _Collationes_ of Cassian read during supper                                                                                                                       |
| **[1917 Code, canon 1252](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0c4de460-291d-4922-807a-94a54b5715d8/ref/1252)**                    | **Abstinence** on all Fridays; **fast and abstinence** on Ash Wednesday, all Fridays and Saturdays of Lent, the Ember Days, and the vigils of Pentecost, the Assumption, All Saints, and Christmas; **fast only** on the other weekdays of Lent; fasting bound from age 21 to 60; one full meal at midday, with meat permitted once at that meal (except where abstinence applied)         |
| **_Paenitemini_ (Pope Paul VI, 1966)**                                                                                         | Simplified the law: **fasting** = one full meal plus "some food in the morning and evening, observing approved local custom"; **abstinence** = no meat, but eggs, dairy, and animal-fat condiments permitted; Ash Wednesday and Good Friday = fast and abstinence; all Fridays = abstinence (unless a solemnity), with episcopal conferences free to substitute other penance outside Lent |
| **[1983 Code of Canon Law, canons 1249–1253](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/927224e3-8c2d-44ed-a9fb-dc736030081d/ref/1249)** | The present universal law (see §6 below); fasting now binds from the age of majority (18) to the beginning of the 60th year                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                |

The **Eucharistic fast** — a separate "natural fast" of reverence before Holy Communion — has its own history: the medieval fast from midnight ([1917 Code, canon 858](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0c4de460-291d-4922-807a-94a54b5715d8/ref/858)) was reduced to three hours by Pope Pius XII (1957) and then to **one hour** by Pope Paul VI (1964), which is the present law (see §8 below).

## 6. The Present Latin Discipline (Code of Canon Law, 1983)

### 6.1 The Law in Brief

| Canon                                                                                      | Content                                                                                                                                                                                                                                        |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| [**1249**](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/927224e3-8c2d-44ed-a9fb-dc736030081d/ref/1249) | All the faithful are bound to do penance, each in his own way; the Church joins the faithful "especially through fast and abstinence"                                                                                                          |
| [**1250**](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/927224e3-8c2d-44ed-a9fb-dc736030081d/ref/1250) | The penitential days and times of the universal Church are **all Fridays of the year** and **the season of Lent**                                                                                                                              |
| [**1251**](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/927224e3-8c2d-44ed-a9fb-dc736030081d/ref/1251) | **Abstinence** from meat (or from another food determined by the episcopal conference) on **all Fridays**, unless a solemnity falls on the Friday; **abstinence and fasting** on **Ash Wednesday** and **Good Friday**                         |
| [**1252**](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/927224e3-8c2d-44ed-a9fb-dc736030081d/ref/1252) | **Abstinence** binds from the completion of the **fourteenth year**; **fasting** binds from the age of **majority (18)** until the beginning of the **sixtieth year**; pastors and parents are to educate minors in a genuine sense of penance |
| [**1253**](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/927224e3-8c2d-44ed-a9fb-dc736030081d/ref/1253) | **Episcopal conferences** may determine the observance more precisely and may substitute other forms of penance (especially works of charity and piety) in whole or in part for abstinence and fast                                            |

### 6.2 What Fasting Means (Latin Practice)

The ordinary Catholic fast is **not** a total fast of water only. It consists of:

- **One full meal** a day;
- **Two smaller meals** ("collations") which together must **not** equal another full meal;
- **No eating between meals**.

Water and other non-nutritive liquids, and medicine, do not break the fast. Pope Paul VI stated the norm authoritatively: "The law of fasting allows only one full meal a day, but does not prohibit taking some food in the morning and evening, observing — as far as quantity and quality are concerned — approved local custom" (_Paenitemini_, Norm II). A strict water-only fast is a legitimate **voluntary** practice — many saints observed it — but it is never what the Church's law requires.

### 6.3 What Abstinence Means (Latin Practice)

Abstinence excludes the **flesh of warm-blooded animals** — mammals and birds. It does **not** exclude:

- Fish and shellfish;
- Eggs;
- Milk and other dairy products.

_Paenitemini_ (Norm II) expressly permits "eggs, the products of milk, and condiments made of animal fat." **Fish, therefore, is not "meat" for the purpose of the law of abstinence**, which is why the Friday fish supper is a venerable Catholic tradition. An individual or parish may voluntarily adopt a stricter practice, but it is not required.

### 6.4 Who Is Excused, and Dispensations

- Those **outside the age limits** (under 14 for abstinence; under 18 or over 59 for fasting) are not bound by the law, though parents and pastors must educate them in the meaning of penance (canon 1252).
- **The sick, the infirm, pregnant and nursing women, manual laborers, and all those for whom fasting would endanger health** are excused, either by the nature of the case or by legitimate dispensation. Fasting is a means, never an end; it must never harm health.
- **Dispensation and commutation**: the pastor can dispense individual faithful and families for a just cause, and can commute the penance into another work of prayer or charity (canon 1245). Those who legitimately cannot fast are encouraged to substitute some other penance — a greater faithfulness to prayer, an act of charity, or the willing acceptance of their cross.

### 6.5 Friday Penance

Canon 1250 makes **every Friday** a penitential day. The universal norm is abstinence from meat (canon 1251), but canon 1253 allows episcopal conferences to substitute "other forms of penance, especially works of charity and exercises of piety" — and many conferences have done so for Fridays outside Lent. Where a conference has substituted another penance, the faithful of that territory follow the conference's norm. **The Fridays of Lent** remain days of abstinence in every territory. When a **solemnity** (such as Christmas or a patronal feast) falls on a Friday, the Friday abstinence does not bind (canon 1251).

## 7. The Eastern Catholic Discipline

The Eastern Catholic Churches observe fasting with a distinctive vigor and structure, governed by the [Code of Canons of the Eastern Churches (CCEO)](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/664b6063-e929-424c-b4ae-1f32e8ddd5c1/ref/880):

- [**CCEO canon 880**](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/664b6063-e929-424c-b4ae-1f32e8ddd5c1/ref/880): only the supreme authority of the Church (or, for particular days, the authority of each Church _sui iuris_) establishes days of penance.
- [**CCEO canon 882**](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/664b6063-e929-424c-b4ae-1f32e8ddd5c1/ref/882): "On the days of penance the Christian faithful are obliged to observe **fast or abstinence in the manner established by the particular law of their Church _sui iuris_**."

Each Eastern Catholic Church therefore determines its own manner and measure of fasting, in accord with its tradition. The common Byzantine pattern is:

| Fast                                              | Duration                                                                                            | Character                                                                                                                                                                  |
| ------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **The Great Fast (Lent)**                         | Forty days + Great and Holy Week                                                                    | The strictest fast: abstinence from meat, dairy, eggs, and fish on most days; wine and oil permitted on certain days; on the strictest weekdays **xerophagy** (dry eating) |
| **Wednesdays and Fridays**                        | Weekly, year-round (except during fast-free weeks)                                                  | Abstinence from meat, dairy, and eggs; fish is traditionally permitted on Wednesdays and Fridays during the Christmas season and some other periods                        |
| **The Nativity (Philip's) Fast**                  | November 15 – December 24                                                                           | Forty days; less strict than the Great Fast — meat, dairy, and eggs are set aside, with fish, wine, and oil on certain days                                                |
| **The Apostles' Fast**                            | From the Monday after All Saints (Sunday after Pentecost) to June 28 (Feast of Sts. Peter and Paul) | Variable length; similar to the Nativity Fast                                                                                                                              |
| **The Dormition Fast**                            | August 1–14                                                                                         | Two weeks of fasting in preparation for the Feast of the Dormition of the Theotokos                                                                                        |
| **Paramony (Eves) of the Nativity and Theophany** | December 24 and January 5                                                                           | Strict fasting days                                                                                                                                                        |

During the Great Fast the weekday **Divine Liturgy of the Presanctified Gifts** is celebrated on Wednesdays and Fridays (and, in some traditions, on other weekdays) — the penitential communion service that accompanies the fasting season (see [liturgy/order-of-the-mass.md](order-of-the-mass.md)). Bright Week (the week after Pascha) and the fast-free weeks are entirely free of fasting.

The Eastern theology of fasting is summed up in the Catechism of the Ukrainian Catholic Church: fasting "takes hold of the entire being: the body through restraint in food and drink, and the soul through restraint of the passions," quoting St. John Chrysostom: "Do you not eat flesh? Feed not upon indecency by means of the eyes… What does it profit if we abstain from birds and fishes; and yet bite and devour our brothers and sisters?" (_Christ – Our Pascha_, §786).

## 8. Fasting and the Eucharist

There are **two distinct fasts** connected with the Blessed Sacrament:

1. **The penitential fast** (treated above), which disposes the soul through self-denial.
2. **The Eucharistic fast** — the "natural fast" of reverence before receiving the Lord. The present Latin law ([canon 919](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/927224e3-8c2d-44ed-a9fb-dc736030081d/ref/919)): "One who is to receive the Most Holy Eucharist is to abstain from any food or drink, with the exception only of **water and medicine**, for at least the period of **one hour before Holy Communion**." The sick and the elderly may receive Holy Communion even if they have taken something within the hour (canon 919 §3). In the Eastern Catholic Churches, the older and stricter Eucharistic fast (from midnight, or the "fast from the evening") is retained in many traditions by particular law — see [liturgy/order-of-the-mass.md](order-of-the-mass.md).

The Eucharistic fast is a matter of reverence for the Real Presence, and it must not be confused with the penitential fast; one can break one without touching the other.

## 9. The Spiritual Meaning of Fasting

Fasting is never an end in itself. The Church's tradition gives it a threefold spiritual purpose, expressed by St. Thomas Aquinas (_Summa Theologiae_, II–II, q. 147, a. 1) and echoed throughout the Fathers:

1. **To restrain the concupiscences of the flesh** — fasting is the great discipline of temperance, taming the appetite and thereby the other passions. "I pommel my body and subdue it" (1 Corinthians 9:27).
2. **To raise the mind to God** — bodily hunger disposes the soul to spiritual hunger and to prayer. Fasting and prayer belong together (Acts 13:2; Matthew 17:21; CCC 1434).
3. **To make satisfaction for sin** — fasting is a penitential offering joined to Christ's Passion, a concrete expression of conversion (cf. Joel 2:12–13).

Above all, fasting must be **joined to charity**. Isaiah 58 declares that the fast God chooses is the fast that feeds the hungry, shelters the homeless, and breaks unjust bonds; Tobit 12:8 joins fasting to almsgiving and prayer; the Catechism teaches that "Scripture and the Fathers insist above all on three forms" of interior penance — "**fasting, prayer, and almsgiving**, which express conversion in relation to oneself, to God, and to others" ([CCC 1434](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/1434)). Pope St. John Paul II preached on Ash Wednesday that "fasting is not an act of pure self-denial, but a way of making ourselves available for the needs of others and for the encounter with God" (General Audience, 5 March 2003).

The authentic Christian fast, therefore:

- is **silent and hidden** — seen by the Father, not by men (Matthew 6:16–18);
- is **ordered to prayer** — hunger of body awakening hunger of soul;
- **issues in charity** — the food not eaten becomes bread for the poor;
- is **joined to the Cross** — a share in Christ's self-emptying, undertaken in the Bride's longing for the Bridegroom (Matthew 9:15).

## 10. Pastoral Guidance and Common Questions

### 10.1 Fasting in Practice

- On a **fast day** (Ash Wednesday, Good Friday): one full meal and two small collations; no snacking between meals. Water does not break the fast.
- On a **day of abstinence** (all Fridays, unless a solemnity; the Fridays of Lent always): no meat. Fish, eggs, and dairy are permitted.
- On **Ash Wednesday and Good Friday**: both — fast _and_ abstinence.
- **Those who cannot fast** should not feel guilt; they are to substitute prayer and charity, and may ask a pastor for a commutation (canon 1245). Fasting that harms health is not the fast of the Church.
- **Voluntary fasting** beyond the law — a stricter Lent, the Wednesday fast, the traditional Ember Days and vigils, or the Eastern weekly fasts — is a venerable devotion, provided it is prudent, healthy, and united to prayer and almsgiving.

### 10.2 Common Questions

**Is fish "meat"?**
No. In the canonical sense "meat" means the flesh of warm-blooded animals (mammals and birds). Fish, shellfish, eggs, and dairy are permitted on days of abstinence (see §6.3).

**Does drinking water break the fast?**
No. Water (and other non-nutritive liquids) and medicine do not break either the penitential fast or the Eucharistic fast (canon 919).

**Is eating blood (e.g., _dinuguan_) a violation of abstinence?**
No. The Council of Florence (_Cantate Domino_, 1442) taught that the apostolic prohibition of blood (Acts 15:29) was temporary — "with the ending of the cause for this apostolic prohibition, the effect also ended" (DS 1350) — and the Church permits the eating of blood dishes. They are not "meat" for the law of abstinence, and they do not violate fasting as long as the quantity rules are observed.

**Does a "solemnity on Friday" excuse from abstinence?**
Yes. When a solemnity falls on a Friday, Friday abstinence does not bind (canon 1251).

**What about coffee, tea, or juice during a fast?**
Custom and common practice permit non-alcoholic beverages during the fast; the law's concern is food and the quantity of meals. Those who wish to be stricter are free to be so.

**I forgot and ate something on a fast day — is that a sin?**
The obligation is serious but it is a matter of the Church's positive law; a genuine lapse through forgetfulness or weakness, especially followed by a return to the discipline, is not the stuff of scrupulosity. Fasting is a school of conversion, not a trap.
