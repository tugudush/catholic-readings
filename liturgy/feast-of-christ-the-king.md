# Solemnity of Our Lord Jesus Christ, King of the Universe

> **Source note:** The feast instituted by Pope Pius XI in 1925 was originally called the **Feast of Christ the King** (_Domi nostri Iesu Christi Regis_). The Roman Missal of Pope St. Paul VI renamed it the **Solemnity of Our Lord Jesus Christ, King of the Universe** and transferred it to the last Sunday of the liturgical year. The devotional shorthand "Christ the King" remains universal; the distinction of titles and dates matters for liturgical accuracy.

## 1. At a Glance

| Detail                                   | Catholic observance                                                                                                                                   |
| ---------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Date in the Roman Rite**               | Last Sunday of Ordinary Time (the thirty-fourth Sunday, unless a higher-rank Sunday displaces the Ordinary Time cycle)                                |
| **Date in the 1925 calendar of Pius XI** | Last Sunday of October, immediately before All Saints' Day                                                                                            |
| **Official Roman title**                 | Solemnity of Our Lord Jesus Christ, King of the Universe (_Domini nostri Iesu Christi Regis Universorum_)                                             |
| **Liturgical rank**                      | Solemnity                                                                                                                                             |
| **Liturgical color**                     | White (or gold)                                                                                                                                       |
| **Universal Holy Day of Obligation?**    | No as such, but as a Sunday it carries the ordinary Sunday precept everywhere                                                                         |
| **Theological center**                   | The universal kingship of the incarnate Son: Christ reigns over minds, wills, hearts, families, and nations, and "will restore all things in himself" |
| **Position in the year**                 | The final Sunday of the liturgical year; the Church then begins Advent, awaiting the coming of the same King                                          |

The solemnity is not a commemoration of one mystery of Christ's life among others. It gathers the entire year's proclamation — the mysteries celebrated from Advent through Pentecost and the whole of Ordinary Time — under the confession that all of it belongs to the one who is "Alpha and Omega" (Revelation 1:8) and to whose kingdom there will be no end (Luke 1:33).

## 2. Institution by Pope Pius XI: _Quas Primas_ (1925)

The feast was instituted by Pope Pius XI in the encyclical [_Quas Primas_ (11 December 1925)](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/7c11976c-2b26-420f-838d-7694b899e54b/ref/28). The occasion was the aftermath of the First World War: the rise of secularism, the growing conviction that religion was a private affair without public standing, and the spread of ideologies that denied the social rights of Christ the King. The Church's answer was not a political program but a liturgical act — an annual, universal profession that Christ is King in the strict sense, with consequences for public as well as private life.

Pius XI assigned the observance to the last Sunday of October, before All Saints' Day, so that the Church would "set the crowning glory upon the mysteries of the life of Christ already commemorated during the year" and then celebrate those who share in his triumph ([_Quas Primas_ § 29](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/7c11976c-2b26-420f-838d-7694b899e54b/ref/29)). He chose a Sunday deliberately so that the whole people of God — not only clergy bound to the Divine Office, but the laity as well — could take part. The Pope also required that the faithful be taught the meaning of the feast, and that the annual act of consecration of the human race to the Sacred Heart of Jesus, composed by Pius IX for the 1900 Holy Year and promoted by Leo XIII in _Annum Sacrum_ (1899), be renewed publicly on this day.

The political note of the encyclical should be read in its proper context. Pius XI intended the feast as a reminder "that not only private individuals but also rulers and princes are bound to give public honor and obedience to Christ" ([_Quas Primas_ § 32](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/7c11976c-2b26-420f-838d-7694b899e54b/ref/32)). The affirmation concerns the social kingship of Christ — the claim of God's law upon nations, laws, and education — and belongs to the Church's constant doctrine that civil society is subject to God; it is not a call for any particular constitutional arrangement, and later magisterial teaching on religious liberty (_Dignitatis Humanae_) must be read alongside it. The feast's durable center is Christological, not political.

## 3. The Doctrine of Christ's Kingship

### 3.1 Grounded in the hypostatic union

Pius XI teaches that Christ is King "in the strict and proper sense" of the word. As man, he received from the Father a kingdom and dominion over all creatures; as the divine Word, he possesses with the Father that dominion by which all things are governed ([_Quas Primas_ § 7](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/7c11976c-2b26-420f-838d-7694b899e54b/ref/7)). The kingship is grounded in the **hypostatic union**: the authority is not conferred by any human institution but belongs to the incarnate Son as Lord of creation and Redeemer.

### 3.2 Scripture

The encyclical assembles the scriptural witness to the kingship of the Word:

- He is called "King of kings and Lord of lords" (Revelation 19:16) and "ruler of the kings of the earth" (Revelation 1:5).
- The Father has "given him authority over all flesh" (John 17:2) and "all power in heaven and on earth" (Matthew 28:18).
- The annunciation promises a kingdom without end: "of his kingdom there shall be no end" (Luke 1:32–33).
- Even in the Passion the title stands: "You say that I am a king" (John 18:37), and the inscription on the Cross reads "Jesus of Nazareth, the King of the Jews" (John 19:19).

The Psalter and the prophets supply the royal vocabulary — "The Lord is king, in splendor robed" (Psalm 93:1); "His empire shall be extended, and of peace there shall be no end" (Isaiah 9:7) — which the liturgy of the feast takes up extensively.

### 3.3 Spiritual and universal: interior reign and social reign

Pius XI distinguishes two dimensions, which the feast holds together:

- **Interior reign**: Christ reigns in minds because he is Truth to be received and believed; in wills, because his grace draws human freedom toward the good; in hearts, through his love and mercy ([_Quas Primas_ § 7](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/7c11976c-2b26-420f-838d-7694b899e54b/ref/7)). This is the reign of conversion and of sanctity.
- **Social (external) reign**: because his kingship is real and universal, individuals, families, and societies are bound to acknowledge it in law, education, and public life. The kingdom "is not of this world" in its origin and manner (John 18:36) — it is not advanced by worldly force — but it is not irrelevant to this world: it claims the whole of life, private and public.

The feast therefore guards against two opposite distortions: reducing Christ's kingship to a private sentiment with no public claim, or confusing it with political domination or confessional coercion. Its mode is Christ's own: truth, love, service, and the salvation of souls, exercised through the Church's spiritual weapons rather than the sword.

### 3.4 Eschatological dimension

Because the solemnity closes the liturgical year immediately before Advent, it also carries an eschatological weight: the King who has come and now reigns hidden in grace will come again in glory to judge the living and the dead (CCC 668–679). The judgment scenes of the Mass readings — especially Matthew 25:31–46, the Last Judgment, read on the A-cycle Sunday — make explicit that the King identifies himself with "the least of these my brethren." The liturgical-year placement thus teaches that history itself is moving toward the manifestation of the kingdom that is already present in mystery (CCC 671; see the general treatment of the last things in [eschatology/README.md](../eschatology/README.md)).

## 4. The Liturgy of the Feast

### 4.1 The Collect

The Roman Missal's collect (Third Typical Edition) gathers the doctrine of the feast into a single prayer:

    Almighty ever-living God,
    whose will is to restore all things
    in your beloved Son, the King of the universe,
    grant, we pray, that the whole creation,
    set free from slavery,
    may render your majesty service
    and ceaselessly proclaim your praise.
    Who lives and reigns with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit,
    God, for ever and ever. Amen.

The prayer expresses the cosmic scope of the kingship: all creation, delivered from bondage to sin, is summoned to serve God and proclaim his praise — the reconciliation and renewal of the universe anticipated in the kingdom.

### 4.2 The Preface

The Roman Missal provides a proper Preface, _Dignus est Agnus_, which proclaims the anointing of the eternal priest and universal king: he "as King of kings and Lord of lords" restores all things in himself and presents to the Father an eternal and universal kingdom — "a kingdom of truth and life, a kingdom of holiness and grace, a kingdom of justice, love and peace." These four descriptions, taken from _Quas Primas_, name the character of Christ's reign and are the feast's most quoted liturgical formula.

### 4.3 Readings and the Gospel of the Kingdom

The three-year cycle moves through the kingdom's paradox:

| Year | Gospel           | Emphasis                                                                     |
| ---- | ---------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| A    | Matthew 25:31–46 | The King on the throne of judgment, hidden in the least of the brethren      |
| B    | John 18:33b–37   | Before Pilate: "My kingdom is not of this world... You say that I am a king" |
| C    | Luke 23:35–43    | The crucified King: "Today you will be with me in Paradise"                  |

Year B and Year C in particular teach that the kingship is exercised through humiliation and self-gift: the throne of the universe is the Cross.

### 4.4 The Act of Consecration of the Human Race

_Quas Primas_ (§§ 30–31) ordered that the public consecration of the human race to the Sacred Heart of Jesus, composed by Pius IX and ordered by Leo XIII in _Annum Sacrum_ (1899) for 11 June 1900, be renewed annually on this feast. The act of consecration is a devotion of reparation and enthronement, not a sacrament; many families and parishes retain the related practice of enthronement of the Sacred Heart in the home. For the full treatment of the devotion, see [liturgy/sacred-heart-devotion.md](sacred-heart-devotion.md).

## 5. Historical Development

| Date                   | Development                                                                                                                                                                                                                 | Significance                                                                                                                                                                                |
| ---------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **1899**               | Leo XIII, _Annum Sacrum_, orders the consecration of the human race to the Sacred Heart.                                                                                                                                    | Establishes the annual public consecration later tied to the feast.                                                                                                                         |
| **1925**               | Pius XI, _Quas Primas_ (11 December), institutes the Feast of Christ the King for the last Sunday of October.                                                                                                               | The feast becomes a universal annual profession of Christ's social and spiritual kingship.                                                                                                  |
| **1926**               | First observance, 31 October 1926.                                                                                                                                                                                          | The Church worldwide keeps the new feast.                                                                                                                                                   |
| **1969**               | Pope St. Paul VI's reform of the liturgical year (Universal Norms on the Liturgical Year, norm 6) transfers the solemnity to the last Sunday of Ordinary Time and renames it "Our Lord Jesus Christ, King of the Universe." | The feast becomes the proper close of the liturgical year, oriented to eschatology and cosmic renewal; the emphasis moves from the social-political note to the universal, cosmic kingship. |
| **1970 / 1971 onward** | First celebrations on the new date in the reformed calendar.                                                                                                                                                                | The solemnity now directly precedes Advent (except when a higher Sunday intervenes, when it may fall as early as 20 November).                                                              |

The change of date and title was more than administrative. Pius XI's placement before All Saints emphasized the year's Christological cycle culminating in the saints' triumph; Paul VI's placement at the year's end emphasizes the eschatological horizon — the year ends where history ends, with the King coming in glory — and the title "King of the Universe" underlines the cosmic extent of the reign (Colossians 1:15–20, read in Year C). Both emphases are legitimate and complementary readings of the same dogma.

## 6. Latin and Eastern Catholic Perspectives

The solemnity as a fixed calendar day belongs to the Roman liturgical calendar; Pius XI himself noted that the Eastern liturgies, like the Roman, ceaselessly praise Christ as King and Lord ([_Quas Primas_ § 12](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/7c11976c-2b26-420f-838d-7694b899e54b/ref/12)). The precise forms of the witness differ:

- **Latin Church**: keeps the Solemnity of Our Lord Jesus Christ, King of the Universe as the final Sunday of Ordinary Time, with its proper collect, preface, and readings.
- **Byzantine tradition**: no direct calendar equivalent exists, but the same faith is confessed liturgically — in the Akathist hymn to the Most Holy Name of Jesus ("King of Heaven" — _Царю Небесный_), in the _Polychronion_ addressed to earthly authorities "under the Kingship of Christ," and in the eschatological Office of the Last Judgment Sunday (Meatfare Sunday), which reads Matthew 25:31–46 as the Byzantine pre-Lent gate: the Church contemplates the coming judgment of the King just as the Roman calendar does at year's end.
- **Other Eastern Catholic Churches**: analogous solemnities of the Name of Jesus, of the Last Judgment, or of the End of the Year in some calendars carry the same doctrine. No single universal Eastern counterpart feast is established in the sources; the kingship itself, however, is confessed in every rite, since it belongs to the faith and not to one calendar (CCC 559; see the general treatment in [church-history/eastern-catholic-churches.md](../church-history/eastern-catholic-churches.md)).

## 7. Devotional and Pastoral Practices

Common observances connected with the solemnity include:

- **Enthronement of the Sacred Heart** in the home, often made on or near the feast, following the rite promoted by Fr. Mateo Crawley-Boevey.
- **Public procession and Benediction**, historically associated with the feast's institution and the Act of Consecration of the Human Race.
- **Renewal of the act of consecration to the Sacred Heart** at parish or family level, as _Quas Primas_ ordered.
- **Exposition of the Blessed Sacrament and Holy Hours**, since the King is really present in the tabernacle (see [liturgy/eucharistic-adoration.md](eucharistic-adoration.md)).
- **Pilgrimages and parish patronal feasts** where Christ the King is the patronal title — a very common parish dedication in the twentieth century, particularly in the Philippines, where numerous parishes bear the title "Christ the King."
- **Preparation for Advent**: homiletically, the solemnity is frequently used to teach the full sweep of the liturgical year before the Church re-enters the expectation of the King's coming.

None of these practices is required by law apart from the Sunday obligation; the feast's precept is the ordinary Sunday precept. The devotions are popular piety in the sense of the [Directory on Popular Piety and the Liturgy](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/4fba701f-fda0-47a5-a2de-2e210d81f770/ref/197) and must never be confused with, or substituted for, the liturgy itself.

## 8. Doctrinal Classification

| Element                                                                           | Doctrinal weight                                                                                                                                                                                              |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Christ's kingship over all creation, grounded in the hypostatic union             | **Dogma / constant doctrine** (Scripture: Psalm 93, Luke 1:33, John 18:37, Colossians 1:16–18; taught in _Quas Primas_ § 7)                                                                                   |
| The obligation of individuals and societies to acknowledge Christ                 | **Doctrinal teaching** of the ordinary magisterium (_Quas Primas_ §§ 32–33), to be read with the later development on religious liberty (_Dignitatis Humanae_)                                                |
| The institution of the feast on the last Sunday of October (1925)                 | **Ecclesiastical discipline**, superseded in 1969                                                                                                                                                             |
| The present date and title (last Sunday of Ordinary Time, "King of the Universe") | **Ecclesiastical discipline** (Universal Norms, norm 6), mutable by authority of the Roman See                                                                                                                |
| Enthronement of the Sacred Heart, processions, consecration rites                 | **Popular piety and devotion**, encouraged, never obligatory, never adding to public revelation                                                                                                               |
| Speculative readings of "the Kingdom" (e.g., millenarian earthly reigns)          | **Theological error** where they contradict CCC 676; the kingdom "grows mysteriously in the hearts of those planted within it by God's Word," and its final fulfillment is not a earthly political millennium |
