# The Alleged St. Jerome Quotation on Masses for the Souls in Purgatory

> **Verdict in brief.** The quotation that circulates under the name of St. Jerome —
>
> _"When Mass is celebrated for a soul in Purgatory, that burning fire suspends its action, and the soul ceases to suffer for the entire time that the celebration of the Sacrifice lasts."_
>
> together with the companion claim,
>
> _"For every Mass that is said, many souls leave Purgatory and fly to heaven."_
>
> — is **not found in any authentic work of St. Jerome**. Both sayings are a **spurious attribution** that circulates in modern devotional literature and on social media, usually carrying the unverifiable citation "(S. Hier., c. cum Mart. de celebr. Miss.)". **No such passage exists in Jerome's corpus, and the citation does not correspond to any known work of his.**
>
> The **practice the post commends** — having the Holy Sacrifice of the Mass offered for the faithful departed — is entirely Catholic, is the most excellent of all suffrages for the dead, and is defined by the Council of Trent ([DH 1820](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/17f50f07-de81-4bf5-997c-f41ee830c033/ref/1820)). But the **two precise claims** the post attaches to St. Jerome — that purgatorial suffering is _wholly suspended_ during Mass, and that _"many souls"_ are released at _every_ Mass — are **unverified private claims**, not doctrine, and the first of them exceeds anything the Church has ever defined.

This document is a source-critical verification of a claim encountered in devotional circulation. It also surveys the same and related claims as they appear in the Church's genuine private revelations (§6), and shows why even the approved mystics do not supply the quotation. For the Church's actual doctrine on Purgatory and on the means of remitting temporal punishment, see [purgatory.md](purgatory.md); for the magisterial sources, see [doctrinal-foundations.md](doctrinal-foundations.md). For a source-critical treatment of a comparable body of devotional claims, see [Cameron Riecker on Purgatory](cameron-riecker-purgatory-eyewitnesses.md).

## 1. The Claim as It Circulates

The text verified here is reproduced, in substance, from a widely shared social-media post (Facebook, X/Twitter, and messaging apps) presented as a discovery of "what happens to the flames of Purgatory when Holy Mass is celebrated." Its argument runs as follows:

1. St. Jerome records a teaching "that every Christian should know by heart."
2. The alleged words of Jerome are given as: _"When Mass is celebrated for a soul in Purgatory, that burning fire suspends its action, and the soul ceases to suffer for the entire time that the celebration of the Sacrifice lasts."_ — citation: **(S. Hier., c. cum Mart. de celebr. Miss.)**
3. A "second revelation from the same Holy Doctor" is added: _"For every Mass that is said, many souls leave Purgatory and fly to heaven."_ — citation: "(St. Jerome, cited work)".
4. The post draws the devotional conclusion that offering Masses for the dead is "an effective act of mercy," presenting God with "the only sacrifice He cannot refuse."

Point 4 is pastorally sound in its _conclusion_ (the Mass for the dead is genuine and efficacious) but the _reasoning_ rests on points 1–3, which do not hold. The remainder of this document shows why.

## 3. Why the Attribution to St. Jerome Fails

### 3.1 The Words Are Absent from Jerome's Corpus

St. Jerome (c. 347–420) is one of the most voluminous writers of Christian antiquity: the letters (some 150), the biblical commentaries (on Matthew, the Minor and Major Prophets, the Pauline Epistles), the polemical treatises (_Against Helvidius_, _Against Jovinian_, _Against Vigilantius_, _Against John of Jerusalem_), the ascetical and historical works, and the prefaces to the Vulgate. In none of these — nor in the spurious and doubtful works that early editors appended "among the works of Jerome" — is the sentence about the fire of Purgatory suspending its action to be found. No edition, no critical apparatus, and no patristic database attributes it to him.

The saying has the hallmarks of a **Pseudo-Jerome** text: a pious sentence composed in the style of a Father, later circulated with his name attached to give it weight. This is a very common pattern in popular devotional literature. (On the parallel danger of treating hagiographical or devotional compositions as if they were the saint's own words, see the source note at the head of [saints/st-jerome.md](../saints/st-jerome.md).)

### 3.2 The Citation Is a Phantom Reference

The appended reference — "(S. Hier., c. cum Mart. de celebr. Miss.)" — cannot be matched to anything Jerome wrote:

- **"S. Hier."** is the standard abbreviation for _Sanctus Hieronymus_ (St. Jerome).
- **"c."** conventionally abbreviates _caput_ (chapter) or _contra_ (against), but neither reading yields a work of Jerome on the celebration of Mass.
- **"de celebr. Miss."** (_de celebratione Missarum_, "on the celebration of Masses") is **not a title of any work by Jerome**. Jerome wrote no treatise on the Mass. His only sustained discussion of the Eucharistic sacrifice occurs _incidentally_, in his letters and in _Against Vigilantius_, where he defends the Church's practice of offering the Sacrifice at the tombs of the martyrs — never in a work "on the celebration of Masses."

A citation that names a genre-title Jerome never used, in a work that does not exist, is evidence of a _manufactured_ rather than a _transmitted_ reference. This is exactly the kind of citation that accumulates around a pious saying once it begins to circulate anonymously.

### 3.3 What Jerome Actually Wrote About the Dead

Jerome's authentic teaching is genuinely relevant to the topic — which is why the spurious quotation is plausible to readers who do not check it. But what he actually wrote differs from the claim:

- **He defended the Church's prayer and offering for the dead.** In _Against Vigilantius_ (406) — written against a presbyter who derided prayers for the dead, the veneration of relics, and the offering of the Sacrifice at the martyrs' tombs — Jerome argues: "Does the bishop of Rome do wrong when he offers sacrifices to the Lord over the venerable bones of the dead men Peter and Paul…? And not only is the bishop of one city in error, but the bishops of the whole world…" (_Against Vigilantius_ 8; see also §§6 and 9–10). He likewise insists that the saints, having passed from this life, are _more_, not less, able to pray for the living (§6). This establishes Jerome as a witness to the ancient Christian practice of prayer and sacrifice connected with the dead — but it is a defence of an _existing practice_, not a revelation about the mechanics of purgatorial fire.
- **He read 1 Corinthians 3 as a purifying fire.** Following St. Ambrose, Jerome applies the "saved, yet so as by fire" of [1 Corinthians 3:11–15](https://www.drbo.org/x/d?b=drb&c=1co&g=3&p=11) to a purification after death in his _Commentary on Amos_ (i, 4). The Catholic Encyclopedia's summary of the patristic and theological tradition lists Jerome among those who so interpreted the text (see _Catholic Encyclopedia_, "Purgatory").
- **He held that some sins are forgiven in the world to come.** Like St. Augustine and St. Gregory the Great, Jerome interpreted [Matthew 12:32](https://www.drbo.org/x/d?b=drb&c=mt&g=12&p=32) ("neither in this world, nor in the world to come") as implying a state in which sins can still be remitted after death.

In short: **Jerome attests prayer and offering for the dead, and a purification by fire after death; he nowhere says that the fire is suspended for the duration of every Mass, nor that a fixed number of souls is released at each Mass.**

## 4. What the Church Actually Teaches

### 4.1 The Defined Doctrine

The practice and doctrine the post means to commend are sound and are defined by the Church:

- **The Council of Florence** (1439), decree _Laetentur Caeli_: "the suffrages of the living faithful are of advantage" to the souls being cleansed after death, "namely, the **sacrifices of Masses**, prayers, and almsgiving, and other works of piety" ([DH 1304](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/17f50f07-de81-4bf5-997c-f41ee830c033/ref/1304)).
- **The Council of Trent** (1563), _Decree on Purgatory_, Session XXV: "The Catholic Church, instructed by the Holy Ghost… has taught that **there is a purgatory**, and that the souls there detained are helped by **the suffrages of the faithful, and principally by the acceptable sacrifice of the altar**" ([DH 1820](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/17f50f07-de81-4bf5-997c-f41ee830c033/ref/1820)).
- **The Catechism of the Catholic Church**: "The Eucharistic sacrifice is also offered for the faithful departed who 'have died in Christ but are not yet wholly purified,' so that they may be able to enter into the light and peace of Christ" ([CCC 1371](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/para-1371)); "All who die in God's grace and friendship, but still imperfectly purified, are indeed assured of their eternal salvation; but after death they undergo purification…" ([CCC 1030](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/1030)).

The doctrine of suffrage for the dead flows from the **communion of saints**: "our prayer for them is capable not only of helping them, but also of making their intercession for us effective" ([CCC 958](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/958)). For the full catalogue of means of remitting temporal punishment, see [purgatory.md](purgatory.md) §5.

### 4.2 "Per Modum Suffragii": The Manner of Application

This is the crucial point the viral quotation obscures. The Church applies the benefits of the Mass — and of indulgences — to the dead **by way of suffrage** (_per modum suffragii_), that is, as a **petition offered to God's mercy**, not as a juridical sentence executed by the Church and not as a mechanically guaranteed effect. Theologians, following St. Bonaventure and St. Thomas Aquinas, hold that an indulgence applied to a soul in Purgatory "does not absolve juridically the souls… but [acts] rather as a suffrage":

> "Haec non tenet modum judicii, sed potius suffragii."

The reason is that judgment over the dead is reserved to God; the Church can offer, but cannot sentence. Hence the popes attach to indulgences for the dead the restriction **"per modum suffragii et deprecationis"** (see _Catholic Encyclopedia_, "Purgatory", §Indulgences). The **application** of the Mass's infinite treasury to a particular soul is therefore a matter of God's free and merciful disposition — which the Church confidently expects, but which no one may reduce to a formula such as "so many souls per Mass."

### 4.3 The Church Has Not Defined the Nature, Intensity, or Duration of Purgatorial Punishment

Trent deliberately refused to define _what_ the purgatorial punishment is (whether material fire, and in what sense), _where_ it is, or _how long_ it lasts. The Council instead commanded bishops "to exclude from their preaching difficult and subtle questions which tend not to edification," and forbade "all curious searching into things that are unnecessary or not revealed" ([DH 1820](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/17f50f07-de81-4bf5-997c-f41ee830c033/ref/1820)). It also established that Purgatory is never measured in earthly time — a point underscored in 1967 when Blessed Paul VI abolished the old "days and years" computation of indulgences (_Indulgentiarum Doctrina_); see [purgatory.md](purgatory.md) §6.

A claim such as "the fire suspends its action for the entire time the celebration lasts" is precisely the kind of **speculative detail** about the nature and temporality of purgatorial punishment that the Church has declined to define. It may be a pious opinion; it cannot be presented as a teaching of a Doctor of the Church.

### 4.4 Why "The Only Sacrifice He Cannot Refuse" Is Imprecise

The post's closing flourish — that in the Mass "we are presenting to God the only sacrifice He cannot refuse" — is devotionally moving but theologically imprecise. God is not _compelled_ by anything, even by the Sacrifice of His Son; the Mass is not a mechanism that constrains the divine will. What the Mass does is unite the Church's petition to the one, perfect, and infinitely meritorious Sacrifice of Christ, which the Father **always accepts** in the order of redemption — but the _application_ of that sacrifice to a particular soul, and the manner and measure of its effect, remain a matter of God's wise and merciful freedom (§4.2). The Mass is the most _excellent_ suffrage, not an _automatic_ one. The Church's own prayer — _"and with your spirit… that my sacrifice and yours may be acceptable to God, the almighty Father"_ — is itself a petition, not a claim of right.

## 5. The Genuine Seed of the Idea: the Thirty Gregorian Masses

Stories of the dead being released through Masses are ancient and, in their proper genre, edifying. The most famous is the origin of the **Thirty Gregorian Masses**:

> St. Gregory the Great (_Dialogues_, Book IV, ch. 55) recounts a monk named **Justus** of his own monastery who, on his deathbed, confessed to having secretly kept three gold pieces — a violation of monastic poverty. Though Justus repented and died in the communion of the Church, Gregory judged the matter grave, and ordered that the Holy Sacrifice be offered for him on **thirty consecutive days**. Before the thirty days were complete, Justus appeared to his brother Copiosus and announced that he had been received into communion and released from his suffering.

This story is the recognised source of the practice of the **"Gregorian Masses"** — thirty Masses offered on consecutive days for a deceased person. The story is a **narrative in a hagiographical dialogue**, not a doctrinal definition; it teaches, rightly, that the Mass offered for the dead is efficacious. It does **not** say that _every_ Mass frees a fixed number of souls, nor that Gregory's own Masses (or anyone's) automatically emptied Purgatory. The precise formulation in the viral post — "for every Mass that is said, many souls leave Purgatory" — is a later devotional _embellishment_ of this kind of story. A nineteenth-century popular synthesis (Charles Arminjon, _The End of This Present World and the Mysteries of the Future Life_, pp. 123–125, 182) repeats traditions of this genre; such works are valuable as devotional reading but are not primary sources and carry no magisterial weight.

## 6. The Same Claim in Other Private Revelations

The post appeals to a Father of the Church. But readers often ask the deeper question: does the same idea — Masses and prayers emptying Purgatory, even by a fixed number — appear in the Church's **own mystics**? In part, it does; and the comparison is instructive, because **the Church's handling of those revelations is the opposite of what the viral post implies.** Two things must be said at the outset:

1. **Related claims genuinely occur in approved private revelations.** Several canonized mystics report visions of souls freed through the Mass and through prayer for the dead. This is consistent with — and a devotional help toward — the defined doctrine that the Mass is the most excellent suffrage (§4).
2. **But none of them contains the pseudo-Jerome claim** that the fire "suspends its action" for the whole duration of Mass; and the most widely circulated **numeric** promises have been formally cautioned against, or declared of uncertain origin, by the Holy See.

### 6.1 St. Gertrude the Great (1256–1302) — the "1,000 souls" prayer

The prayer most often shared under her name — _"Eternal Father, I offer Thee the Most Precious Blood of Thy Divine Son Jesus, in union with all the Masses said throughout the world today, for all the holy souls in Purgatory…"_ — is a devotional composition associated with her; the promise that **"1,000 souls are released from Purgatory each time it is said"** is a later accretion. That promise is **not found in St. Gertrude's authentic writings** (the _Legatus Divinae Pietatis_, or _Revelations_), and the prayer is **not an officially indulgenced one**. Catholic sources note that the Holy See has rejected the notion that a determinate number of souls is released by the recitation of a specified prayer.

St. Gertrude's genuine legacy is her compassion for the souls in Purgatory and her prayers for them — matters of devotion, well attested in her _Revelations_. She is not the source of a "1,000 souls per prayer" formula.

### 6.2 St. Mechtilde of Hackeborn (1241–1298) — the _Book of Special Grace_

St. Mechtilde was Gertrude's fellow nun and confidante. Her _Liber Specialis Gratiae_ ("Book of Special Grace"; English: _Select Revelations of S. Mechtild_) contains an "Our Father for the souls in Purgatory," and the tradition reports that when she prayed it she "saw legions of souls" ascend from Purgatory to heaven. This is private revelation — edifying and permitted reading, but not doctrine. Note what it does **not** contain: no fixed number per recitation, and no claim that the fire of Purgatory is suspended during Mass.

### 6.3 St. Bridget of Sweden (1303–1373) — the "Fifteen Prayers" and their promises

The most instructive parallel is St. Bridget. The "Fifteen Prayers" ("the Fifteen O's") and the related "Twelve-Year" and "Seven Prayers" devotions carry attached **promises**, for example:

> "I will deliver 15 souls of his lineage from Purgatory." … "33 souls would be released from Purgatory" (if the prayers are said devoutly 33 times on Good Friday).

Those promises, however, are **not** part of the Church's authenticated heritage of Bridget's revelations. The **Holy Office**, on **28 January 1954** (published in the _Acta Apostolicae Sedis_), issued this warning:

> "Since it is asserted in this pamphlet that God made to St. Bridget certain promises, **whose supernatural origin is uncertain**, let local ordinaries take care not to grant permission for publishing or reprinting pamphlets or other writings which contain these promises."

This is precisely how the Church treats numeric promises: she may approve a **devotion** — the recitation of the prayers — while refusing to certify the **promises printed on the pamphlet**.

### 6.4 St. Catherine of Genoa (1447–1510) — the _Treatise on Purgatory_

The classic mystical text on Purgatory — praised by Pope Benedict XVI in a General Audience of 12 January 2011 — describes Purgatory not as a place with a quantified fire but as an **inner purification wrought by God's love**. It contains nothing about the fire's suspension during Mass and no count of souls released. Much Catholic reflection on the "pain of loss" is owed to her; the viral claim is not.

### 6.5 St. Faustina Kowalska (1905–1938) — the _Diary_

St. Faustina records visits from souls in Purgatory and the value of the Divine Mercy Chaplet for the dying and the dead. The Divine Mercy devotion is approved and the Feast instituted; but the _Diary_'s concrete visions remain private revelation, and it contains **no** "many souls per Mass" formula. See [miracles/prophecies-visions/divine-mercy-st-faustina.md](../miracles/prophecies-visions/divine-mercy-st-faustina.md) and the parallel treatment in [hell-private-revelations.md](hell-private-revelations.md).

### 6.6 The modern devotional current

Most of the vivid detail in popular circulation — levels and regions, intense fire and cold, souls freed in great numbers — descends from **nineteenth- and twentieth-century devotional compilations**, above all Fr. F.X. Schouppe, S.J., _Purgatory: Explained by the Lives and Legends of the Saints_ (1893), and later booklets such as Fr. Paul O'Sullivan's _Read Me or Rue It_. These assemble edifying apparition stories of very uneven evidential value: useful for stirring devotion, unreliable when read as chronicles. The pseudo-Jerome text belongs to this same popular current.

### 6.7 What is _not_ found

A careful search of the patristic corpus and of the standard approved mystics locates **no authentic instance** of the claim that "the fire of Purgatory suspends its action for the entire duration of Mass." It appears to be unique to the pseudo-Jerome text that circulates today. The nearest genuine analogues are (a) the Thirty Gregorian Masses (§5) and (b) the devotional traditions of St. Mechtilde and St. Gertrude — none of which makes that claim.

### 6.9 The principle

The Church may approve a **devotion** (e.g., the Fifteen Prayers _as prayers_) while **refusing to certify the promises** printed with it (Holy Office, 1954). She may approve a **mystic's cult** without canonizing every detail of the mystic's visions ([CCC 67](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/0583c069-d4bf-42dd-97de-c19f0b80150f/ref/67)). And she has repeatedly declined to turn the hidden things of Purgatory into arithmetic — a refusal built into Trent's own prohibition of "curious searching" ([DH 1820](https://www.magisterium.com/docs/17f50f07-de81-4bf5-997c-f41ee830c033/ref/1820)). The right response to a devotional claim is therefore **not** to abandon prayer for the dead, but to keep the three tiers distinct: **doctrine**, **approved devotion**, and **unverified private claim**.

## 9. Pastoral Guidance

1. **Do not circulate the quotation as St. Jerome's.** Even when the _conclusion_ is good, a false attribution is a lie about a Doctor of the Church and a scandal to anyone who later discovers it. "Let what you say be simply 'Yes' or 'No'" ([Matthew 5:37](https://www.drbo.org/x/d?b=drb&c=mt&g=5&p=37)); the cause of truth is not served by fabricated evidence.
2. **Keep the practice, correct the reasoning.** Have Masses offered for your dead. Ask the parish for a Mass intention for a deceased relative; have Masses offered on anniversaries and on All Souls' Day; pray for the holy souls; obtain indulgences applicable to them. These are the real "effective acts of mercy," and they need no spurious quotation to commend them.
3. **Be precise about efficacy.** Tell people that the Mass is the _most excellent_ suffrage — not that it is automatic or quantifiable. God is not compelled; He is merciful. The Church's confidence rests on His promise and His fatherly love, applied _per modum suffragii_.
4. **Distinguish devotion from doctrine.** The _descriptions_ of Purgatory found in private revelations and pious literature (St. Catherine of Genoa, St. Faustina, the Mystics) can move the heart, but they are not doctrine and must never be taught as if they were. See [purgatory.md](purgatory.md) §7 and [hell-private-revelations.md](hell-private-revelations.md) for the same principle applied elsewhere.
5. **Correct with charity.** Many who share the post have good intentions and a genuine love for the holy souls. The correction should affirm that love while gently supplying the authentic sources.

## 10. How to Test a Devotional Quotation

A simple checklist, useful for any quotation encountered online (this one fails steps 2, 3, and 4):

1. **Who is quoted, and in what genre did that author actually write?** (Jerome wrote no treatise on the Mass.)
2. **Is the citation a _real_, locatable reference?** ("S. Hier., c. cum Mart. de celebr. Miss." is not.)
3. **Can the passage be found in a critical edition or a patristic database?** (It cannot.)
4. **Does the claim exceed what the Church has defined?** (Yes: it defines the manner and effect of purgatorial punishment, which the Church has left undefined.)
5. **Does it contradict or merely go beyond the deposit of faith?** (Here, it goes beyond — which is why the false attribution, rather than the devotion itself, is the chief problem.)
6. **Is there a genuine source that says the same _good_ thing without the fabrication?** (Yes — Cyril of Jerusalem, Augustine, Trent, the Catechism: see §4 and §7.)
